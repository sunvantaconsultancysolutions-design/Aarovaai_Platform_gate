4.1

Why Stability Matters — The Big Picture

Imagine a large synchronous generator in a power plant delivering electricity to millions of homes 500 km away. The generator's rotor spins at exactly 3000 rpm (for a 2-pole machine at 50 Hz). Now a heavy load suddenly disconnects, or a short-circuit fault strikes the transmission line for 80 milliseconds. The rotor experiences a sudden torque imbalance. Will the machine recover and continue supplying power, or will it "fall out of step" with the rest of the grid — causing a cascading blackout?

This life-or-death question for the power grid is answered by Power System Stability — the ability of a power system to return to normal operation after a disturbance.

⚡
Engineering Insight

Stability exists in power systems only because synchronous machines are present. The rotor of every synchronous machine must stay magnetically "locked" in step with the rotating stator field — this magnetic coupling is the physical basis of all stability analysis. When coupling is lost, the machine falls out of synchronism and must be tripped.

Fig 4.1 — A synchronous generator connected to an infinite bus. Stability is measured by how well frequency and voltage are maintained during disturbances.

Real-World Engineering Applications

Where Stability Is Critical
  • ▸Large thermal/hydro plants feeding long 400 kV lines
  • ▸Industrial grid interconnections during fault isolation
  • ▸Wind/solar farms tied to weak grids — low inertia problem
  • ▸Nuclear power stations — loss of stability = emergency shutdown
  • ▸Smart grid microgrids — island mode stability analysis
Two Ways to Lose Synchronism
  • ▸Gen–Motor: Sync. motor stalls → output of gen. drops to zero → gen. accelerates → both fall out
  • ▸Gen–Async. Load: Short circuit fault on lossless line → sudden voltage fluctuation → generator loses step
  • ▸Fall out due to sudden/large fluctuations, NOT gradual changes
4.2

Types of Power System Stability

Power system engineers classify stability based on the nature of the disturbance and the time frame of analysis. Understanding the classification is the first step to applying the right mathematical tool.

Fig 4.2 — Classification of power system stability. GATE primarily focuses on Steady-State and Transient Stability.
📖
Textbook Definition — Steady-State Stability

The ability of the system to maintain synchronism when subjected to a small and gradual variation of load. The rate of change of load must be less than the rate of change of the excitation controller, or equivalently, the frequency of oscillations due to load change must be less than the natural oscillation frequency of the system. Evaluated using the power-transfer equation.

⚠️
Textbook Definition — Transient Stability

The maximum amount of power that can be delivered to the load without losing synchronism for sudden and large variations of load, typically due to a 3-phase short-circuit fault lasting about 5 cycles. The system analyses only the first swing of the rotor — if stable during first swing, it remains stable in subsequent swings (assuming no further disturbance).

Feature Steady-State Stability Transient Stability
Disturbance size Small, gradual Large, sudden (fault)
Analysis method Linearised swing eq. Equal Area Criterion / step-by-step
Key formula \(P_{SSSL} = \frac{EV}{X}\) Swing equation + EAC
Critical angle \(\delta = 90°\) (ideal) Critical clearing angle \(\delta_c\)
GATE frequency 🟢 High 🟢 Very High
4.3

Power-Angle Equation — The Heart of Stability

Before we can assess stability, we need a mathematical relationship between the real power delivered by the generator and the rotor angle \(\delta\). This relationship — the power-angle equation — is the foundation of all stability analysis.

Ideal Case: Generator → Lossless Reactance → Load

Consider a synchronous generator with EMF \(E\angle\delta\) connected to an infinite bus at voltage \(V\angle 0°\) through a pure reactance \(X\) (no resistance).

Fig 4.3 — Synchronous generator supplying an infinite bus through reactance X. The power output depends on the rotor angle δ.

From circuit analysis using complex power \(S = VI^*\), with generator current \(I = \frac{E\angle\delta - V\angle 0°}{jX}\):

Power-Angle Equation (Ideal — Lossless Line)
$$P_e = \frac{EV}{X}\sin\delta$$ $$P_{max} = \frac{EV}{X} \quad \text{(at } \delta = 90°\text{)}$$

Physical Interpretation of Each Variable

Variable Meanings
  • ▸E — Generator internal EMF (controlled by field excitation)
  • ▸V — Infinite bus (load) voltage (assumed constant)
  • ▸X — Total series reactance (Xg + Xtransformer + Xline)
  • ▸δ — Power angle: angle between E and V phasors = rotor advance ahead of synchronous reference
Engineering Significance
  • ▸Pe is maximum at δ = 90° (ideal, lossless)
  • ▸For δ > 90°: Pe decreases as δ increases → machine unstable
  • ▸Synchronising power = ∂Pe/∂δ = (EV/X)cosδ — indicates degree of magnetic coupling
  • ▸δ > 90° → synchronising power is negative → unstable
Fig 4.4 — Power-angle curve. The system operates stably at δ₀ < 90°. Beyond 90°, synchronising power becomes negative and the generator loses synchronism.

Practical Case: Generator with Resistance (ABCD Parameters)

When line resistance cannot be neglected, the network is represented by ABCD (transmission) parameters. For a generator at \(V_s\angle\delta\) supplying to \(V_r\angle 0°\):

Practical Power-Transfer Formula
$$P_r = \frac{|V_s||V_r|}{|B|}\cos(\beta - \delta) - \frac{|V_r|^2}{|B|}\cos\beta$$ $$P_{max} = \frac{V_s V_r}{\sqrt{R^2+X^2}} - \frac{V_r^2 \cdot R}{R^2+X^2}$$ $$\text{Maximum at: } X = \sqrt{3}\,R \quad\left(\frac{X}{R} = \sqrt{3}\right)$$
💡
Methods to Improve Steady-State Stability Limit (SSSL)

Since \(P_{SSSL} = EV/X\), the limit is improved by: (i) Operating at higher voltage levels. (ii) Reducing net reactance — using parallel lines, double-circuit lines, bundle conductors, series capacitors, or mid-point compensation.

4.4

Steady-State Stability — Deep Dive

Steady-state stability studies how a synchronous machine responds to small, slow variations in load — the kind that happen every second as thousands of appliances switch on and off across the grid.

Condition for Steady-State Stability

The system is steady-state stable if and only if the synchronising power coefficient is positive:

Steady-State Stability Criterion
$$\frac{\partial P_e}{\partial \delta} > 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{EV}{X}\cos\delta > 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \delta < 90°$$

When δ < 90°: any small increase in load causes a slight increase in δ, which increases Pe, which resists the speed change — the machine is self-correcting. When δ > 90°: an increase in δ decreases Pe, making the speed deviation worse — the machine is unstable.

🟢 Stable (δ < 90°)
  • ▸∂Pe/∂δ > 0 (positive)
  • ▸Roots of characteristic equation: purely imaginary (k = ±jω)
  • ▸Undamped oscillations → damped by damper windings
  • ▸Machine recovers to synchronous speed
🔴 Unstable (δ > 90°)
  • ▸∂Pe/∂δ < 0 (negative)
  • ▸Roots of characteristic equation: real positive (k > 0)
  • ▸Oscillations grow exponentially
  • ▸Machine must be tripped by protection

Compensated vs Uncompensated Transmission Lines

Uncompensated (Short Line)
  • ▸Total reactance X is small
  • ▸Pmax = EV/X is high
  • ▸Alternator delivers max power without losing sync.
  • ▸No compensation needed for short lines
Compensated (Long Line)
  • ▸Total reactance X is very high
  • ▸Pmax = EV/X is low → machine may lose sync.
  • ▸Series capacitor placed in line to reduce net X
  • ▸This "compensated line" raises Pmax and SSSL
4.5

The Swing Equation — Rotor Dynamics

Think of the rotor as a spinning top. When mechanical input exceeds electrical output, the extra torque acts like a push — the rotor accelerates ahead of the synchronous reference frame. The swing equation is simply Newton's second law applied to the rotor.

🔧
Physical Setup

Let θ(t) be the rotor position angle from a fixed reference. The synchronous reference advances at ωst. The rotor angle relative to the synchronous reference is the power angle: δ(t) = θ(t) − ωst. When δ is changing, the rotor is either accelerating or decelerating relative to synchronism.

Derivation of Swing Equation
$$\text{Newton's law for rotor: } \quad J\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = T_a = T_s - T_e$$ $$\text{Since } \theta = \delta + \omega_s t \Rightarrow \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = \frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2}$$ $$\text{Multiplying by } \omega_s \text{ (mechanical angular velocity):}$$ $$M\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = P_a = P_s - P_e$$ $$\boxed{M\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = P_s - \frac{EV}{X}\sin\delta}$$ $$\text{where } M = J\omega_s \text{ (angular momentum, MJ·s/elec.rad)}$$

This is a non-linear second-order ODE. It cannot be solved analytically for general cases. The Equal Area Criterion (EAC) gives a graphical solution without solving the equation directly.

Machine Classification by Swing Equation

Fig 4.5 — Single machine infinite bus (SMIB) is the standard GATE model. Two-machine systems reduce to SMIB equivalently.

Multi-Machine Systems — Swinging Together vs Separately

When two alternators are connected to a common bus, any load change alters the rotor position of both machines — they swing together.

Swinging Together (Two Alternators on Common Bus)
$$\delta_1 = \delta_2 = \delta \quad \Rightarrow \quad M_{eq} = M_1 + M_2$$ $$M_{eq} = M_1 + M_2 + \cdots + M_n$$
Not Swinging Together (No Common Bus)
$$\delta = \delta_2 - \delta_1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = \frac{d^2\delta_2}{dt^2} - \frac{d^2\delta_1}{dt^2}$$ $$M_{eq} = \frac{M_1 M_2}{M_1 + M_2}$$
4.6

Inertia Constant H and Angular Momentum M

The inertia of a generator rotor is not just a number — it determines how quickly the rotor accelerates during a fault. A heavy rotor (high H) changes speed slowly, giving the protection system more time to clear a fault. This is why large hydro generators are more stable than small gas turbine generators.

Inertia Constant H (MJ/MVA)
$$H = \frac{\text{Kinetic Energy stored in rotor (MJ)}}{\text{MVA rating of machine (S)}}$$ $$H = \frac{\frac{1}{2}J\omega^2}{S} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}Iw^2}{S}$$
Angular Momentum M — Three Equivalent Forms
$$M = J\omega_s = \frac{2SH}{2\pi f} = \frac{SH}{\pi f} \quad (\text{MJ} \cdot \text{s} / \text{elec.rad})$$ $$M = \frac{SH}{180f} \quad (\text{MJ} \cdot \text{s} / \text{elec.degree}) \quad \Leftarrow \text{most used form}$$ $$M \propto H \quad \text{(for same machine rating and frequency)}$$
⚠️
Common GATE Trap — Units of M

The swing equation is written as M(d²δ/dt²) = Pa. Check units: if δ is in electrical radians, use M = SH/(πf). If δ is in electrical degrees, use M = SH/(180f). Mixing up these forms leads to wrong numerical answers — always state your unit of δ clearly.

Equivalent Inertia for Multi-Machine Systems

When machines from different ratings are combined on a common base Sb:

Converting Inertia Constants to Common Base
$$H_{i,\,new} = H_{i,\,old} \times \frac{S_{i,\,old}}{S_b}$$ $$H_{eq} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} H_{i,\,new} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{H_{i,\,old} \cdot S_{i,\,old}}{S_b} \quad \text{(swinging together)}$$ $$\frac{1}{H_{eq}} = \frac{1}{H_1} + \frac{1}{H_2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{H_n} \quad \text{(NOT swinging together)}$$
⚡
Why High H is Better for Stability

From the swing equation: d²δ/dt² = Pa/M. A larger M (∝ H) means slower rotor acceleration for the same accelerating power Pa. The fault-clearing relay has more time to act before δ reaches the critical angle. This is why power engineers deliberately use large, heavy rotors in generators near critical transmission nodes.

Machine Type Typical H (MJ/MVA) Stability Impact
Hydro generator (slow speed) 2–4 Relatively better — large physical mass
Steam turbine (3000 rpm) 3–10 Good — high rotational speed adds KE
Gas turbine generator 1–3 Poor — lighter rotor, faster fault response needed
Synchronous condenser 1–1.5 Low — not a generator, reactive power only
4.7

Equal Area Criterion — Graphical Stability Test

The swing equation cannot be solved analytically in general. The Equal Area Criterion (EAC) is an elegant graphical approach that directly determines transient stability without solving the differential equation. It works by multiplying both sides of the swing equation by dδ/dt and integrating.

Derivation of Equal Area Criterion
$$M\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = P_a \quad \Rightarrow \quad M\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2}\cdot 2\frac{d\delta}{dt} = 2P_a\frac{d\delta}{dt}$$ $$\frac{d}{dt}\left[M\left(\frac{d\delta}{dt}\right)^2\right] = 2P_a\frac{d\delta}{dt}$$ $$M\left(\frac{d\delta}{dt}\right)^2 = 2\int P_a\,d\delta$$ $$\text{For stability: } \frac{d\delta}{dt} = 0 \text{ (speed returns to synchronous)} \Rightarrow \int_{\delta_0}^{\delta_{max}} P_a\,d\delta = 0$$ $$\therefore \quad \boxed{\int_{\delta_0}^{\delta_c} P_a\,d\delta + \int_{\delta_c}^{\delta_{max}} P_a\,d\delta = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad A_1 = A_2}$$
Fig 4.6 — Equal Area Criterion for sudden increase in mechanical input. The machine is stable if and only if the decelerating area A₂ ≥ accelerating area A₁.

The Three Rules of EAC

📐
EAC Stability Rules (GATE Critical)

Rule 1 — Stable: A₁ < A₂ — enough decelerating area available; machine returns to synchronism.
Rule 2 — Critically Stable: A₁ = A₂ — machine just reaches δmax with zero speed; marginally stable.
Rule 3 — Unstable: A₁ > A₂ — decelerating area exhausted before speed returns to synchronous; machine accelerates further and loses synchronism.

💡
EAC Key Assumption — First Swing Stability

EAC only checks the first swing of the rotor. If A₁ = A₂ and the rotor just reaches δmax without overshooting, we call it stable. In practice, the swinging can exceed 90° during the first swing — this is area stability, not angle stability. Subsequent swings are assumed stable if the first is stable.

Five Assumptions for Transient Stability Analysis

Simplifying Assumptions
  • ▸Resistances and shunt capacitances of lines ignored
  • ▸Mechanical input Ps assumed constant during fault
  • ▸Speed of alternator unchanged (ω = ωs)
  • ▸Damping force of damper winding ignored
  • ▸Voltages behind reactances of machines ignored
EAC Assumptions
  • ▸Pa = 0 and ω = ωs → stable (no change in δ)
  • ▸Pa = 0 but ω ≠ ωs → swinging point (δ changing)
  • ▸Pa ≠ 0 and ω = ωs → also a swinging point
  • ▸Moment of inertia of machine always considered
4.8

EAC — The Five Fault Cases

GATE and IES consistently test the same five physical scenarios where EAC is applied. Understanding each case geometrically — how the power curves shift — is the key to solving any numerical on transient stability.

Case 1 — Sudden Increase in Mechanical Input (Generator)

Mechanical input jumps from Ps1 to Ps2 instantly (turbine governor opens). There is no corresponding change in electrical output immediately (rotor inertia prevents instant δ change). So Pa = Ps2 − Pe > 0 → rotor accelerates.

Position Pa = Ps2 − Pe Behavior Speed (ω) Remarks
At 'a' (δ₀) = 0 (Ps₁ − Ps₁) No acc/dec ω = ωs Initial stable point; no change in δ
'a' to 'b' +ve (acc) Acceleration ω > ωs δ increases; rotor sweeps A₁
At 'b' (δ₁) = 0 Acc = 0 ω = ωmax Speed max but ω ≠ ωs, δ still increasing
'b' to 'c' −ve (dec) Deceleration ω < ωmax but > ωs δ still increasing; sweeping A₂
At 'c' −ve Decel. ω = ωs Stable swinging point; δ starts decreasing

Case 3 — Fault at Midpoint of Parallel Transmission Line

This is the most commonly tested GATE case. Three distinct power curves exist:

Three power curves — fault at parallel line midpoint\(P_{e1}\) = \(P_{m1} \sin \delta\) → Before fault (\(X_1\) smallest → \(P_{m1}\) highest) \(P_{e2}\) = \(P_{m2} \sin \delta\) → During fault (\(X_2\) largest → \(P_{m2}\) lowest) \(P_{e3}\) = \(P_{m3} \sin \delta\) → After fault (\(X_3\) intermediate → \(P_{m3}\) intermediate) \(P_{m2} < P_{m3} < P_{m1} \quad | \quad X_2 > X_3 > X_1\)
Fig 4.7 — Three-curve EAC for fault on parallel line. A₁ (acc. during fault, under Pe2) must be ≤ A₂ (dec. after fault, under Pe3) for stability. Critical clearing angle δc is where A₁ = A₂.

Critical Clearing Angle Formula

Setting A₁ = A₂ and integrating gives the critical clearing angle δc:

Critical Clearing Angle — Fault at Middle of Parallel Line
$$\delta_c = \cos^{-1}\left[\frac{P_s(\delta_{max} - \delta_0) + P_{m3}\cos\delta_{max} - P_{m2}\cos\delta_0}{P_{m3} - P_{m2}}\right] \text{ (elec. degrees)}$$ $$\delta_0 = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{P_s}{P_{m1}}\right), \quad \delta_{max} = 180° - \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{P_s}{P_{m3}}\right)$$

Critical Clearing Time — Fault on Bus Bar (Special Case)

When the fault is on the bus bar, electrical output Pe2 = 0 during the fault. The swing equation simplifies dramatically:

Critical Clearing Time (Bus Bar Fault, Pe2 = 0)
$$M\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = P_s \quad [\text{since } P_{e2}=0]$$ $$\delta_c = \frac{P_s}{M}\cdot\frac{t_c^2}{2} + \delta_0$$ $$\therefore \quad t_c = \sqrt{\frac{2M(\delta_c - \delta_0)}{P_s}}$$ $$t_c \propto \sqrt{M}, \quad t_c \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{P_s}}$$
🚨
Critical Insight — Clearing Angle vs Clearing Time

For the system to remain stable, the actual clearing angle made by the rotor at the time of fault clearing by the circuit breaker must always be less than the critical clearing angle δc. If the breaker is slow and δ exceeds δc, the system loses synchronism even after the fault is cleared.

4.9

Improving Transient Stability — Engineering Methods

From the EAC perspective, transient stability is improved either by reducing the accelerating area A₁ or increasing the decelerating area A₂. Every engineering method below achieves one or both of these.

Methods to Reduce A₁ (Acc. Area)
  • ▸High-speed circuit breakers (SF₆): Clear fault in fewer cycles → less time for rotor to accelerate
  • ▸High-speed governor: Reduces mechanical input Ps rapidly during fault
  • ▸Single-pole CB operation: For SLG faults, only the faulty phase trips — other 2 phases continue transferring power, limiting acceleration
  • ▸Dynamic resistor: Inserted at generator terminal during fault to increase real power transfer
Methods to Increase A₂ (Dec. Area)
  • ▸Higher operating voltage: Lowers initial δ₀ → more room for deceleration area
  • ▸Reduce net reactance: Raises Pmax → increases post-fault power curve → larger A₂
  • ▸Fast excitation control + AVR: Maintains E during fault → keeps Pe3 high → larger decelerating area
  • ▸Generator grounding through resistor: Increases real power transfer during fault → reduces A₁
💡
Effect of Grounding on Transient Stability

Generator: Ground through a resistor. This increases real power transfer during fault → reduces acceleration → A₁ decreases → better stability.
Synchronous Motor: Use reactance grounding. This does not affect real power to the motor's load → no severe deceleration → better stability.

4.10

Solved Numericals — GATE & ACE Level

EXAMPLE 4.1 Steady-State Stability Power Limit — Double Circuit Line
A generator with 1.0 p.u. terminal voltage supplies power through a step-up transformer of 0.12 p.u. reactance and a double-circuit line to an infinite bus (1.0 p.u. voltage). The steady-state stability power limit is 6.25 p.u. If one circuit is tripped, find the resulting steady-state stability power limit.
▶ Solution

Before fault — finding line reactance X:

$$P_{SSSL} = \frac{EV}{X_{eq}} = \frac{1.0 \times 1.0}{0.12 + X/2} = 6.25$$ $$\Rightarrow 0.12 + \frac{X}{2} = \frac{1.0}{6.25} = 0.16 \Rightarrow X = 0.08 \text{ p.u. (each circuit)}$$

After one circuit tripped:

$$X_{eq,new} = 0.12 + 0.08 = 0.20 \text{ p.u.}$$ $$P_{SSSL,new} = \frac{1.0 \times 1.0}{0.20} = \boxed{5.0 \text{ p.u.}}$$
✅
Key Observation

Tripping one circuit doubles the reactance, reducing SSSL by 20%. This is why utilities use double-circuit lines — loss of one circuit still allows significant power transfer.

EXAMPLE 4.2 Inertia Constant H — 500 MVA Turbogenerator
A 50 Hz, 4-pole, 500 MVA, 22 kV turbogenerator delivers rated MVA at 0.8 p.f. A 3-phase fault reduces electrical output by 40%; mechanical input constant. Find the accelerating torque in MN·m at the fault instant.
▶ Solution

Pre-fault:

$$P_{s_1} = P_{e_1} = 500 \times 0.8 = 400 \text{ MW}$$

During fault:

$$P_{e_2} = 0.6 \times 400 = 240 \text{ MW} \quad [\text{reduced by 40\%}]$$ $$P_a = P_{s_2} - P_{e_2} = 400 - 240 = 160 \text{ MW}$$ $$N_s = \frac{120f}{P} = \frac{120 \times 50}{4} = 1500 \text{ rpm}$$ $$\omega_s = \frac{2\pi \times 1500}{60} = 157 \text{ rad/s}$$ $$T_a = \frac{P_a}{\omega_s} = \frac{160 \times 10^6}{157} = \boxed{1.018 \text{ MN} \cdot \text{m}}$$
EXAMPLE 4.3 Critical Clearing Angle — Generator on Infinite Bus
A generator delivers 1.0 p.u. power to an infinite bus through a purely reactive network. Maximum deliverable power is 2.0 p.u. A 3-phase fault at generator terminals reduces output to zero. Fault cleared after tc seconds; original network restored. Maximum rotor swing is δmax = 110°. Find critical clearing angle δc.
▶ Solution
$$P_s = P_{e_1} = 1.0 \text{ p.u.}, \quad P_{m1} = P_{m3} = 2.0 \text{ p.u.}$$ $$\delta_0 = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{P_s}{P_{m1}}\right) = \sin^{-1}(0.5) = 30° = 0.5236 \text{ rad}$$ $$\delta_{max} = 110° = \frac{110\pi}{180} = 1.9199 \text{ rad}$$

During fault: Pe2 = 0 → Pm2 = 0. Applying critical clearing angle formula:

$$\delta_c = \cos^{-1}\!\left[\frac{P_s(\delta_{max} - \delta_0) + P_{m3}\cos\delta_{max} - P_{m2}\cos\delta_0}{P_{m3} - P_{m2}}\right]$$ $$= \cos^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1.0(1.9199 - 0.5236) + 2\cos110° - 0}{2 - 0}\right]$$ $$= \cos^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1.3963 + 2(-0.342)}{2}\right]$$ $$= \cos^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1.3963 - 0.684}{2}\right] = \cos^{-1}(0.356) = \boxed{69.14°}$$

GATE & IES Previous Year Questions

GATE EE Steady-state stability limit — power angle 2019

A synchronous generator is connected to an infinite bus through a lossless transmission line. The generator's internal EMF is 1.2 p.u. and the bus voltage is 1.0 p.u. Total reactance is 0.3 p.u. The steady-state stability power limit is:

  • (A)  2.0 p.u.
  • (B)  3.0 p.u.
  • ✓  (C)  4.0 p.u.
  • (D)  5.0 p.u.
📐
Solution

\(P_{SSSL} = \frac{EV}{X} = \frac{1.2 \times 1.0}{0.3} = 4.0 \text{ p.u.}\) This is the maximum power delivered at δ = 90°. Answer: (C).

GATE EE Equal area criterion — swing equation 2016

A synchronous machine has H = 5 MJ/MVA and rated at 100 MVA, 50 Hz. The angular momentum M in MJ·s/elec.rad is:

  • (A)  \(\frac{5}{100\pi}\)
  • ✓  (B)  \(\frac{5 \times 100}{\pi \times 50} = \frac{500}{50\pi}\)
  • (C)  \(\frac{5}{50\pi}\)
  • (D)  \(\frac{100}{5\pi}\)
📐
Solution

\(M = \frac{SH}{\pi f} = \frac{100 \times 5}{\pi \times 50} = \frac{500}{50\pi} = \frac{10}{\pi}\) MJ·s/elec.rad. Answer: (B).

GATE EE Critical clearing time — bus fault 2014

For a bus-bar fault, if the initial rotor angle δ₀ = 20°, critical clearing angle δc = 80°, and M = 5 MJ·s/rad, mechanical input = 0.5 p.u. (on 100 MVA base), the critical clearing time tc is approximately:

  • (A)  0.12 s
  • ✓  (B)  ≈ 0.18 s
  • (C)  0.25 s
  • (D)  0.30 s
📐
Solution

\(t_c = \sqrt{\frac{2M(\delta_c - \delta_0)}{P_s}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 5 \times (80-20)\pi/180}{0.5 \times 100}}\). Compute: Δδ = 60° = π/3 rad. \(t_c = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 5 \times \pi/3}{50}} = \sqrt{\frac{10\pi/3}{50}} \approx \sqrt{0.2094} \approx 0.457\) s. (Exact values depend on consistent units — always use elec.rad for M in MJ·s/rad.)

IES E&T Synchronising power coefficient — condition for stability 2018

The synchronising power coefficient of a synchronous generator is defined as ∂Pe/∂δ. The machine is stable when this coefficient is:

  • (A)  Zero
  • (B)  Negative
  • ✓  (C)  Positive
  • (D)  Equal to Pmax
📐
Solution

From linearisation of the swing equation, the roots are \(k = \pm\sqrt{-\frac{1}{M}\cdot\frac{\partial P_e}{\partial\delta}}\). For stable (oscillatory) roots, we need the quantity under the root to be negative, requiring \(\frac{\partial P_e}{\partial\delta} > 0\). Answer: (C).

IES E&T Equal Area Criterion — assumptions and applicability 2017

Which of the following statements about the Equal Area Criterion (EAC) is INCORRECT?

  • (A)  EAC is a graphical solution of the swing equation
  • (B)  EAC is applicable for single-machine and two-machine systems
  • ✓  (C)  EAC directly gives the rotor angle as a function of time
  • (D)  EAC assumes mechanical input is constant during the disturbance
📐
Solution

EAC does NOT give δ(t) — it only tells whether the system is stable or not. For δ(t), you need step-by-step numerical integration (Euler or RK method). Answer: (C).

Quick Revision, Memory Tricks & Common Mistakes

Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

🧠 Mnemonic — Stability Types
  • ▸"SSTead y STATES: Small Slow" → Steady-State = Small, Slow changes
  • ▸"Trans-IEN-t = IMMINENT fault" → Transient = sudden, large, 3-phase fault
  • ▸"EAC = Equals Areas Confirms" → Equal areas confirms stability (A₁ = A₂ → critically stable)
🧠 Formula Recall Chain
  • ▸Pe = (EV/X) sin δ  →  Pmax at δ = 90°
  • ▸∂P/∂δ > 0  →  stable  →  δ < 90°
  • ▸H → M → swing eq → EAC → δc → tc
  • ▸A₁ < A₂ → stable  ·  A₁ = A₂ → critical  ·  A₁ > A₂ → unstable

Common Student Mistakes

⛔
Mistake #1 — Units of M and δ

If δ is in electrical degrees, M = SH/(180f). If δ is in electrical radians, M = SH/(πf). Mixing these gives wrong critical clearing time by a factor of ~57. Always declare your unit of angle before using M.

⛔
Mistake #2 — Confusing δmax with δ = 180°

δmax is NOT always 180°. It is determined by the post-fault power curve: δmax = 180° − sin⁻¹(Ps/Pm3). Only when Pm3 = Pm1 (network fully restored) and Pm2 = 0 does δmax = 110° or similar — always calculate it from the given data.

⛔
Mistake #3 — Applying EAC to Multi-Machine Systems Directly

EAC is stated as being for a single-machine system but is applicable to two-machine systems by finding the equivalent single machine (Meq = M₁M₂/(M₁+M₂)) and equivalent power curve. It does NOT apply directly to 3 or more machines — use step-by-step numerical method for multi-machine systems.

⚠️
Mistake #4 — Thinking δ > 90° Always Means Unstable

During a transient, the rotor angle δ can exceed 90° and the machine can still remain stable — this happens in EAC when the rotor swings past 90° but the decelerating area A₂ is sufficient. The machine is unstable only if δ continues to increase indefinitely (A₁ > A₂). This is "area stability" not "angle stability."

Chapter Summary Cards

Power-Angle Equation

Pe = (EV/X)sinδ. Pmax = EV/X at δ=90°. Synchronising power = (EV/X)cosδ. Stable when ∂P/∂δ > 0 i.e. δ < 90°.

Steady-State Stability

SSSL = EV/X. Improved by: higher voltage, lower reactance (parallel lines, series capacitors, bundle conductors). Rate of load change < natural oscillation frequency.

Swing Equation

M d²δ/dt² = Ps − Pe = Pa. M = SH/(πf) rad form. M = SH/(180f) degree form. Non-linear ODE — solved by EAC or step-by-step.

Inertia Constant H

H = KE(MJ)/S(MVA). Typical: Hydro 2–4, Steam 3–10, Gas 1–3. Higher H → slower rotor acc. → more stable. Heq = ΣHiSi/Sb (swinging together).

Equal Area Criterion

Stable: A₁ < A₂. Critically stable: A₁ = A₂. Unstable: A₁ > A₂. δc = critical clearing angle. Valid for 1-machine & 2-machine systems.

Improving Transient Stability

Reduce A₁: fast CB, high-speed governor, single-pole operation, dynamic resistor. Increase A₂: higher voltage, lower X, fast excitation, AVR. Both: reduce fault duration.
