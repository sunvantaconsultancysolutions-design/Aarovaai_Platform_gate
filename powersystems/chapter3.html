01

Big Picture — Why Travelling Waves?

⚡
The Engineering Problem This Topic Solves

When a lightning bolt strikes a transmission tower, or a circuit breaker operates suddenly, a surge wave is launched onto the power line. This wave can travel hundreds of kilometres in milliseconds, bounce off transformers, and build up dangerous voltages that destroy insulation. Understanding how these waves behave is the foundation of power system protection and insulation co-ordination.

In steady-state analysis, we model transmission lines using lumped or distributed parameters and assume sinusoidal voltages at power frequency (50 Hz). But in transient analysis, sudden disturbances such as switching operations, lightning strikes, or short circuits generate high-frequency, high-magnitude waves that propagate along the line at near-light speed. These are travelling waves (also called surges or transient waves).

Where Does This Topic Appear in Industry?

⚡ Power System Protection
  • ▸Surge arresters are sized based on wave reflection coefficients at transformer terminals
  • ▸Cable–overhead line junctions need careful wave impedance matching
  • ▸Travelling wave relays detect faults using wave arrival times
🔌 Insulation Co-ordination
  • ▸BIL (Basic Insulation Level) is determined from worst-case surge voltages
  • ▸HVDC converters deal with travelling wave reflections at cable terminations
  • ▸Smart grid sensors exploit wave velocities for fault location

02

Intuitive Understanding — The LC Tank Analogy

Think of a transmission line as a long chain of tiny inductors and capacitors, alternating in sequence. When a surge enters at one end, it charges the first capacitor through the first inductor, then the energy flows forward to charge the next capacitor through the next inductor, and so on — like a bucket-brigade passing water down the line.

💡
The Water Pipe Analogy

Imagine a long garden hose lying flat. You suddenly open a valve at one end. A pressure wave travels through the hose at the speed of sound in water — not at the speed you fill a bucket. Similarly, the voltage wave travels down the transmission line at near-light speed, while the electrons (current) barely move. The energy travels as a wave, not by bulk flow.

Figure 2.2 — Distributed LC model. Each dx segment has inductance L·dx and capacitance C·dx. The wave charges capacitors progressively as it travels.

The key insight from the bucket-brigade model: at the wavefront, energy stored in inductors exactly equals energy stored in capacitors, giving us the fundamental relationship from which wave velocity and surge impedance are derived.

03

Velocity of Wave Propagation

🔑
Core Principle

At the travelling wavefront, the energy being stored in the magnetic field (inductor) exactly equals the energy being stored in the electric field (capacitor). This energy balance gives us the wave velocity formula.

Mathematical Derivation

Consider a line with inductance per km = L and capacitance per km = C. Let the wave travel at velocity v.

Step 1 — Voltage equation (Faraday's law):
Total magnetic flux linkages up to distance \(x\) is \(\psi_x = L \cdot I \cdot x\).

Eq. 1 — Voltage–Flux Relationship \[ V = \frac{d\psi_x}{dt} = LI\,v \qquad \cdots (1) \] where \(v = dx/dt\) is the wave velocity (km/s).

Step 2 — Current equation (Gauss's law):
Total electric flux linkages up to distance \(x\) is \(\lambda_x = C \cdot V \cdot x\).

Eq. 2 — Current–Charge Relationship \[ I = \frac{d\lambda_x}{dt} = CV\,v \qquad \cdots (2) \]

Step 3 — Multiply equations (1) and (2):

Derivation of Wave Velocity \[ V \cdot I = (LC)(VI)\,v^2 \implies v^2 = \frac{1}{LC} \] \[ \boxed{v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \text{ m/s}} \]

Substituting L and C for Overhead Lines

For an overhead line, the inductance and capacitance per unit length are:

Line Parameters (Overhead Transmission) \[ L = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{GMD}}{\text{GMR}}\right) \text{ H/m}, \qquad C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln\!\left(\dfrac{\text{GMD}}{r}\right)} \text{ F/m} \]

When we substitute and simplify (noting that \(\ln(\text{GMD}/\text{GMR}) \approx \ln(\text{GMD}/r)\) for solid conductors), the logarithmic terms cancel:

General Wave Velocity Formula \[ v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \] where \(c = 3 \times 10^8\) m/s is the speed of light in vacuum.

Velocity — Line-Type Comparison

Line Type εr Velocity Practical Value
Lossless Overhead Line (OH) 1 (air) \(3\times10^8\) m/s 3×10⁵ km/s
Attenuated Overhead Line ≈1 Reduced by attenuation 2.5–2.8×10⁵ km/s
Underground Cable (UG) >1 (solid dielectric) \(3\times10^8/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\) m/s Slower than OH line
⚠️
GATE Trap — Cable vs OH Line Speed

For underground cables, \(\varepsilon_r > 1\), so \(v_{\text{cable}} < v_{\text{OH line}}\). Students often assume the cable is faster. Remember: denser medium → slower wave, just like light slowing down in glass.

04

Propagation Constant & Wave Length

Propagation Constant (γ)

The propagation constant captures how a voltage or current wave changes as it travels — both its physical displacement (phase shift) and its attenuation (reduction in magnitude).

Propagation Constant — General Form \[ \gamma = \sqrt{zy} \] \[ z = r + j\omega L \;\; \Omega/\text{km} \qquad \text{(series impedance per km)} \] \[ y = g + j\omega C \;\; \mho/\text{km} \qquad \text{(shunt admittance per km)} \] \[ \gamma = \alpha + j\beta \]
Symbol Name Unit Physical Meaning
\(\alpha\) Attenuation constant Nepers/km Decrement in V, I wave magnitude per km
\(\beta\) Phase constant (quadrature component) Radians/km Physical displacement (phase shift) of wave per km

Special Cases of Propagation Constant

✅ Lossless Line (r = 0, g = 0)
  • ▸\(\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{LC}\)
  • ▸\(\alpha = 0\) — no attenuation
  • ▸\(\beta = \omega\sqrt{LC} = 1.047\times10^{-3}\) rad/km
⚡ Attenuated Line (r ≠ 0, g ≠ 0)
  • ▸\(\gamma = \sqrt{(r+j\omega L)(g+j\omega C)}\)
  • ▸\(\alpha > 0\) — magnitude decreases with distance
  • ▸\(\beta > 1.047\times10^{-3}\) rad/km

Wave Length (λ)

The wavelength of a travelling wave on a transmission line is the distance the wave must travel to complete one full cycle (one repetition), equivalent to a phase shift of \(2\pi\) radians or 360°.

Wavelength Formula \[ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{\beta} \text{ km} \] For a lossless OH line at 50 Hz: \(\beta = 1.047\times10^{-3}\) rad/km \[ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{1.047\times10^{-3}} \approx 6000 \text{ km} \]
💡
Physical Significance of 6000 km Wavelength

A 6000 km wavelength means that even on a 600 km line, the wave only travels 1/10th of a wavelength before reaching the end — which is why transmission lines shorter than 600 km are often treated as electrically short in steady-state but not in transient analysis.

05

Surge Impedance & Surge Impedance Loading (SIL)

Surge / Characteristic / Natural Impedance (Zc)

Surge impedance is the impedance seen by the travelling wave at any point on the line. It characterises the line's response to transient waves — not to steady-state 50 Hz current.

Surge Impedance — General Definition \[ Z_c = \sqrt{\frac{z}{y}} = \sqrt{\frac{r + j\omega L}{g + j\omega C}} \] For a lossless line \((r = 0,\; g = 0)\): \[ Z_c = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \quad \text{(pure resistance)} \]
📌
Key Insight — Why Pure Resistance?

For a lossless line, \(Z_c = \sqrt{L/C}\) is a real number — it looks like a resistor to the wave. However, it does not dissipate power. It simply controls the ratio of voltage to current in the travelling wave, just as characteristic impedance in RF coaxial cables determines signal levels.

Typical Values of Surge Impedance

Line Type Zc Range Typical Value
Overhead Line (OH) 200 – 500 Ω 400 Ω
Underground Cable (UG) 40 – 80 Ω 40 Ω
💡
Why is UG Cable Zc Much Lower?

Underground cables have much larger capacitance per km (conductors close together, solid dielectric) and lower inductance (no magnetic field spreading into air). Since \(Z_c = \sqrt{L/C}\), a large C and small L both drive \(Z_c\) down. This is why when a wave crosses an OH–UG junction, a large portion is reflected back.

Surge Impedance Loading (SIL)

SIL is also known as characteristic impedance loading, natural loading, or ideal loading. It is the condition when the load impedance exactly matches the surge impedance of the line.

SIL Condition: Z_L = Z_C \[ \text{SIL} = \frac{V_r^2}{Z_c} \text{ MW (3-phase)} \] At SIL: \(\quad \frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 \quad\) (magnetic energy = electric energy)
Figure 2.3 — At SIL, the load is purely resistive and equals Zc. Both ends have unity power factor, |Vr| = |Vs|, and the line neither sources nor sinks reactive power.

Three Load Conditions vs SIL

Condition ZL vs Zc Reactive Power Voltage Relation Current Relation δ vs βℓ Economic Loading
Loading = SIL \(Z_L = Z_c\) Q = 0 (neither source nor sink) \(|V_r| = |V_s|\) \(|I_r| = |I_s|\) \(\delta = \beta\ell\) —
Loading > SIL \(Z_L < Z_c\) Line acts as sink of Q (inductive load) \(|V_r| < |V_s|\) \(|I_r| > |I_s|\) \(\delta > \beta\ell\) OH line
Loading < SIL \(Z_L > Z_c\) Line acts as source of Q (Ferranti effect) \(|V_r| > |V_s|\) \(|I_r| < |I_s|\) \(\delta < \beta\ell\) UG cable
🎯
GATE Insight — Ferranti Effect Connection

The famous Ferranti effect (receiving end voltage > sending end voltage) corresponds exactly to Loading < SIL. The lightly loaded or unloaded line generates reactive power capacitively, raising the receiving-end voltage. This is a direct application of the SIL concept — frequently tested in GATE.

06

Wave Travelling Analysis — Reflection & Refraction

🔑
When Does Reflection Occur?

Whenever a travelling wave reaches a discontinuity — any point where the medium (impedance) changes — the wave splits. Part is refracted (transmitted) forward, and part is reflected backward. The magnitudes depend solely on the impedances \(Z_c\) and \(Z_L\).

Equivalent Circuit at the Discontinuity

At the moment \(t = T^+\) (just after the wave reaches the end), the equivalent circuit is a Thevenin source of \(2V\) in series with \(Z_c\), connected to the load \(Z_L\).

Figure 2.4 — Thevenin equivalent at discontinuity: source = 2V, series impedance = Zc, load = ZL.

Reflection and Refraction Coefficients

Voltage Refraction (Transmission) Coefficient \[ V_2 = 2V \cdot \frac{Z_L}{Z_L + Z_c} \] \[ \alpha_V = \frac{V_2}{V} = \frac{2Z_L}{Z_L + Z_c} \]
Voltage Reflection Coefficient \[ V_1 = V_2 - V = V\left[\frac{Z_L - Z_c}{Z_L + Z_c}\right] \] \[ \beta_V = \frac{V_1}{V} = \frac{Z_L - Z_c}{Z_L + Z_c} \]
Current Coefficients \[ I_2 = \frac{2V}{Z_L + Z_c} = 2I\cdot\frac{Z_c}{Z_L + Z_c} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha_I = \frac{2Z_c}{Z_L + Z_c} \] \[ I_1 = I_2 - I \quad\Rightarrow\quad \beta_I = \frac{I_1}{I} = -\left[\frac{Z_L - Z_c}{Z_L + Z_c}\right] = -\beta_V \]
📐
Important Relationship: Refracted = Incident + Reflected

Always remember: \(V_2 = V + V_1\) and \(I_2 = I + I_1\). The refracted (transmitted) wave is the superposition of the incident and reflected waves at the junction. This is the boundary condition at the discontinuity.

Special Terminal Conditions

Terminal Condition ZL Vrefraction Vreflection Irefraction Ireflection
Open Circuit \(\infty\) 2 (doubles!) +1 0 −1
Short Circuit 0 0 −1 2 (doubles!) +1
Matched Load \(Z_c\) 1 (no reflection) 0 1 (no reflection) 0
\(Z_L < Z_c\) < Zc < 1 −ve (inverted) > 1 +ve
🚨
Critical Insight — Open Circuit Doubles Voltage!

At an open-circuit terminal, the reflected voltage coefficient is +1, meaning the voltage wave reflects with the same sign and doubles to \(2V\). This is why transformer windings connected to open-ended cables are at risk — lightning surges can appear at twice their incident magnitude. Surge arresters protect against this doubling effect.

Open & Short Circuit Wave Propagation — Step-by-Step

A step voltage wave \(V\) is injected at the sending end at \(t = 0\). The time for one propagation \(T = \text{length}/\text{velocity}\). We track voltage and current at each time instant.

Figure 2.5 — Open circuit line: voltage doubles to 2V at the receiving end at t = T⁺. Current wave is totally reflected with inverted sign. Full cycle completes in 4T.

Short Circuit Line

💡
Short Circuit is the Dual of Open Circuit

In a short circuit line, it is the current that doubles at the receiving end (not voltage). Voltage at the receiving end is always zero (short circuit constraint). The current increases by \(I\) at each propagation, reaching \(4I\) after 4 propagations before the cycle repeats.

Property Open Circuit End Short Circuit End
Refracted voltage 2V (doubles) 0 (zero)
Voltage reflection coeff. +1 −1
Refracted current 0 (zero) 2I (doubles)
Current reflection coeff. −1 +1
Cycle period 4T (where T = line length / velocity)

Bifurcated / Forked / Parallel Lines

When a transmission line branches into two (or more) parallel lines at a junction, a bifurcated (or forked) line situation arises. This is common at substations where a main trunk line feeds multiple outgoing feeders.

Figure 2.6 — Bifurcated line: at the junction, the two outgoing lines are in parallel. The voltage refracted is the same in both; currents split according to their surge impedances.

Analysis of Bifurcated Lines

The two outgoing lines \(Z_2\) and \(Z_3\) are connected in parallel at the junction. Their equivalent impedance is:

Bifurcated Line Equations \begin{align*} Z_{\text{eq}} &= \frac{Z_2 Z_3}{Z_2 + Z_3} \\[8pt] V_2 &= 2V \cdot \frac{Z_{\text{eq}}}{Z_{\text{eq}} + Z_1} = 2V \cdot \frac{Z_2 Z_3}{Z_2 Z_3 + Z_1 Z_2 + Z_1 Z_3} \\[8pt] \text{Voltage refraction coeff.} &= \frac{V_2}{V} = \frac{2Z_2 Z_3}{Z_1 Z_2 + Z_2 Z_3 + Z_3 Z_1} \\[8pt] I_2 &= \frac{V_2}{Z_2}, \quad I_3 = \frac{V_2}{Z_3}, \quad I_1 = (I_2 + I_3) - I \end{align*}
⚠️
GATE Tip — Voltage Is Same in Both Branches!

The refracted voltage \(V_2\) is identical in both branches \(Z_2\) and \(Z_3\), because they are connected in parallel at the same junction. Only the currents differ — \(I_2 = V_2/Z_2\) and \(I_3 = V_2/Z_3\). This is tested directly in GATE numericals.

Solved Numerical Examples

EXAMPLE 1 Wave Velocity in Underground Cable (Relative Permittivity = 4)
Find the velocity of wave propagation in an underground cable having relative permittivity εr = 4 of its dielectric material.
Solution
Step 1 For an underground cable, \(v = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\) m/s
Step 2 Substituting \(\varepsilon_r = 4\): \(v = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{\sqrt{4}} = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{2} = 1.5\times10^8\) m/s
Answer v = 1.5 × 10⁸ m/s = 1.5 × 10⁵ km/s — exactly half the speed of light in vacuum. This is significantly slower than an overhead line.
EXAMPLE 2 Time for Surge to Travel on 600 km OH Line (IAS-07)
The time taken for a surge to travel a 600 km long overhead transmission line is:
(a) 6 s    (b) 1 s    (c) 0.02 s    (d) 0.002 s
Solution
Step 1 For a lossless OH line: \(v = 3\times10^5\) km/s
Step 2 \(T = \dfrac{\text{Length}}{\text{Velocity}} = \dfrac{600}{3\times10^5} = 2\times10^{-3}\) s = 0.002 s
Answer (d) 0.002 s ✓ — The wave traverses 600 km in just 2 milliseconds!
EXAMPLE 3 Wavelength from Propagation Constant
The propagation constant of a transmission line is \((0.15\times10^{-3} + j1.5\times10^{-3})\). Find the wavelength of the travelling wave.
Solution
Step 1 From \(\gamma = \alpha + j\beta\): the phase constant \(\beta = 1.5\times10^{-3}\) rad/km
Step 2 \(\lambda = \dfrac{2\pi}{\beta} = \dfrac{2\pi}{1.5\times10^{-3}}\) km
Answer \(\lambda = \dfrac{2\pi}{1.5\times10^{-3}}\) km ≈ 4189 km
EXAMPLE 4 Surge Impedance from Generalised Circuit Constants
The generalised circuit constants of a 132 kV line are: A = D = 0.98∠0.3°, B = 82.5∠76°Ω, C = 0.0005∠90° mho. Find the surge impedance.
Solution
Step 1 For ABCD parameters: \(Z_c = \sqrt{\dfrac{B}{C}}\) (since A = D, this simplifies)
Step 2 \(Z_c = \sqrt{\dfrac{82.5\angle76°}{0.0005\angle90°}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{82.5}{0.0005}\angle(76°-90°)} = \sqrt{165000\angle{-14°}}\)
Step 3 \(Z_c = \sqrt{165000}\angle(-7°) = 406.2\angle{-7°}\) Ω
Answer Zc = 406.2∠−7° Ω — Close to typical OH line surge impedance of 400 Ω.
EXAMPLE 5 Short-Circuit Sending End Voltage & Current after 0.008 s
An overhead line 600 km long has surge impedance 500 Ω. The receiving end is short-circuited and a voltage of 6000 V is applied. Find the sending-end voltage and current after 0.008 s.
Solution
Step 1 Incident voltage V = 6000 V, Incident current I = V/Zc = 6000/500 = 12 A
Step 2 Time for one propagation: \(T = 600/(3\times10^5) = 2\times10^{-3}\) s = 2 ms
Step 3 After 0.008 s = 8 ms = 4T: this is 4 propagation periods. For a short-circuit line, current increases by I each propagation. After 4T: \(I_s = V = 6000\) V (sending end) and \(I_s = 5I = 60\) A
Answer Vs = 6000 V and Is = 60 A
EXAMPLE 6 Bifurcated Line — Voltage and Current Distribution
A lightning wave (20 kV surge) travels on an OH line of surge impedance Z₁ = 400 Ω and enters two parallel cables Z₂ = 400 Ω and Z₃ = 40 Ω at a junction. Find voltages and currents entering each cable.
Solution
Step 1 \(Z_{\text{eq}} = \dfrac{Z_2 Z_3}{Z_2 + Z_3} = \dfrac{400\times40}{400+40} = \dfrac{16000}{440} = 36.36\) Ω
Step 2 Refracted voltage: \(V_2 = 2V\cdot\dfrac{Z_{\text{eq}}}{Z_{\text{eq}} + Z_1} = 2\times20\times\dfrac{36.36}{36.36+400} = \dfrac{1454.4}{436.36} = 3.333\) kV
Step 3 Current in Z₂: \(I_2 = V_2/Z_2 = 3333/400 = 8.33\) A
Step 4 Current in Z₃: \(I_3 = V_2/Z_3 = 3333/40 = 83.33\) A
Answer V₂ = V₃ = 3.333 kV (same voltage at both branches), I₂ = 8.33 A, I₃ = 83.33 A. Note how the cable (lower Z) carries much higher current!

Common Mistakes & Memory Tricks

Memory Tricks — Mnemonics

🧠 Mnemonic 1 — Open vs Short
  • ▸"OCV, SCI" — Open Circuit doubles Voltage; Short Circuit doubles Current
  • ▸OC → Vref = 2, Iref = 0 | SC → Vref = 0, Iref = 2
🧠 Mnemonic 2 — SIL Conditions
  • ▸"Heavy OH, Light Cable" — economical loading for OH is > SIL (heavy), for UG cable is < SIL (light)
  • ▸OH: inductive load preferred | UG: capacitive preferred
🧠 Mnemonic 3 — Wave Coefficients
  • ▸"2 over Sum" for refraction: always \(\dfrac{2Z_L}{Z_L+Z_c}\)
  • ▸"Difference over Sum" for reflection: always \(\dfrac{Z_L-Z_c}{Z_L+Z_c}\)
🧠 Mnemonic 4 — Z_c Values
  • ▸"OH-400, UG-40" — Easy factor of 10 difference to remember
  • ▸OH has higher L, lower C → higher √(L/C)
  • ▸UG has lower L, higher C → lower √(L/C)

Concept Building Questions & Answers

❓
Q1: What happens to wave velocity if line capacitance doubles?

Since \(v = 1/\sqrt{LC}\), doubling C gives \(v' = 1/\sqrt{L \cdot 2C} = v/\sqrt{2}\). The wave slows down by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.414. This is why underground cables (high C) have lower wave velocities.

❓
Q2: Why does the current reflect with opposite sign at an open circuit?

At the open end, current must be zero (no path for current). The incident current \(I\) arrives; to make total current zero, the reflected current must be \(-I\). Hence \(\beta_I = -1\) for open circuit. This is purely a boundary condition requirement, not a loss of energy.

⚠️
Common Student Mistakes — GATE Traps

1. Open vs Short confusion: Voltage doubles at Open circuit, Current doubles at Short circuit — not the other way.

2. Refraction vs Reflection sign: Voltage reflection coefficient can be negative (when ZL < Zc), but voltage refraction coefficient is always positive.

3. Bifurcated voltage: Both outgoing lines get the SAME refracted voltage — only currents differ.

4. SIL units: SIL = V²/Zc gives VA (or MW for 3-phase when V is line voltage and losses are zero). Don't confuse with 3-phase power formula.

5. UG cable Zc: Cable has LOWER Zc (≈40 Ω), NOT higher. Many students think the underground cable is more 'resistant' and assign it higher impedance.

Quick Revision Sheet

Wave Velocity

\(v = 1/\sqrt{LC}\) m/s. OH lossless: \(3\times10^5\) km/s. UG cable: \(3\times10^8/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\) m/s.

Propagation Constant

\(\gamma = \sqrt{zy} = \alpha + j\beta\). Lossless: \(\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{LC}\), \(\alpha = 0\), \(\beta = \omega\sqrt{LC}\).

Surge Impedance

\(Z_c = \sqrt{L/C}\) (lossless). OH: ~400 Ω. UG Cable: ~40 Ω. OH always higher than UG.

SIL Formula

\(\text{SIL} = V_r^2/Z_c\) MW. Condition: \(Z_L = Z_c\), \(\frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \frac{1}{2}CV^2\), Q = 0.

Voltage Coefficients

Refraction: \(2Z_L/(Z_L+Z_c)\). Reflection: \((Z_L-Z_c)/(Z_L+Z_c)\). Always: Refracted = Incident + Reflected.

OC & SC Terminals

OC: V doubles (×2), I = 0. SC: I doubles (×2), V = 0. Matched (\(Z_L=Z_c\)): no reflection, all transmitted.

Wavelength

\(\lambda = 2\pi/\beta\) km. At 50 Hz on lossless OH: \(\lambda \approx 6000\) km.

Bifurcated Lines

\(Z_{eq} = Z_2Z_3/(Z_2+Z_3)\). \(V_2\) same in both branches. \(I_2 = V_2/Z_2\), \(I_3 = V_2/Z_3\).

SIL vs Loading

Load > SIL → Line sinks Q, \(|V_r|<|V_s|\). Load < SIL → Line sources Q (Ferranti), \(|V_r|>|V_s|\).
