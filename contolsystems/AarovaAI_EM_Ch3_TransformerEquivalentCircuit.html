3.1

Why This Topic Matters

RACEBig Picture Introduction

An ideal transformer simply steps voltage up or down. But every real transformer has resistances, leakage reactances, and a core that consumes power. The equivalent circuit is the tool that makes a real transformer mathematically tractable — it replaces the actual transformer with a network of resistors, inductors, and an ideal transformer that you can analyse with Kirchhoff's laws.

Without the equivalent circuit, you cannot answer any of these engineering questions:

  • What is the terminal voltage at full load? (Voltage Regulation)
  • How much power is wasted as heat in the windings and core? (Efficiency)
  • What are the winding parameters? (OC and SC Tests)
  • Will the transformer overheat at 110% load? (Thermal design)
  • What fault current flows during a secondary short circuit? (Protection design)
🌍
Engineering Reality

A 315 kVA distribution transformer serving a city block has a nameplate efficiency of 99.1%. That missing 0.9% = 2.8 kW dissipated as heat, 24 hours/day, 365 days/year = 24,500 kWh/year wasted — worth ₹1.8 lakh in electricity at ₹7/unit. The equivalent circuit is how engineers calculate this and design for better efficiency.

3.2

Intuitive Understanding

RACEIntuitive Explanation — Analogy First

Think of the transformer as a water pipe system with a pressure converter:

  • Primary resistance R₁: friction in the input pipe — water pressure drops as flow increases
  • Leakage reactance X₁: some water squirts sideways and never reaches the converter — wasted effort
  • Core (Rc ∥ Xm): the pressure converter itself — Rc is the friction inside it (core loss), Xm is the inertia it takes to spin up (magnetising energy)
  • Secondary resistance R₂, reactance X₂: same friction and leakage in the output pipe
  • Load ZL: the end user — tap, motor, house

The equivalent circuit simply draws this as an electrical circuit so we can apply KVL and KCL to calculate voltage drops and losses exactly.

🔑
The Central Idea

A transformer has two physically separate circuits linked only by a magnetic field. The equivalent circuit refers all quantities to one side using the turns ratio, collapsing the two circuits into one. Now you can treat it like a simple series–parallel AC circuit.

Exact Equivalent Circuit — Derivation

RACECore Theory + Mathematical Derivation

Step 1 — Non-Ideal Effects in a Real Transformer

Four non-ideal effects must be modelled:[1,2]

Primary winding resistanceCopper conductor has finite resistivityR₁ in series with primary
Primary leakage reactanceFlux that links primary but NOT secondaryjX₁ in series with primary

Magnetising reactanceCore needs reactive power to sustain fluxjXm in shunt (parallel)

Secondary winding resistanceCopper conductor in secondaryR₂ in series with secondary

Step 2 — The Exact Equivalent Circuit

Fig 3.1 — Exact equivalent circuit referred to primary side. R₂' = a²R₂, X₂' = a²X₂, where a = N₁/N₂
Basis: Chapman [1] §2.4–2.5; Kothari & Nagrath [2] §2.5

Step 3 — Referring Secondary Quantities to Primary

Referring Secondary Parameters to Primary Side (a = N₁/N₂)
R₂' = a² · R₂ (referred secondary resistance) X₂' = a² · X₂ (referred secondary leakage reactance) V₂' = a · V₂ (referred secondary voltage) I₂' = I₂ / a (referred secondary current) Z_L' = a² · Z_L (referred load impedance) Equivalently, referred to secondary side: R₁' = R₁ / a², X₁' = X₁ / a² Rc' = Rc / a², Xm' = Xm / a²
⚠️
GATE Trap — Impedance Scales as a², Not a

Voltage and current scale by a and 1/a respectively. But impedance (= V/I) scales as (a)/(1/a) = a². Students who write R₂' = a·R₂ will get the wrong answer. Always: impedances → a², voltages → a, currents → 1/a.

3.6

Phasor Diagram

RACEVisual Learning — Phasor Construction Step-by-Step

The phasor diagram shows the phase relationships between all voltages and currents in the transformer. It is constructed from the secondary side outward to the primary.[1,2]

Construction Steps (Lagging Load — Most Common)

  • Step 1: Draw V₂ as reference phasor (horizontal, pointing right)
  • Step 2: Draw I₂ lagging V₂ by angle φ₂ (for inductive/lagging load)
  • Step 3: Add I₂R₂ (in phase with I₂) to V₂ → gives a point along the way to E₂
  • Step 4: Add jI₂X₂ (90° ahead of I₂) → the tip is E₂ (= E₁/a)
  • Step 5: The core current I₀ = Ic (in phase with E₁) + Im (lagging E₁ by 90°)
  • Step 6: Total primary current I₁ = I₂' + I₀ (phasor sum)
  • Step 7: Add I₁R₁ (in phase with I₁) and jI₁X₁ (90° ahead) to E₁ → gives V₁
Fig 3.3 — Phasor diagram for lagging power factor load. Note: V₁ leads E₁ which leads V₂. The voltage regulation is positive for lagging loads.
Basis: Chapman [1] §2.5, Fig.2-17; Kothari & Nagrath [2] §2.5, Fig.2.14

Phasor Diagram — Leading Load

💡
Leading Load — Voltage Regulation Can Be Negative!

For a capacitive (leading p.f.) load: I₂ leads V₂. The jI₂X₂ drop partially cancels the I₂R₂ drop. In some cases, V₁ < V₂' — meaning the secondary voltage actually rises when load is applied. This is why voltage regulation can be negative for leading loads — a counterintuitive but important GATE concept.

3.8

Open-Circuit & Short-Circuit Tests

RACECore Theory — Test Procedures and Calculations
Fig 3.4 — OC test (left): LV energised at rated voltage, HV open. SC test (right): HV energised at reduced voltage (~5–10% rated), LV shorted.
Basis: Chapman [1] §2.5, pp.95–101; Kothari & Nagrath [2] §2.6

Calculations from Test Data

Open Circuit Test → Core Branch Parameters
Applied to LV side at rated voltage V_oc, I_oc, P_oc: Core loss component (active current): Ic = P_oc / V_oc Magnetising current (reactive): Im = √(I_oc² − Ic²) Core resistance: Rc = V_oc / Ic = V_oc² / P_oc Magnetising Xm: Xm = V_oc / Im No-load power factor: cosφ₀ = P_oc / (V_oc · I_oc)
Short Circuit Test → Series Branch Parameters
Applied to HV side at reduced voltage V_sc (to give rated I_sc = I₁_rated): Equivalent impedance: |Zeq| = V_sc / I_sc Equivalent resistance: Req = P_sc / I_sc² Equivalent reactance: Xeq = √(|Zeq|² − Req²) Full-load copper loss ≈ P_sc (since I_sc = I_rated) % Impedance voltage = (V_sc / V_rated) × 100%
💡
Why LV Side for OC Test?

Safety: the LV side has a lower voltage rating. If you energised the HV side and the LV side was open, you would measure the correct parameters — but handling a live 11kV terminal is dangerous. The LV side at 415V is safe to energise in a lab. The measured Rc and Xm must then be referred to the HV side if you want the HV-referred equivalent circuit.[1]

3.9

Voltage Regulation

RACECore Theory — Derivation and Engineering Significance

Voltage regulation (VR) quantifies how much the secondary terminal voltage drops (or rises) from no-load to full-load, expressed as a percentage of the full-load voltage.[1]

Voltage Regulation — Exact and Approximate Formulas
Exact definition: VR = (|V₂_NL| − |V₂_FL|) / |V₂_FL| × 100% Referred to primary: VR = (|V₁| − |aV₂_FL|) / |aV₂_FL| × 100% Approximate formula (per-unit drops, valid when Zeq << V): VR% ≈ ε_R cosφ + ε_X sinφ (lagging load) VR% ≈ ε_R cosφ − ε_X sinφ (leading load) Where: ε_R = (I₂ · Req) / V₂ × 100% = per-unit resistance drop ε_X = (I₂ · Xeq) / V₂ × 100% = per-unit reactance drop
Load TypeVR SignPhysical MeaningExample
Lagging (inductive)Positive (+)V₂ drops under load — VR is positiveMotor loads, induction furnaces
Unity p.f.Positive (+)V₂ still drops, but less than laggingResistive heaters
Leading (capacitive)Can be negative!V₂ actually rises under loadCapacitor banks, lightly loaded cables
Zero VR conditionZeroV₂ under load = V₂ at no loadAt specific leading p.f.: cosφ = Xeq/Zeq
🔑
Key Insight — Why Reactance Matters More Than Resistance

For a typical distribution transformer, Xeq ≈ 4–6% and Req ≈ 1–2%. For a lagging load at cosφ = 0.8 (sinφ = 0.6): VR% ≈ (2)(0.8) + (5)(0.6) = 1.6 + 3.0 = 4.6%. The reactance term contributes 3% while resistance contributes only 1.6%. This is why reducing leakage inductance is the primary design strategy for low-regulation transformers.

3.10

Efficiency

RACECore Theory — Losses, Efficiency, and Optimal Loading
Efficiency Formula and Maximum Efficiency Condition
η = P_out / P_in = P_out / (P_out + P_core + P_cu) For a transformer supplying fraction x of full load at power factor cosφ: P_out = x · S_rated · cosφ P_core = P_i (constant — independent of load) P_cu = x² · P_cu_FL (varies as square of load) η = (x · S · cosφ) / (x · S · cosφ + P_i + x²·P_cu_FL) Condition for maximum efficiency: dη/dx = 0 ⟹ P_i = x² · P_cu_FL (iron loss = copper loss) Load fraction at maximum efficiency: x_max_η = √(P_i / P_cu_FL) All-day efficiency (for distribution transformers): η_all_day = Σ(P_out · t) / [Σ(P_out · t) + 24·P_i + Σ(x²·P_cu_FL·t)]
💡
Why P_core = P_copper for Maximum η — Intuition

Think of it this way: as load increases, copper loss (∝ I²) grows faster than output power (∝ I). Meanwhile core loss stays fixed. There is a sweet spot where the marginal increase in output exactly offsets the marginal increase in total losses — that sweet spot occurs when P_core = P_copper. Beyond that, further load increase makes the denominator (losses) grow faster than the numerator (output).

3.11

Concept Building Questions

RACETeach Yourself by Questioning
CBQ Set — Equivalent Circuit
Q1. Why is the shunt branch (Rc ∥ Xm) placed in parallel, not in series?
Because core loss and magnetisation are driven by the applied voltage across the core — not by the load current. They exist even at no-load. A parallel branch carries current proportional to the voltage across it, which is exactly what core phenomena do. A series element would carry load current, which is wrong.
Q2. What happens to Xm if the core material is changed to air (no iron)?
Xm would decrease dramatically (by a factor of ~1000–10000× depending on original permeability). Air has μ_r ≈ 1 vs iron μ_r ≈ 5000. The magnetising current would become enormous — the transformer would draw a huge no-load current and be impractical.
Q3. Why does voltage regulation increase (worsen) as power factor becomes more lagging?
The leakage reactance voltage drop jI₂X_eq is 90° ahead of I₂. For a lagging load, I₂ lags V₂, so jI₂X_eq adds almost directly in phase with V₂ — maximum voltage drop. For a leading load, the jI₂X_eq component partially cancels the resistive drop.
Q4. If transformer rating doubles (2× kVA, same voltage), what happens to R_eq and X_eq?
Larger kVA → larger conductor cross-section → lower R_eq (halves). Core is larger → lower X_eq (approximately halves). Per-unit values remain approximately the same. This is why %Z is a more meaningful design parameter than the ohmic values.
Q5. Why is a transformer rated in kVA, not kW?
Transformer losses (copper + core) depend on current and voltage — not on load power factor. The transformer doesn't know whether the current is going to a motor (lagging) or a capacitor bank (leading). kVA captures the total current (which determines copper loss) and voltage (which determines core loss), regardless of phase angle.

Worked Examples

RACEBasic → GATE-Level → Conceptual
BASICFind equivalent circuit from OC and SC test data
A 10 kVA, 2200/220 V, 50 Hz single-phase transformer has the following test data: OC test (LV side): V_oc = 220 V, I_oc = 0.45 A, P_oc = 70 W SC test (HV side): V_sc = 112 V, I_sc = 4.55 A, P_sc = 215 W Find the equivalent circuit parameters referred to HV (primary) side.
Solution
Turns ratio: a = 2200/220 = 10 ─── FROM OC TEST → Shunt Branch (referred to LV then × a²) ─── Ic = P_oc / V_oc = 70/220 = 0.318 A Im = √(0.45² − 0.318²) = √(0.2025 − 0.1011) = √0.1014 = 0.318 A Rc_LV = V_oc / Ic = 220/0.318 = 692 Ω Xm_LV = V_oc / Im = 220/0.318 = 692 Ω Referred to HV (primary) side: Rc = a² × Rc_LV = 100 × 692 = 69,200 Ω ≈ 69.2 kΩ Xm = a² × Xm_LV = 100 × 692 = 69,200 Ω ≈ 69.2 kΩ ─── FROM SC TEST → Series Branch (already on HV side) ─── |Zeq| = V_sc / I_sc = 112/4.55 = 24.6 Ω Req = P_sc / I_sc² = 215/(4.55²) = 215/20.7 = 10.4 Ω Xeq = √(24.6² − 10.4²) = √(605 − 108) = √497 = 22.3 Ω Rated primary current = 10,000/2200 = 4.55 A ← same as I_sc ✓
GATE LEVELVoltage regulation and efficiency at given load and power factor
Using the transformer from Example 1, find: (a) Voltage regulation at full load, 0.8 p.f. lagging. (b) Efficiency at full load, 0.8 p.f. lagging. (c) Load at which maximum efficiency occurs and the maximum efficiency value.
Solution
(a) Voltage Regulation: ε_R = (I_FL × Req) / V₁_rated = (4.55 × 10.4) / 2200 = 47.3/2200 = 0.0215 = 2.15% ε_X = (I_FL × Xeq) / V₁_rated = (4.55 × 22.3) / 2200 = 101.5/2200 = 0.0461 = 4.61% cosφ = 0.8, sinφ = 0.6 VR% ≈ ε_R cosφ + ε_X sinφ = 2.15(0.8) + 4.61(0.6) = 1.72 + 2.77 = 4.49% (b) Full-Load Efficiency: P_out = 10,000 × 0.8 = 8,000 W P_core = P_oc = 70 W (from OC test) P_cu_FL = P_sc = 215 W (from SC test, at rated current) η = 8000 / (8000 + 70 + 215) = 8000/8285 = 96.56% (c) Maximum Efficiency: x_max_η = √(P_core/P_cu_FL) = √(70/215) = √0.326 = 0.571 → Maximum η at 57.1% of full load = 5.71 kVA P_out at max η = 0.571 × 10,000 × 0.8 = 4,568 W P_cu at max η = (0.571)² × 215 = 0.326 × 215 = 70 W = P_core ✓ η_max = 4568/(4568 + 70 + 70) = 4568/4708 = 97.03%
💡
Notice

At maximum efficiency (57.1% load), η = 97.03% — slightly higher than full-load efficiency (96.56%). Distribution transformers are deliberately designed for x_max_η ≈ 50–70% because they operate lightly loaded most of the day. If max η were at 100% load, the transformer would waste more energy averaged over 24 hours.

CONCEPTUALZero voltage regulation — at what power factor?
For the transformer in Example 1, at what leading power factor is the voltage regulation exactly zero?
Solution
For zero VR with leading load: VR% = ε_R cosφ − ε_X sinφ = 0 ε_R cosφ = ε_X sinφ tanφ = ε_R / ε_X = 2.15 / 4.61 = 0.466 φ = arctan(0.466) = 25° cosφ = cos(25°) = 0.906 (leading) Physical meaning: At 0.906 leading power factor, the capacitive reactive current reduces the secondary voltage by exactly the amount the resistance would raise it — net effect: V₂ under load = V₂ at no load. For cosφ > 0.906 leading: VR becomes negative (V₂ rises under load)

Memory Tricks & Quick Revision Sheet

RACEMemory Techniques + One-Page Revision
🧠 Memory Techniques
Which test gives which parameters?
"OC = Open Core" — OC test gives Core parameters (Rc, Xm). "SC = Short Copper" — SC test gives Copper (series) parameters (Req, Xeq).
Which side for which test?
"Low-Open, High-Short" — OC test on Low-voltage side; SC test on High-voltage side. The side with the ammeter and wattmeter is the energised side.
Impedance scaling rule
"Impedance is Angry (a²)" — When referring to primary, Z' = a² × Z. Voltage scales by a, current by 1/a, impedance by a × a = a².
Maximum efficiency condition
"Iron Equals Copper at the Peak" — Maximum efficiency occurs when P_iron (core) = P_copper. Not at full load unless deliberately designed so.
VR sign rule
"Lagging Lowers, Leading Lifts" — Lagging load always gives positive VR (voltage drops). Leading load can give negative VR (voltage rises). Unity is always small and positive.
