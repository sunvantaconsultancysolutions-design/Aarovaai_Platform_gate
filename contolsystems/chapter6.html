6.1

Introduction to Frequency Response

While transient analysis focuses on how a system responds to sudden changes (like step or impulse inputs), Frequency Response Analysis examines the steady-state behavior of a system when subjected to a continuous sinusoidal input of varying frequencies.

For a linear time-invariant (LTI) system with transfer function \( G(s) \), substituting \( s = j\omega \) yields the sinusoidal transfer function \( G(j\omega) \). This complex function can be separated into its magnitude \( |G(j\omega)| \) and phase \( \angle G(j\omega) \).

sinusoidal Input & Output

If the input is \( r(t) = A \sin(\omega t) \), the steady-state output will be a sinusoid of the same frequency, but with a modified amplitude and phase shift:

\( c_{ss}(t) = A |G(j\omega)| \sin(\omega t + \angle G(j\omega)) \)

🌍 Real-World Application

Audio Amplifiers: You want an amplifier to have a "flat" magnitude response across the human hearing range (20 Hz to 20 kHz). If it drops off at high frequencies, the audio sounds muffled. Bode plots map this exact characteristic.

6.2

Frequency Domain Specifications

Just as we had time-domain specifications like rise time and overshoot, the frequency domain has its own metrics that correlate directly with system performance. Let \( M(j\omega) \) represent the closed-loop frequency response magnitude.

Resonant Peak (\(M_r\))

The maximum value of the closed-loop magnitude \( |M(j\omega)| \). It indicates relative stability; a higher \(M_r\) implies a larger time-domain peak overshoot (\(M_p\)).

Resonant Frequency (\(\omega_r\))

The frequency at which the resonant peak \( M_r \) occurs. It relates to the time-domain damped natural frequency (\(\omega_d\)) and indicates the speed of the transient response.

Standard 2nd Order System Formulas
$$ M_r = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \quad \text{and} \quad \omega_r = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} $$
Valid only for underdamped systems where \( 0 \le \zeta \le 0.707 \). If \( \zeta > 0.707 \), no resonance occurs (the peak is at \(\omega = 0\)).

Bandwidth (\(\omega_b\))

The range of frequencies over which the closed-loop magnitude \( |M(j\omega)| \) remains within \( -3 \text{ dB} \) (or \( 1/\sqrt{2} \)) of its zero-frequency value. A larger bandwidth means a faster system rise time and better reproduction of rapidly changing inputs, but it also makes the system more susceptible to high-frequency noise.

6.3

Bode Plot Construction

A Bode plot consists of two distinct graphs plotted against a logarithmic frequency scale (log \(\omega\)):
1. Magnitude Plot: \( 20 \log_{10} |G(j\omega)| \) in decibels (dB) versus \(\log \omega\).
2. Phase Plot: \( \angle G(j\omega) \) in degrees versus \(\log \omega\).

🤖 Aarova AI — Core Standard Forms
To draw a Bode plot efficiently, express the transfer function in the time-constant form: \( (1 + sT) \) rather than the pole-zero form \( (s + p) \). This ensures the low-frequency magnitude asymptote is a straight horizontal line determined solely by the DC gain \( K \).

Building Blocks of a Bode Plot

Transfer Function Factor Magnitude Plot Asymptote Phase Contribution
Constant Gain \( K \) Horizontal line at \( 20 \log_{10}(K) \) dB. \( 0^\circ \) (if \(K>0\)), \( 180^\circ \) (if \(K<0\))
Poles at Origin \( 1/s^N \) Straight line passing through \(\omega=1\) at 0 dB, with slope \( -N \times 20 \) dB/dec. Constant \( -N \times 90^\circ \)
Zeros at Origin \( s^N \) Straight line passing through \(\omega=1\) at 0 dB, with slope \( +N \times 20 \) dB/dec. Constant \( +N \times 90^\circ \)
Simple Pole \( 1/(1+sT) \) 0 dB until corner frequency \(\omega_c = 1/T\), then slope drops to \( -20 \) dB/dec. \( -\tan^{-1}(\omega T) \)
Simple Zero \( (1+sT) \) 0 dB until corner frequency \(\omega_c = 1/T\), then slope rises to \( +20 \) dB/dec. \( +\tan^{-1}(\omega T) \)

6.4

Polar Plot & Mappings

A Polar Plot is a plot of the complex frequency response \( G(j\omega) \) on the complex plane (real axis vs. imaginary axis) as the frequency \( \omega \) is varied from \( 0 \) to \( \infty \). It captures both magnitude and phase in a single continuous locus.

📐 Polar Plot Shortcuts

For a transfer function of Type \( N \) (number of poles at origin) and Order \( M \) (total number of poles):
Start Point (\(\omega \to 0\)): Magnitude is \(\infty\) (if \(N>0\)), and phase is \( -N \times 90^\circ \).
End Point (\(\omega \to \infty\)): Magnitude approaches 0, and phase approaches \( -(M - Z) \times 90^\circ \), where Z is the number of zeros.

6.5

Nyquist Stability Criterion

The Nyquist criterion relates the open-loop frequency response \( G(j\omega)H(j\omega) \) to the number of closed-loop poles in the right-half \( s \)-plane, without needing to compute the roots explicitly. It relies on the principle of argument from complex analysis.

Nyquist Equation
$$ N = P - Z $$
Where:
\( N \) = Number of counter-clockwise encirclements of the critical point \((-1, j0)\).
\( P \) = Number of open-loop poles in the right-half plane (RHP).
\( Z \) = Number of closed-loop poles in the RHP (for a stable system, we require \( Z = 0 \)).

Gain Margin (GM) and Phase Margin (PM)

Relative stability measures how close the Nyquist plot comes to the critical point \((-1, j0)\).

  • Phase Crossover Frequency (\(\omega_{pc}\)): The frequency where the phase angle is exactly \( -180^\circ \). The plot intersects the negative real axis.
  • Gain Margin (GM): The additional gain required to make the system marginally stable. Calculated as \( GM = \frac{1}{|G(j\omega_{pc})|} \). In dB: \( GM_{dB} = -20 \log_{10} |G(j\omega_{pc})| \).
  • Gain Crossover Frequency (\(\omega_{gc}\)): The frequency where the magnitude is exactly \( 1 \) (or \( 0 \) dB). The plot intersects the unit circle.
  • Phase Margin (PM): The additional phase lag required to bring the system to marginal stability. Calculated as \( PM = 180^\circ + \angle G(j\omega_{gc}) \).

Stability Check: For a minimum-phase system, both GM and PM must be positive for the system to be stable. This implies \( \omega_{gc} < \omega_{pc} \).

6.6

GATE & IES Exam Concepts

CONCEPT 1Minimum vs. Non-Minimum Phase Systems

A minimum phase system has all its poles and zeros in the Left Half Plane (LHP). The magnitude uniquely determines the phase characteristics via the Bode integral.

A non-minimum phase system contains at least one pole or zero in the Right Half Plane (RHP). Example: \( \frac{1-sT}{1+sT} \) (an all-pass filter). The phase lag is far greater than a minimum phase system with the same magnitude response.

CONCEPT 2Nyquist Contour Detour at the Origin

If the open-loop transfer function has a pole at the origin (e.g., Type 1 system), the Nyquist contour in the \(s\)-plane cannot pass directly through \(s=0\). It must take an infinitesimally small semi-circular detour in the right half plane: \( s = \lim_{r \to 0} r e^{j\theta} \) where \( \theta \) goes from \( -90^\circ \) to \( +90^\circ \).

Exam Trap

This tiny semi-circle maps to an infinite-radius semicircle in the \(G(j\omega)\) plane. For a Type \(N\) system, the mapping covers \( N \times 180^\circ \) in a clockwise direction. Failing to account for this mapping leads to an incorrect encirclement count \(N\) and a wrong stability conclusion.

6.7

Worked Examples (GATE & IES)

Example 01
Gain Margin from Transfer Function

Calculate the exact phase crossover frequency and the Gain Margin (in dB) for an open-loop transfer function \( G(s) = \frac{10}{s(s+2)(s+5)} \).

Solution
1. Substitute \( s = j\omega \):
$$ G(j\omega) = \frac{10}{j\omega(j\omega+2)(j\omega+5)} = \frac{10}{j\omega(-\omega^2 + 7j\omega + 10)} = \frac{10}{-7\omega^2 + j\omega(10 - \omega^2)} $$
2. Find Phase Crossover Frequency (\(\omega_{pc}\)):
At \(\omega_{pc}\), the phase is \(-180^\circ\). This means the imaginary part of the denominator must be zero.
$$ \omega(10 - \omega^2) = 0 $$ Since \(\omega > 0\), we have \( \omega_{pc}^2 = 10 \implies \omega_{pc} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16 \text{ rad/s} \).
3. Calculate Magnitude at \(\omega_{pc}\):
Substitute \(\omega^2 = 10\) back into the real part of the denominator:
$$ G(j\omega_{pc}) = \frac{10}{-7(10) + j(0)} = \frac{10}{-70} = -\frac{1}{7} $$ The magnitude is \( |G(j\omega_{pc})| = 1/7 \).
4. Calculate Gain Margin:
$$ GM = \frac{1}{|G(j\omega_{pc})|} = 7 $$ In decibels: \( GM_{dB} = 20 \log_{10}(7) \approx 16.9 \text{ dB} \).
✔ Ans: \(\omega_{pc} = 3.16 \text{ rad/s}\), GM = \(16.9 \text{ dB}\).
Example 02
Bode Plot Corner Frequencies and Slopes

An open-loop transfer function is given by \( G(s) = \frac{40(s+2)}{s^2(s+10)} \). Determine the corner frequencies and the initial and final asymptotic slopes of the magnitude Bode plot.

Solution
1. Convert to Time-Constant Form:
$$ G(s) = \frac{40 \times 2(1 + 0.5s)}{s^2 \times 10(1 + 0.1s)} = \frac{8(1 + 0.5s)}{s^2(1 + 0.1s)} $$
2. Identify Corner Frequencies:
- From the zero \( (1 + 0.5s) \): \( \omega_{c1} = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2 \text{ rad/s} \)
- From the pole \( (1 + 0.1s) \): \( \omega_{c2} = \frac{1}{0.1} = 10 \text{ rad/s} \)
3. Determine Asymptotic Slopes:
- Initial Slope: Dominated by the \( 1/s^2 \) term at the origin. Slope = \( -2 \times 20 = \mathbf{-40 \text{ dB/dec}} \).
- Intermediate Slope (between 2 and 10 rad/s): The zero at \(\omega=2\) adds \(+20 \text{ dB/dec}\). New slope = \( -40 + 20 = \mathbf{-20 \text{ dB/dec}} \).
- Final Slope (above 10 rad/s): The pole at \(\omega=10\) subtracts \(20 \text{ dB/dec}\). Final slope = \( -20 - 20 = \mathbf{-40 \text{ dB/dec}} \).
✔ Ans: Corner freqs: 2 rad/s and 10 rad/s. Initial slope: -40 dB/dec. Final slope: -40 dB/dec.
Example 03
Nyquist Stability Criterion Application

The Nyquist plot of a system's open-loop transfer function \( G(s)H(s) \) encircles the \((-1, j0)\) point exactly once in the clockwise direction. If the open-loop transfer function has two poles in the right-half \(s\)-plane, determine the number of closed-loop poles in the right-half plane. Is the system stable?

Solution
1. Identify the given parameters:
- Open-loop poles in RHP: \( P = 2 \).
- The Nyquist equation defines \( N \) as the number of counter-clockwise encirclements. Since the encirclement is clockwise, \( N = -1 \).
2. Apply the Nyquist Equation:
$$ N = P - Z $$ Where \( Z \) is the number of closed-loop poles in the RHP.
$$ -1 = 2 - Z $$ $$ Z = 2 + 1 = 3 $$
3. Conclude Stability:
Since \( Z = 3 \) (which is greater than 0), the closed-loop system has 3 poles in the right-half plane. Therefore, the closed-loop system is strictly unstable.
✔ Ans: 3 closed-loop poles in RHP. System is UNSTABLE.
