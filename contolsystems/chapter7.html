7.1

Introduction to Control Action

In many practical engineering scenarios, altering the gain \( K \) alone (as seen in Root Locus) is insufficient to simultaneously meet stringent transient (speed, overshoot) and steady-state (error) specifications. A controller or compensator is an additional block inserted into the system (usually in the forward path) to reshape the root locus or Bode plot.

Controllers (P, I, D)

Controllers modify the error signal directly using proportional, integral, and derivative mathematical operations. They add distinct poles (often at the origin) and zeros to the system.

Compensators (Lead, Lag)

Compensators are physical networks (like RC circuits or OP-AMP filters) designed to inject a specific phase lead or phase lag at critical frequencies to improve stability margins.

7.2

P, PI, PD, and PID Controllers

1. Proportional (P) Controller

The control signal is proportional to the error: \( G_c(s) = K_p \). It acts as a simple amplifier. While it can reduce steady-state error (by increasing loop gain), it cannot eliminate it for Type 0 systems and can cause instability if \( K_p \) is too high.

2. Integral (I) & Proportional-Integral (PI) Controller

The I-controller integrates the error over time, which ensures that even a tiny error will eventually produce a corrective action.

PI Controller Transfer Function
$$ G_c(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s} = K_p \left(1 + \frac{1}{T_i s}\right) = \frac{K_p(s + K_i/K_p)}{s} $$
Adds a pole at the origin (\( s=0 \)) and a finite zero at \( s = -K_i/K_p \).
  • Advantage: Increases system Type by 1, driving steady-state error to zero for step inputs.
  • Disadvantage: The added pole at the origin shifts the root locus to the right, reducing relative stability and making the transient response sluggish.

3. Derivative (D) & Proportional-Derivative (PD) Controller

The D-controller anticipates future errors by responding to the rate of change of the error. Pure derivative control is never used alone.

PD Controller Transfer Function
$$ G_c(s) = K_p + K_d s = K_p (1 + T_d s) $$
Adds a single finite zero at \( s = -1/T_d \).
  • Advantage: Adds a LHP zero, pulling the root locus left. This drastically improves damping, reduces overshoot, and speeds up the transient response.
  • Disadvantage: Amplifies high-frequency noise (since derivative gain increases with frequency).

4. Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) Controller

Combines the zero steady-state error of the PI controller with the fast, stable transient response of the PD controller. It adds two zeros and one pole at the origin.

7.3

Phase Lead Compensator

A lead compensator acts somewhat like a PD controller but is physically realizable without infinite high-frequency gain. It introduces a positive phase angle (phase lead) into the system.

Lead Compensator Transfer Function
$$ G_c(s) = \frac{1 + a T s}{1 + T s} \quad \text{where } a > 1 $$
The zero is at \( -1/(aT) \) and the pole is at \( -1/T \). Since \( a > 1 \), the zero is closer to the origin than the pole.
Maximum Phase Lead (\(\phi_m\))

The maximum phase lead occurs at the geometric mean of the corner frequencies:

\( \omega_m = \frac{1}{T\sqrt{a}} \)

The maximum angle is given by:
\( \sin(\phi_m) = \frac{a - 1}{a + 1} \)

Effects of Lead Compensation
  • Improves phase margin and gain margin.
  • Increases bandwidth, resulting in a faster response (lower rise time).
  • Acts as a high-pass filter.
7.4

Phase Lag Compensator

A lag compensator approximates a PI controller. It provides high gain at low frequencies to improve steady-state error, while attenuating high frequencies to maintain stability.

Lag Compensator Transfer Function
$$ G_c(s) = \frac{1 + a T s}{1 + T s} \quad \text{where } a < 1 $$
The pole is at \( -1/T \) and the zero is at \( -1/(aT) \). Since \( a < 1 \), the pole is closer to the origin than the zero.
📉 Primary Purpose: Attenuation
Unlike a PI controller, a lag compensator does not increase the system Type, so it doesn't drive steady-state error perfectly to zero. Instead, it allows the designer to massively increase the DC gain \( K \) (which lowers error) without ruining the phase margin, because the compensator acts as a low-pass filter to attenuate the magnitude at the phase crossover frequency.
7.5

Lead-Lag Compensator

This combines both networks: it provides phase lag at low frequencies to improve steady-state accuracy, and phase lead at high frequencies to improve transient response and stability margins. It acts as a band-reject (notch) filter.

Fig 7.1: Pole-zero map of a Lead-Lag compensator. The lag pair is closer to the imaginary axis to dominate low-frequency behavior.
7.6

GATE & IES Exam Concepts

CONCEPT 1PD Controller vs. Lead Compensator

A pure PD controller (\( K_p + K_d s \)) has infinite gain at high frequencies (\(\omega \to \infty\)), meaning it drastically amplifies high-frequency sensor noise.

A Lead compensator adds a high-frequency pole alongside the zero. This limits the high-frequency gain to a constant maximum value (\(1/a\)), mitigating the noise amplification issue while still providing the required phase lead.

CONCEPT 2PI Controller vs. Lag Compensator

A PI controller adds a pole exactly at the origin (\(s=0\)), which mathematically increases the system Type by 1. If you have a Type 0 system (which has a steady-state error to a step input), adding a PI controller drops that step error to absolutely zero.

A Lag compensator adds a pole very close to the origin, but not exactly on it. It does not change the system Type. It only reduces the steady-state error by a finite factor.

7.7

Worked Examples (GATE & IES)

Example 01
Maximum Phase Lead Frequency Calculation

A phase lead compensator has the transfer function \( G_c(s) = \frac{1 + 0.4s}{1 + 0.1s} \). Determine the frequency at which the maximum phase lead occurs, and the value of the maximum phase angle.

Solution
1. Map to Standard Form:
Standard form: \( \frac{1 + aTs}{1 + Ts} \).
Comparing terms: \( T = 0.1 \) and \( aT = 0.4 \).
Therefore, \( a = \frac{0.4}{0.1} = 4 \).
2. Calculate Frequency for Max Phase Lead (\(\omega_m\)):
$$ \omega_m = \frac{1}{T\sqrt{a}} = \frac{1}{0.1\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{0.1 \times 2} = \frac{1}{0.2} = \mathbf{5 \text{ rad/s}} $$
3. Calculate Maximum Phase Lead Angle (\(\phi_m\)):
$$ \sin(\phi_m) = \frac{a - 1}{a + 1} = \frac{4 - 1}{4 + 1} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6 $$ $$ \phi_m = \sin^{-1}(0.6) = \mathbf{36.87^\circ} $$
✔ Ans: \(\omega_m = 5 \text{ rad/s}\), \(\phi_m = 36.87^\circ\).
Example 02
PID Controller Parameters

A PID controller is defined by the transfer function \( G_c(s) = 25 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2s} + 0.5s \right) \). Determine the proportional gain (\(K_p\)), integral time constant (\(T_i\)), and derivative time constant (\(T_d\)).

Solution
1. Identify the Standard PID Form:
The standard time-constant form of a PID controller is:
$$ G_c(s) = K_p \left( 1 + \frac{1}{T_i s} + T_d s \right) $$
2. Extract Parameters by Direct Comparison:
- Proportional Gain (\(K_p\)): The constant multiplier outside the bracket is \( \mathbf{25} \).
- Integral Time Constant (\(T_i\)): The term is \( \frac{1}{2s} \), which maps to \( \frac{1}{T_i s} \). Thus, \( T_i = \mathbf{2 \text{ seconds}} \).
- Derivative Time Constant (\(T_d\)): The term is \( 0.5s \), which maps to \( T_d s \). Thus, \( T_d = \mathbf{0.5 \text{ seconds}} \).
✔ Ans: \(K_p = 25\), \(T_i = 2\text{ s}\), \(T_d = 0.5\text{ s}\).
Example 03
Steady State Error Improvement via Lag Compensator

An uncompensated Type 1 system has a velocity error constant \( K_v = 10 \). A lag compensator \( G_c(s) = \frac{1 + 0.5s}{1 + 5s} \) is cascaded in the forward path. Calculate the new velocity error constant and the percentage reduction in steady-state error for a unit ramp input.

Solution
1. Analyze the Uncompensated System:
Original error constant \( K_{v\_old} = 10 \).
Steady-state error for a unit ramp: \( e_{ss\_old} = \frac{1}{K_{v\_old}} = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1 \).
2. Determine the DC Gain of the Lag Compensator:
The DC gain of the compensator is evaluated at \( s = 0 \):
$$ G_c(0) = \frac{1 + 0}{1 + 0} = 1 $$ Wait, a standard lag compensator in the form \( \frac{1 + aTs}{1 + Ts} \) has a DC gain of 1. It does not improve steady-state error unless combined with an amplifier. But wait, if the compensator was written as \( \frac{s + z}{s + p} = \frac{s + 2}{s + 0.2} \), its DC gain would be \( \frac{2}{0.2} = 10 \).
Since the problem gives \( G_c(s) = \frac{1 + 0.5s}{1 + 5s} \), at \( s \to 0 \), the gain is exactly 1. Therefore, the new \( K_v \) is STILL 10!
3. The Catch (Real-World Application):
In practice, a lag compensator is used alongside a proportional amplifier. If we increased the system gain \( K \) by a factor of 10 to get \( K_{v\_new} = 100 \), it would destroy the phase margin. The lag compensator is then inserted (with DC gain = 1) to attenuate the high frequencies and restore the phase margin, while preserving the new \( K_v = 100 \).
But purely inserting \( \frac{1 + 0.5s}{1 + 5s} \) without changing \( K \) results in 0% reduction in steady-state error.
✔ Ans: New \(K_v = 10\). Reduction in error = 0%.
