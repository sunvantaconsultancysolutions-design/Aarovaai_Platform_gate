5.1

Concept of Root Locus

The transient behavior of a closed-loop system is dictated by the location of its poles in the \(s\)-plane. In design, we adjust a controller gain \( K \) to modify system behavior. The root locus is a graphical representation of the paths (loci) traced by the closed-loop poles as the open-loop gain \( K \) varies continuously from \( 0 \) to \( \infty \).

💡 Why use Root Locus?
  • Avoids solving high-order polynomials repeatedly for different gains.
  • Shows the transition between underdamped, critically damped, and unstable regimes.
  • Allows visual optimization of settling time, overshoot, and frequency of oscillation.
🌍 Real-World Autopilot Design

When designing a drone pitch controller, high gain speeds up reaction time but induces oscillation. The root locus visually maps this threshold, showing at what exact gain the drone's poles drift too close to the imaginary axis, creating a dangerous overshoot.

5.2

Angle & Magnitude Criteria

For a standard negative feedback control system, the characteristic equation is: $$ 1 + G(s)H(s) = 0 \implies G(s)H(s) = -1 $$ This complex relation yields two fundamental conditions that form the basis of all root locus construction:

1. The Angle Criterion

A point \( s_0 \) lies on the root locus if and only if the angle of \( G(s_0)H(s_0) \) is an odd multiple of \( 180^\circ \):

Angle Criterion (Negative Feedback)
$$ \angle G(s)H(s) = \pm (2q + 1)180^\circ \quad \text{for } q = 0, 1, 2, \dots $$
This is a phase condition that depends only on the angles of open-loop poles and zeros.

2. The Magnitude Criterion

Once a point \( s_0 \) is confirmed to be on the locus via the angle criterion, the value of the open-loop gain \( K \) at that point is determined using the magnitude condition:

Magnitude Criterion
$$ |G(s)H(s)| = 1 \implies K = \frac{\prod \text{distances from poles to } s}{\prod \text{distances from zeros to } s} $$
Gain K is always positive and proportional to the pole-zero geometric distance product.
5.3

Construction Rules 1 to 4

Plotting a complete locus is simplified using standard geometric rules derived from the angle and magnitude criteria. Let \( P \) be the number of open-loop poles and \( Z \) be the number of open-loop zeros:

Rule 1: Symmetry

The root locus is symmetric about the real axis. Since control system coefficients are real, the closed-loop poles must be either real or occur in complex conjugate pairs.

Rule 2: Starting & Ending Points

The branches of the root locus start at the open-loop poles (when \( K = 0 \)) and end at the open-loop zeros (when \( K \to \infty \)). If \( P > Z \), then \( P - Z \) branches end at infinity.

Rule 3: Number of Branches

The number of individual, continuous branches on the root locus is equal to the number of closed-loop poles, which is given by \( N = \max(P, Z) \).

Rule 4: Existence on Real Axis

A point on the real axis lies on the root locus if and only if the total number of open-loop poles and zeros to its right is odd.

5.4

Asymptotes & Centroid

When open-loop poles outnumber zeros (\( P > Z \)), the excess \( P - Z \) branches diverge to infinity. As they travel to infinity, they follow straight-line asymptotic paths.

1. Angle of Asymptotes

The angles that the asymptotes make with the real axis are given by:

Asymptote Angle Formula
$$ \theta_l = \frac{(2l + 1)180^\circ}{P - Z} \quad \text{for } l = 0, 1, 2, \dots, (P - Z - 1) $$

2. Centroid (\(\sigma_g\))

The asymptotes intersect at a single point on the real axis called the centroid. The centroid can lie in either the left-hand or right-hand plane:

Centroid Formula
$$ \sigma_g = \frac{\sum (\text{Real parts of open-loop poles}) - \sum (\text{Real parts of open-loop zeros})}{P - Z} $$
Note: Substitute actual numerical real parts of poles/zeros directly into this formula.
5.5

Breakaway & Break-in Points

Breakaway points occur on the real-axis segments of the root locus where two closed-loop poles moving towards each other collide and split off into complex conjugate branches. Similarly, break-in points occur where complex conjugate branches return to the real axis.

🛠️ Finding Breakaway/Break-in Points

1. Formulate the loop gain equation \( K = f(s) \) from \( 1 + G(s)H(s) = 0 \).
2. Differentiate with respect to \( s \) and set to zero: \( \frac{dK}{ds} = 0 \).
3. Solve for \( s \). The resulting roots are potential breakaway/break-in points.
4. Crucial Verification step: Verify if the solved value of \( s \) lies on a valid segment of the root locus (using Rule 4 or the Angle Criterion).

5.6

Imaginary Axis Crossings

The points where the root locus branches intersect the imaginary axis (\( j\omega \) axis) represent the boundary of system stability. These crossings give the frequency of sustained oscillations and the marginal gain \( K_{mar} \).

Method 1: Routh-Hurwitz

Form the characteristic equation incorporating gain \( K \). Construct the Routh array and find \( K_{mar} \) by setting the \( s^1 \) row to zero. Substitute \( K_{mar} \) into the auxiliary equation from the \( s^2 \) row to solve for \( s = \pm j\omega_{osc} \).

Method 2: s = jω Substitution

Directly substitute \( s = j\omega \) into the closed-loop characteristic equation. Equate the real part and imaginary part independently to zero. Solve the two resulting equations simultaneously for \( \omega \) and \( K \).

5.7

Complex Angles of Departure/Arrival

When the open-loop transfer function contains complex poles or complex zeros, we must determine the angles at which the root locus branches depart from the poles or arrive at the zeros. These are derived directly from the angle criterion:

Angle of Departure (from complex pole \( p_i \))
$$ \phi_d = 180^\circ - \sum \phi_p + \sum \phi_z $$
Where \( \sum \phi_p \) is the sum of angles of vectors from all other poles to \( p_i \), and \( \sum \phi_z \) is the sum of angles from all zeros to \( p_i \).
Angle of Arrival (at complex zero \( z_j \))
$$ \phi_a = 180^\circ - \sum \psi_z + \sum \psi_p $$
Where \( \sum \psi_z \) is the sum of angles of vectors from all other zeros to \( z_j \), and \( \sum \psi_p \) is the sum of angles from all poles to \( z_j \).
5.8

GATE & IES Exam Concepts

CONCEPT 1System Stability and Adding Poles/Zeros

Adding poles and zeros fundamentally alters the root locus shape and coordinates:

Adding a LHP Pole
  • Pushes the root locus branches to the right.
  • Reduces the gain margin and range of stability.
  • Makes the transient response slower and more oscillatory.
Adding a LHP Zero
  • Pulls the root locus branches to the left.
  • Improves absolute and relative stability.
  • Speeds up transient rise times and increases damping.
CONCEPT 2Complementary Root Locus (Positive Feedback)

If a system uses **positive feedback**, the closed-loop characteristic equation becomes \( 1 - G(s)H(s) = 0 \). This changes the construction rules:

$$ \text{Angle Criterion:} \quad \angle G(s)H(s) = \pm 2q \times 180^\circ \quad (0^\circ, 360^\circ, \dots) $$ $$ \text{Asymptote Angles:} \quad \theta_l = \frac{2l \times 180^\circ}{P - Z} $$
⚠️
Real Axis Rule Swap

For positive feedback, the locus exists on the real-axis segments where the total number of open-loop poles and zeros to the right is even (including zero).

CONCEPT 3The Breakaway Verification Trap

Students often forget to check if the roots derived from \( \frac{dK}{ds} = 0 \) are actually on the root locus. A breakaway point is only valid if the gain \( K \) calculated at that point is positive and the point lies on a root locus branch.

⛔
Exam Trap

If \( \frac{dK}{ds} = 0 \) yields complex conjugate roots, they can only be valid breakaway points if they satisfy the angle criterion. Otherwise, discard them as mathematical artifacts.

5.9

Worked Examples (GATE & IES)

Example 01
Detailed Plot Parameter Computation

A unity feedback system has the open-loop transfer function \( G(s) = \frac{K}{s(s+3)(s+6)} \). Calculate the centroid (\(\sigma_g\)), asymptote angles (\(\theta_l\)), and the breakaway points.

Solution
1. Identify Poles and Zeros:
Poles: \( p_1 = 0 \), \( p_2 = -3 \), \( p_3 = -6 \) (number of poles \( P = 3 \)).
Zeros: None (number of zeros \( Z = 0 \)).
Excess poles: \( P - Z = 3 - 0 = 3 \).
2. Compute Centroid (\(\sigma_g\)):
$$ \sigma_g = \frac{\sum (\text{Real parts of poles}) - \sum (\text{Real parts of zeros})}{P - Z} $$ $$ \sigma_g = \frac{0 - 3 - 6 - 0}{3} = \frac{-9}{3} = \mathbf{-3} $$
3. Calculate Asymptote Angles (\(\theta_l\)):
$$ \theta_l = \frac{(2l + 1)180^\circ}{3} \quad \text{for } l = 0, 1, 2 $$ $$ l = 0: \quad \theta_0 = \frac{180^\circ}{3} = \mathbf{60^\circ} $$ $$ l = 1: \quad \theta_1 = \frac{3 \times 180^\circ}{3} = \mathbf{180^\circ} $$ $$ l = 2: \quad \theta_2 = \frac{5 \times 180^\circ}{3} = \mathbf{300^\circ} \quad (\text{or } -60^\circ) $$
4. Solve for Breakaway Points:
From characteristic equation: \( 1 + \frac{K}{s(s+3)(s+6)} = 0 \implies K = -s(s+3)(s+6) \)
$$ K = -s^3 - 9s^2 - 18s $$ Differentiate with respect to \( s \) and set to zero: $$ \frac{dK}{ds} = -3s^2 - 18s - 18 = 0 \implies s^2 + 6s + 6 = 0 $$ Using quadratic formula: $$ s = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 24}}{2} = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{12}}{2} = -3 \pm \sqrt{3} \approx -1.268 \text{ or } -4.732 $$ Verification:
- Real axis root locus exists on \( [-3, 0] \) and \( (-\infty, -6] \).
- \( s = -1.268 \) lies in the range \( [-3, 0] \). It is a valid breakaway point.
- \( s = -4.732 \) lies in the range \( [-6, -3] \). It is invalid as no locus branch exists there.
✔ Ans: Centroid: \(\sigma_g = -3\), Angles: \(60^\circ, 180^\circ, 300^\circ\), Breakaway Point: \(s = -1.268\)
Example 02
Complex Angle of Departure Calculation

An open-loop transfer function is given by \( G(s)H(s) = \frac{K}{s(s^2 + 2s + 5)} \). Compute the angle of departure of the root locus branch starting from the complex pole in the upper half-plane.

Solution
1. Identify Open-Loop Poles:
- Pole at origin: \( p_1 = 0 \)
- Solving \( s^2 + 2s + 5 = 0 \): $$ s = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = -1 \pm j2 $$ Upper complex pole is \( p_2 = -1 + j2 \); lower complex pole is \( p_3 = -1 - j2 \).
2. Calculate Angles of Vectors to Upper Complex Pole \( p_2 = -1 + j2 \):
We find the angle of the vector drawn from all other poles to \( p_2 \):
- Angle from pole \( p_1 = 0 \): $$ \theta_1 = 180^\circ - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{1}\right) = 180^\circ - 63.43^\circ = \mathbf{116.57^\circ} $$ - Angle from complex conjugate pole \( p_3 = -1 - j2 \) (located directly below \( p_2 \)): $$ \theta_3 = \mathbf{90^\circ} $$
3. Apply Angle of Departure Formula:
Since there are no open-loop zeros (\( Z = 0 \)): $$ \phi_d = 180^\circ - \sum \theta_p + \sum \theta_z $$ $$ \phi_d = 180^\circ - (\theta_1 + \theta_3) + 0 $$ $$ \phi_d = 180^\circ - (116.57^\circ + 90^\circ) = 180^\circ - 206.57^\circ = \mathbf{-26.57^\circ} \quad (\text{or } \mathbf{333.43^\circ}) $$
✔ Ans: Angle of departure \( \phi_d = -26.57^\circ \) (or \(333.43^\circ\))
Example 03
Tuning Gain K for Damping Ratio Specification

A unity feedback system has a forward plant \( G(s) = \frac{K}{s(s+4)} \). Using the magnitude criterion, determine the value of gain \( K \) when the closed-loop poles have a damping ratio of \(\zeta = 0.5\).

Solution
1. Identify Damping Line in the s-plane:
For a damping ratio \( \zeta = 0.5 \), the closed-loop poles lie on the line passing through the origin at an angle: $$ \theta = \cos^{-1}(\zeta) = \cos^{-1}(0.5) = 60^\circ \text{ with the negative real axis.} $$ The real part of the poles is \( -\zeta\omega_n \). Since the open-loop poles are at \( 0 \) and \( -4 \), the breakaway point is at \( s = -2 \). The complex branches of the locus go vertically up and down from \( -2 \), meaning the real part of the closed-loop poles on the complex branches is always \( -2 \). $$ -\zeta\omega_n = -2 \implies -0.5\omega_n = -2 \implies \omega_n = 4 \text{ rad/s} $$
2. Determine Pole Coordinates:
The imaginary part is \( \omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} = 4\sqrt{1-0.25} = 4\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2\sqrt{3} \).
The target point on the root locus is \( s_0 = -2 + j2\sqrt{3} \).
3. Apply Magnitude Criterion to Find Gain \( K \):
$$ |G(s_0)| = 1 \implies \frac{K}{|s_0||s_0 + 4|} = 1 \implies K = |s_0||s_0 + 4| $$ - Distance from pole at \( 0 \) to \( s_0 \): $$ |s_0| = |-2 + j2\sqrt{3}| = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{4 + 12} = 4 $$ - Distance from pole at \( -4 \) to \( s_0 \): $$ |s_0 + 4| = |2 + j2\sqrt{3}| = \sqrt{(2)^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{4 + 12} = 4 $$ Therefore: $$ K = 4 \times 4 = \mathbf{16} $$
✔ Ans: Gain \( K = 16 \)
5.10

Chapter Summary

🔑 Geometric Formulas
  • Angles of asymptotes: \( \theta_l = \frac{(2l+1)180^\circ}{P-Z} \)
  • Centroid: \( \sigma_g = \frac{\sum \text{Re}(p) - \sum \text{Re}(z)}{P-Z} \)
  • Breakaway derivatives: solve \( \frac{dK}{ds} = 0 \)
  • Departure angle: \( \phi_d = 180^\circ - \sum \theta_p + \sum \theta_z \)
📊 Behavior vs. Gain
  • \( K=0 \): System operates at open-loop poles.
  • \( K \to \infty \): Loci approach open-loop zeros or infinity.
  • Real axis rule: Locus exists where poles/zeros to right is odd.
  • Gain margin: Ratio of crossing gain \( K_{mar} \) to design gain.
🤖 Aarova AI — Chapter Takeaway
The Evans root locus is a graphical calculator. It turns algebra into geometry. By understanding how poles drift under the influence of gain, you gain direct control over transient specifications and absolute stability limits. Master the rules of centroid, asymptotes, and breakaway points, and s-plane analysis becomes an intuitive map rather than a mathematical obstacle.
