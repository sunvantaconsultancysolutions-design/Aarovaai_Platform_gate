4.1

Why Temperature Is the Most Measured Variable

Of all the physical variables encountered in engineering practice, temperature is the most universally measured. Almost every chemical reaction rate, material property, phase-change boundary, electrical resistance, and thermodynamic state is a function of temperature. Doebelin (2004) observes that temperature transducers outnumber pressure transducers by nearly three to one in installed industrial instrumentation.[1]

🌡️
Engineering Insight

Unlike force or pressure — which can be measured by direct mechanical contact — temperature is a thermodynamic state variable. You cannot measure it directly; you must infer it from some other physical property (resistance, emf, radiation) that changes reproducibly with temperature. This indirect nature is what makes temperature measurement both challenging and fascinating.

Where Temperature Measurement Appears

Fig. 4.1 — Representative industrial domains where temperature measurement is critical. [Doebelin, Measurement Systems, 5th ed.]

Temperature affects material properties exponentially in many cases — the Arrhenius law \(k = A e^{-E_a/RT}\) governs reaction rates, and the resistivity of metals follows \(\rho(T) = \rho_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]\). An error of even 1 °C in a semiconductor diffusion furnace can shift threshold voltages of thousands of transistors. This motivates the need for accurate, reliable sensors.

4.2

Classification of Temperature Sensors

Golding and Widdis (1963) classify all temperature-measuring devices into two fundamental groups based on whether the sensing element must physically touch the object.[2]

Fig. 4.2 — Complete classification tree of temperature sensing devices. [Adapted from Golding & Widdis; Doebelin]

  • ▸Sensor element placed in direct physical contact with the object or medium
  • ▸Thermal equilibrium must be established — introduces loading error (sensor absorbs heat)
  • ▸Suitable for solid, liquid, and gas phase measurements up to ~1700 °C
  • ▸Examples: RTD, Thermocouple, Thermistor, Bimetallic strip, Filled-system thermometer

  • ▸Examples: Total radiation pyrometer, optical pyrometer, infrared camera
4.3

Resistance Thermometer (RTD) — Principle and Materials

A Resistance Temperature Detector (RTD) exploits the well-known physical phenomenon that the electrical resistance of metals increases with temperature. This happens because elevated temperature causes increased lattice vibrations in the crystal structure, which scatter conduction electrons more frequently, thereby increasing resistivity.[1]

🔑
Fundamental Principle

In most pure metals, the mean free path of conduction electrons decreases as temperature rises (more phonon scattering). By the Drude model: \(\rho = m_e/(ne^2\tau)\), where \(\tau\) is the mean collision time. Since \(\tau\) decreases with temperature, resistivity \(\rho\) increases. This makes metals positive temperature coefficient (PTC) devices.

Requirements for RTD Resistance Materials

Not every metal is suitable for precision temperature measurement. Doebelin (2004) lists the following criteria that a good RTD material must satisfy:[1]

Fig. 4.3 — Eight key requirements for selecting RTD resistance materials. [Doebelin, 2004]

💡
Why Platinum Dominates Industry

Platinum is the overwhelming choice for precision RTDs. Its \(R\)–\(T\) curve holds over −263 °C to +545 °C with high precision (ITS-90 defines the temperature scale using a standard Pt RTD). It is chemically inert, commercially available in pure form, and its resistivity increases less rapidly at high temperatures than most metals — a feature that maintains sensitivity at elevated temperatures. The standard Pt100 has \(R_0 = 100\,\Omega\) at 0 °C.

4.4

RTD — Mathematical Derivation

The resistance of a metallic conductor varies with temperature in a manner described by a polynomial expansion. For a pure metal over a broad temperature range, the general expression is:[1,3]

General RTD Equation (Callendar–Van Dusen form)
$$R_t = R_0\bigl[1 + \alpha_1 t + \alpha_2 t^2 + \alpha_3 t^3 + \cdots + \alpha_n t^n\bigr]$$
Variable DefinitionsR₀ = Resistance at reference temperature (\(0^\circ\text{C}\)) [Ω] \(R_t\) = Resistance at temperature t [Ω] \(t\) = Temperature in \(^\circ\text{C}\) (measured from 0°C reference) \(\alpha_1\) = Linear temperature coefficient of resistance [Ω/Ω/°C] \(\alpha_2, \alpha_3, \dots, \alpha_n\) = Higher-order calibration coefficients

Linearised Form — Narrow Temperature Ranges

For most practical industrial applications over a limited temperature span, the higher-order coefficients (\(\alpha_2, \alpha_3, \ldots\)) are negligibly small compared to \(\alpha_1 t\). Truncating after the linear term gives the widely used linearised RTD equation:

Linearised RTD Equation
$$R_t = R_0(1 + \alpha_1 t)$$
⚠️
Common GATE Trap

Students often confuse \(\alpha_1\) (the linear coefficient defined at 0 °C) with the mean coefficient between two arbitrary temperatures. The value \(\alpha = 0.00391\,\Omega/\Omega/°C\) quoted for platinum is the fundamental interval coefficient defined as \(\alpha = (R_{100} - R_0)/(100 \cdot R_0)\), which equals \(\alpha_1\) only approximately. IEC 60751 defines this precisely.

Callendar–Van Dusen Equation for Platinum

For a standard platinum RTD (ITS-90), the Callendar–Van Dusen equation provides sub-millikelvin accuracy by including a quadratic term for \(T \geq 0\,°C\) and a cubic correction for \(T < 0\,°C\):

For T ≥ 0 °C
$$R_t = R_0\bigl(1 + At + Bt^2\bigr)$$
For −200 °C ≤ T < 0 °C
$$R_t = R_0\bigl[1 + At + Bt^2 + C(t - 100)t^3\bigr]$$
Callendar–Van Dusen Constants (IEC 60751)\(A\) = \(3.9083 \times 10^{-3} \, {}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\) \(B\) = \(-5.775 \times 10^{-7} \, {}^\circ\text{C}^{-2}\) \(C\) = \(-4.183 \times 10^{-12} \, {}^\circ\text{C}^{-4}\) (only for T < 0°C; C = 0 for T ≥ 0°C)

Temperature Coefficient of Resistance — Precise Definition

The temperature coefficient \(\alpha\) at any temperature \(T\) is formally defined as the fractional change in resistance per unit temperature change:

Instantaneous Temperature Coefficient
$$\alpha(T) = \frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dT}$$

For the linearised model \(R_t = R_0(1+\alpha_1 t)\), this gives \(\alpha(t) = \alpha_1/(1+\alpha_1 t)\), which is not constant — it decreases slightly as temperature rises. However, the approximation \(\alpha \approx \alpha_1\) is valid for small ranges.

4.5

RTD — Wheatstone Bridge Measurement Circuits

Since an RTD does not generate its own voltage, an external excitation source and a bridge circuit are required to convert resistance change into a measurable voltage. The Wheatstone bridge is the standard arrangement.[1]

Fig. 4.4 — Wheatstone bridge for RTD measurement. At balance, no current flows through galvanometer G. Temperature change unbalances the bridge, producing a measurable output voltage.

Bridge Balance and Output Voltage

At balance, the bridge condition is:

Bridge Balance Condition
$$\frac{R_1}{R_t} = \frac{R_2}{R_3} \quad \Rightarrow \quad R_1 R_3 = R_2 R_t$$

When temperature changes, \(R_t\) changes to \(R_t + \Delta R\), unbalancing the bridge. For a null-deflection method, \(R_1\) or another arm is adjusted until balance is restored — this gives a precise readout. For a deflection method, the output voltage is measured directly:

Bridge Output for Small Imbalance
$$V_{out} = V_s \cdot \frac{\Delta R / R_0}{4 + 2(\Delta R / R_0)} \approx V_s \cdot \frac{\Delta R}{4R_0}$$
📐
Three-Wire RTD Connection

Long lead wires introduce resistance \(R_L\) that appears in series with the RTD, causing error. The standard remedy is the 3-wire connection: two wires carry current on one side and one wire completes the opposite bridge arm. This places \(R_L\) in opposite bridge arms, making the errors self-cancelling. The 4-wire (Kelvin) connection eliminates lead resistance entirely by using separate current and voltage leads.

4.6

Thermocouple — Seebeck Effect and Fundamental Principle

A thermocouple consists of two dissimilar metallic conductors joined at two points called junctions. When the two junctions are maintained at different temperatures, a thermoelectric electromotive force (emf) is produced that drives a current around the circuit. This is the Seebeck effect, discovered by Thomas Johann Seebeck in 1821.[2,4]

Fig. 4.5 — Basic thermocouple circuit illustrating the Seebeck effect. Current flows as long as the two junctions are at different temperatures.

Physical Mechanism of the Seebeck Effect

In a metal, free electrons at the hot junction possess greater kinetic energy than those at the cold junction. They diffuse from hot to cold end, creating a charge separation (contact potential). When two dissimilar metals A and B are joined, each metal produces a different contact potential because their electron densities and work functions differ. The net emf around the closed loop is the algebraic sum of the contact potentials at both junctions — non-zero only because the two metals are different and the junctions are at different temperatures.

The thermoelectric emf depends on the temperature difference and the material combination. The relation between the thermo-electric emf and temperature difference is given by:[1]

Thermo-electric EMF Equation
$$E = \alpha(T - T_0) + \beta(T^2 - T_0^2)$$
Variable DefinitionsE = Thermo-electric emf [volts] \(T\) = Absolute temperature of hot junction [K or °C] \(T_0\) = Absolute temperature of cold (reference) junction [K or °C] \(\alpha, \beta\) = Constants depending on the metals chosen
🔑
Key Insight — What Determines the EMF

The thermo-electric emf depends only on the junction temperatures and the choice of materials. It does not depend on the shape, cross-sectional area, or length of the wires, nor on the method of joining (welding, soldering, or brazing). This makes thermocouples extremely versatile — you can form the measuring junction by simply welding or twisting the wire tips.

4.7

Peltier Effect and Thomson Effect

Peltier Effect (Reverse of Seebeck)

In 1834, Jean Charles Athanase Peltier discovered that when a current is externally forced through the junction of two dissimilar metals, heat is either absorbed or released at the junction, depending on the direction of current flow. This is the Peltier effect — the thermodynamic reverse of the Seebeck effect.[4]

Fig. 4.6 — Peltier effect: external current flowing in the same direction as the Seebeck current causes heat absorption at the hot junction and heat liberation at the cold junction.
Peltier Heat Rate
$$\dot{Q}_{Peltier} = \pi_{AB} \cdot I$$

where \(\pi_{AB}\) is the Peltier coefficient (in volts) for the metal pair A–B, and \(I\) is the current. The Peltier effect is the physical basis of thermoelectric coolers (TECs) used in miniature refrigeration and CPU cooling modules.

Thomson Effect

William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) predicted in 1851 — and later confirmed experimentally — that even within a single homogeneous conductor, heat is generated or absorbed when current flows along a temperature gradient. This is the Thomson effect.

Thomson Heat Generated per Unit Volume
$$\dot{q}_{Thomson} = \mu \cdot I \cdot \frac{dT}{dx}$$

where \(\mu\) is the Thomson coefficient (V/°C), \(I\) is current, and \(dT/dx\) is the temperature gradient along the conductor. The Thomson effect contributes a small but measurable correction to thermocouple emf at very high temperatures.

🧠
Memory Trick — Three Thermoelectric Effects

S–P–T: Seebeck Produces (emf), Peltier Pumps (heat), Thomson Transfers (heat in gradient). Seebeck: \(\Delta T \Rightarrow E\). Peltier: \(I \Rightarrow \Delta T\) at junction. Thomson: \(I + \nabla T \Rightarrow Q\) within conductor. These three are linked by the Kelvin relations: \(\pi = \alpha T\) and \(\mu = T\,d\alpha/dT\).

4.8

Laws Governing Thermocouples

Three fundamental laws govern the behaviour of thermocouple circuits. These laws allow engineers to use extension wires, intermediate junctions, and reference junction compensation without introducing measurement errors.

Law 1 — Law of Homogeneous Metals

⚖️
Law of Homogeneous Metals

A thermoelectric current cannot be sustained in a circuit consisting of a single homogeneous material, however varying in cross-section, by the application of heat alone.

Engineering consequence: Any thermocouple circuit must contain at least two different materials. Heating a single uniform wire produces no net emf regardless of the temperature distribution along it.

Law 2 — Law of Intermediate Metals

⚖️
Law of Intermediate Metals

A third homogeneous material can always be inserted anywhere in a thermocouple circuit without affecting the net emf, provided the two new junctions formed by the insertion are at the same temperature.

Engineering consequence: Voltmeter leads and extension wires introduce additional junctions. As long as both new junctions are at the same temperature (e.g., both at room temperature at the instrument terminals), no error is introduced. This permits using copper voltmeter leads with any thermocouple.

Law 3 — Law of Intermediate Temperatures

⚖️
Law of Intermediate Temperatures

If two dissimilar homogeneous metals produce emf \(E_1\) when junctions are at temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), and emf \(E_2\) when junctions are at \(T_2\) and \(T_3\), then the emf produced with junctions at \(T_1\) and \(T_3\) will be \(E_1 + E_2\).

$$E(T_1, T_3) = E(T_1, T_2) + E(T_2, T_3)$$
Engineering consequence: A thermocouple calibrated against a 0 °C reference can be used with any other reference temperature by applying a suitable correction using tabulated emf values. This is the principle behind reference junction compensation.

4.10

Thermistors — Thermally Sensitive Resistors

A thermistor (contraction of thermal resistor) is a semiconductor device whose resistance changes very significantly with temperature. Unlike metal RTDs (PTC), most thermistors are negative temperature coefficient (NTC) devices — their resistance decreases steeply as temperature rises.[1,3]

💡
Why Semiconductors Behave Differently from Metals

In a semiconductor, increasing temperature generates more electron–hole pairs (intrinsic carrier generation), dramatically increasing the number of current carriers. This carrier concentration effect dominates the mobility reduction from lattice scattering, so resistance falls with temperature — the opposite of metals.

Mathematical Model — Steinhart–Hart and Simpler Forms

The simplest and most widely used model for NTC thermistors relates resistance to absolute temperature through an exponential law:[1]

Basic NTC Thermistor Equation
$$R = \alpha e^{\beta/T}$$
Variable DefinitionsR = Resistance at absolute temperature T [Ω] \(\alpha\) = Pre-exponential constant [Ω] — material and geometry dependent \(\beta\) = Material constant [K] — typically 2000–5000 K \(T\) = Absolute temperature [K]

Comparing resistances at two temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_0\):

Two-Temperature Ratio Form
$$R_1 = R_0 \, e^{\beta(1/T_1 - 1/T_0)}$$

Temperature Coefficient of Resistance for Thermistors

Differentiating \(R = \alpha e^{\beta/T}\) with respect to \(T\) and normalising:

NTC Temperature Coefficient α(T)
$$\alpha(T) = \frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dT} = -\frac{\beta}{T^2}$$

For a typical thermistor with \(\beta = 4000\,\text{K}\) at \(T = 298\,\text{K}\) (25 °C):

Numerical Example (β = 4000 K, T = 25°C = 298 K)
$$\alpha = -\frac{4000}{(298)^2} = -\frac{4000}{88804} \approx -0.045\,\Omega/\Omega/°\text{C}$$
📊
Sensitivity Comparison — GATE Favourite

\(\alpha \approx -4.5\%/°C\) for a typical NTC thermistor versus \(\approx +0.39\%/°C\) for platinum. The thermistor is therefore about 10× more sensitive than an RTD. However, its highly non-linear \(R\)–\(T\) curve and limited temperature range (typically −60 to +150 °C) restrict its use to precision narrow-range applications. Platinum wins for wide-range, high-accuracy work.

Fig. 4.8 — Three common physical forms of thermistors: bead (fastest thermal response), disc (power applications), and rod/probe (remote mounting).

Thermistor Materials and Applications

Modern NTC thermistors are manufactured from sintered metal oxide mixtures — manganese, nickel, cobalt, copper, iron, and zinc oxides — pressed into the desired shape and fired at high temperature. Their high bulk resistivity (0.5 Ω to 750 Ω typical) allows construction of very small sensing elements with high resistance values, eliminating lead-resistance errors without special circuits.

Key applications include: temperature compensation in electronic circuits, liquid level sensors, flow sensors, medical oral and body-surface thermometers, HVAC room sensors, battery temperature monitoring in mobile devices, and time-delay circuits.

4.11

Radiation Pyrometers — Non-Contact Temperature Measurement

When the object is too hot (above ~1500 °C), moving, or in a hostile environment where contact sensors cannot survive, radiation pyrometers provide the solution. They measure the electromagnetic radiation emitted by the hot body and infer temperature from the Stefan–Boltzmann law.[1,5]

Stefan–Boltzmann Law — Physical Foundation

All bodies at temperatures above absolute zero emit electromagnetic radiation. For a perfect blackbody, the total radiated power per unit area is given by the Stefan–Boltzmann law:

Stefan–Boltzmann Law (Blackbody)
$$W = \sigma T^4 \quad \text{where} \quad \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\,\text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}$$

For a real body with emissivity \(\varepsilon\) (0 ≤ ε ≤ 1):

Real Body Radiation
$$W = \varepsilon \sigma T^4$$
Fig. 4.9 — Total radiation pyrometer schematic: radiation is focused by a lens onto a thermopile detector, amplified, and displayed as temperature.

Wien's Displacement Law and Optical Pyrometers

Wien's displacement law states that the wavelength of peak emission shifts with temperature:

Wien's Displacement Law
$$\lambda_{max} T = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \cdot \text{K} \quad (\approx 2898\,\mu\text{m} \cdot \text{K})$$

At 1000 K, peak emission is near 2.9 μm (infrared). At 6000 K (solar surface), peak is ~0.5 μm (visible green). Optical pyrometers compare the brightness of visible radiation from the target against a calibrated filament at the same wavelength (typically 0.65 μm red), using the human eye as a null detector. They are used for furnace and molten metal temperature measurement above 700 °C.

⚠️
Emissivity — The Major Source of Pyrometer Error

Real industrial surfaces have emissivity \(\varepsilon < 1\) (e.g., oxidised steel ε ≈ 0.85, polished aluminium ε ≈ 0.05). A pyrometer set for ε = 1 on polished aluminium (ε = 0.05) would read far below the actual temperature. Two-colour (ratio) pyrometers mitigate this by measuring the ratio of radiances at two wavelengths, which is independent of ε for a grey body.

GATE & IES Previous Year Questions with Analysis

High-Probability GATE Concepts 🟢 HIGH

GATE Insight Seebeck Coefficient and EMF Magnitude

The Seebeck coefficient \(S_{AB}\) for a thermocouple pair A–B is defined as:

$$S_{AB} = \frac{dE}{dT} \approx \alpha - \beta \cdot 2T_0$$

In GATE problems, you will be asked to compute the emf given \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), hot junction temperature \(T\), and cold junction temperature \(T_0\). Always substitute in Kelvin or °C consistently. The quadratic term \(\beta(T^2 - T_0^2)\) can be factored as \(\beta(T - T_0)(T + T_0)\) for quick calculation.

GATE IN RTD — Bridge Measurement Concept Type

An RTD with \(R_0 = 100\,\Omega\) and \(\alpha = 0.004\,°C^{-1}\) is placed in one arm of a Wheatstone bridge. The other three arms each have resistance \(100\,\Omega\). The bridge supply is \(V_s = 4\,\text{V}\). The temperature increases by 25 °C. The output voltage of the bridge is approximately:

  • (A) 10 mV
  • (B) 25 mV ✓
  • (C) 50 mV
  • (D) 100 mV

Solution: \(\Delta R = R_0 \alpha \Delta T = 100 \times 0.004 \times 25 = 10\,\Omega\). For a balanced bridge with small imbalance: \(V_{out} \approx V_s \cdot \Delta R/(4R_0) = 4 \times 10/(4 \times 100) = 0.1\,\text{V}/(10) = 25\,\text{mV}\). ✓

GATE IN Thermocouple — Law of Intermediate Temperatures Concept Type

A thermocouple produces emf = 12.2 mV when the hot junction is at 300 °C and the reference is at 0 °C. When the reference is maintained at 30 °C (instead of 0 °C) and the hot junction is at 300 °C, the emf produced is 10.6 mV. The emf of this thermocouple with the hot junction at 30 °C and the cold junction at 0 °C is:

  • (A) 1.0 mV
  • (B) 1.6 mV ✓
  • (C) 22.8 mV
  • (D) 0.8 mV

Solution: By the law of intermediate temperatures: \(E(300, 0) = E(300, 30) + E(30, 0)\). Therefore \(E(30, 0) = 12.2 - 10.6 = 1.6\,\text{mV}\). This is a direct application of the additivity of thermoelectric emf.

IES E&T Thermistor Sensitivity vs RTD Concept Type

Which of the following statements about thermistors is/are correct?
1. Thermistors are more sensitive than RTDs
2. Thermistors have better linearity than RTDs
3. Thermistors can be used over a wider temperature range than RTDs
4. NTC thermistors have a negative temperature coefficient

  • (A) 1 and 2 only
  • (B) 1 and 4 only ✓
  • (C) 2 and 3 only
  • (D) 1, 3, and 4

Explanation: Statement 1 ✓ — thermistor sensitivity (~4.5%/°C) ≫ RTD (~0.4%/°C). Statement 2 ✗ — thermistors are highly non-linear (exponential). Statement 3 ✗ — RTDs cover −250 to +900 °C; thermistors typically −60 to +150 °C. Statement 4 ✓ — NTC means resistance decreases with temperature.

GATE Insight Peltier vs Seebeck — Which Way is the Effect? 🟢 HIGH

A common GATE trap: the Seebeck effect converts \(\Delta T \rightarrow E\) (temperature difference to emf — used in temperature measurement). The Peltier effect converts \(I \rightarrow \Delta T\) (current to temperature difference at a junction — used in thermoelectric coolers). They are thermodynamically reversible and related by the Kelvin relation \(\pi_{AB} = S_{AB} \cdot T\). Never confuse the direction of cause and effect.

Medium-Probability Topics 🟡 MEDIUM

GATE IN Thermistor α Calculation Concept Type

An NTC thermistor has \(\beta = 3600\,\text{K}\). Its temperature coefficient of resistance at 27 °C is:

  • (A) −4.0 %/°C ✓
  • (B) +4.0 %/°C
  • (C) −0.4 %/°C
  • (D) −40 %/°C
$$\alpha = -\frac{\beta}{T^2} = -\frac{3600}{(300)^2} = -\frac{3600}{90000} = -0.04\,\,/°C = -4\%/°C$$

Note: \(T = 27 + 273 = 300\,\text{K}\). The negative sign confirms NTC behaviour.

Low-Probability but Conceptually Important 🔴 LOW

Concept Note Thomson Effect — Often Overlooked

The Thomson effect is the only thermoelectric effect that occurs within a single conductor rather than at a junction. It is the smallest of the three effects in practice. GATE rarely tests this directly, but IES questions have asked to distinguish it from the Peltier effect. Remember: Peltier acts at junctions, Thomson acts along the bulk of a conductor with a temperature gradient.

1. Homogeneous metals — need 2 dissimilar metals. 2. Intermediate metals — 3rd metal OK if its junctions are isothermal. 3. Intermediate temperatures — emfs add algebraically.

Thermistor Formula

\(R = \alpha e^{\beta/T}\). \(\alpha(T) = -\beta/T^2\). Sensitivity ≈ 10× RTD but range limited to ≈ −60 to +150 °C. Non-linear exponential.

Pyrometer Foundation

Stefan–Boltzmann: \(W = \varepsilon\sigma T^4\). Wien: \(\lambda_{max} T = 2898\,\mu m\cdot K\). Emissivity error is the biggest source of pyrometer inaccuracy.

Sensitivity Order

Thermistor ≫ RTD ≫ Thermocouple
~4.5%/°C vs ~0.4%/°C vs ~40 μV/°C

Accuracy Order

RTD (Pt) > Thermistor (narrow range) > Thermocouple
±0.1°C vs ±0.05°C (limited range) vs ±0.25°C to ±2.2°C

Bridge Output (RTD)

\(V_{out} \approx V_s \cdot \Delta R / (4R_0)\) for small imbalance. For exact: \(V_{out} = V_s \cdot \Delta R / (4R_0 + 2\Delta R)\).

Formula Quick-Reference

Equation Sensor Notes
\(R_t = R_0[1 + At + Bt^2 + C(t-100)t^3]\) Pt RTD Callendar–Van Dusen (IEC 60751). C=0 for t≥0°C
\(E = \alpha(T-T_0) + \beta(T^2 - T_0^2)\) Thermocouple α, β are metal-pair constants; T in °C or K consistently
\(\pi_{AB} = S_{AB} \cdot T\) Thermoelectric Kelvin relation (1st). Peltier coeff = Seebeck coeff × abs. temp
\(\mu = T \, d\alpha/dT\) Thomson Kelvin relation (2nd). Thomson coeff from Seebeck coefficient
\(\alpha_{thermistor} = -\beta/T^2\) Thermistor T in Kelvin; β ≈ 2000–5000 K; result is negative (NTC)
\(W = \varepsilon\sigma T^4\) Pyrometer Stefan–Boltzmann. σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. T in Kelvin
