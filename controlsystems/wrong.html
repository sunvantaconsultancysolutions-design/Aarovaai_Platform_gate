5.1

Instantaneous Power — Where AC Power Analysis Begins

In a DC circuit, power is a single fixed number: P = VI. In an AC circuit, voltage and current are both sinusoids that constantly change with time, and — unless the circuit is purely resistive — they are not even in step with each other. So the very first question in AC power analysis is: what does "power" even mean when both v and i are moving targets? The answer is the instantaneous power p(t), the product of the instantaneous voltage and instantaneous current at every single moment in time.

⚡
Why This Topic Matters

Every electrical bill, every transformer rating (kVA), every capacitor bank installed by a utility, and every impedance-matching network in a communication system traces back to the power quantities defined in this chapter. AC power analysis is the bridge between circuit theory and real-world electrical engineering — power systems, machines, and electronics all borrow this vocabulary.

Let the voltage across a load and the current through it be pure sinusoids of the same angular frequency ω, differing only in magnitude and phase:

Voltage and Current Phasors (Time Domain)
$$v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_v), \qquad i(t) = I_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_i)$$

The instantaneous power delivered to the load is simply their product:

Instantaneous Power
$$p(t) = v(t)\,i(t) = V_m I_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_v)\cos(\omega t + \theta_i)$$

Using the product-to-sum trigonometric identity \(\cos A \cos B = \tfrac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]\), this expands into a very revealing form — a constant term plus a term that oscillates at twice the supply frequency:

Instantaneous Power — Expanded Form
$$p(t) = \frac{1}{2}V_mI_m\cos(\theta_v-\theta_i) \;+\; \frac{1}{2}V_mI_m\cos(2\omega t+\theta_v+\theta_i)$$
Fig 5.1 — v(t) and i(t) as sinusoids of the same frequency, with i lagging v by an inductive phase angle θ. The resulting p(t) = v(t)·i(t) has a constant (average) part plus a ripple at twice the source frequency 2ω.

Reading the Two Terms Physically

Constant Term
  • ▸\(\tfrac{1}{2}V_mI_m\cos(\theta_v-\theta_i)\) does not depend on time
  • ▸This is the net energy transferred to the load per cycle — the useful, billable power
  • ▸It becomes the average power P (Section 5.2)
Double-Frequency Term
  • ▸Oscillates at 2ω — twice the supply frequency
  • ▸Its time-average over a full cycle is exactly zero
  • ▸Represents energy that sloshes back and forth between source and any reactive element (L or C)
🔍
Intuitive Picture

Think of a reactive element (inductor or capacitor) as a spring that stores and releases energy every half-cycle without ever "spending" it. The double-frequency ripple in p(t) is that spring action. Only the constant term does real work — turning a motor shaft, lighting a bulb, or heating a resistor.

What If This Parameter Increases?

Change Effect on p(t) Physical Reason
θ = θv − θi increases (more reactive) Average term shrinks (cosθ↓) Less of vi product is "in phase"; more energy just oscillates
θ = 0 (purely resistive) p(t) never goes negative v and i always same sign — power always flows into the load
θ = 90° (purely reactive) Average term = 0 Energy fully returned to source every quarter cycle — zero net transfer
5.2

Average (Real / Active) Power

The average power P is the time-average of p(t) over one full period T. Because the double-frequency term integrates to zero over a complete cycle, only the constant term survives:

Definition — Time Average of p(t)
$$P = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T p(t)\,dt = \frac{1}{2}V_mI_m\cos(\theta_v-\theta_i)$$

Since RMS values are related to peak values by \(V_{rms}=V_m/\sqrt2\) and \(I_{rms}=I_m/\sqrt2\), the average power is more commonly written in terms of RMS quantities — this is the form used on every nameplate, energy meter, and exam paper:

Average (Real) Power — Standard FormGeneral load: \( P = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos(\theta_v - \theta_i) \) [W] Purely resistive (θv = θi): \( P = I_{rms}^2 R = \dfrac{V_{rms}^2}{R} \) Purely reactive load (θv − θi = ±90°): \( P = 0 \) (no net energy transfer, only oscillation)
✓
Engineering Insight — Only Resistance Dissipates

Average power is dissipated only in the resistive part of a load. Ideal inductors and capacitors consume zero average power over a cycle — they store energy during one quarter cycle and return it during the next. P is what your electricity meter actually measures and what you pay for in kWh.

Average Power for a General Load Impedance

If a load has impedance \(Z = R + jX = |Z|\angle\theta\), and current phasor \(\mathbf I = I_{rms}\angle\theta_i\) flows through it, average power can be computed three equivalent ways:

Three Equivalent Forms of Average Power
$$P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta = I_{rms}^2\,\mathrm{Re}(Z) = I_{rms}^2 R$$

Here θ = θv − θi is exactly the angle of the load impedance Z — this single fact ties together the circuit's impedance triangle and its power triangle (Section 5.4), and is one of the most tested conceptual links in GATE circuit-theory papers.

5.3

Reactive Power

The energy that sloshes back and forth without being consumed still has to be supplied and withdrawn by the source, cycle after cycle — and it still causes real I²R losses in the wires that carry it, even though the load itself absorbs zero net energy. Engineers quantify this "borrowed and returned" power with the reactive power Q, using the sine of the angle rather than the cosine:

Reactive Power\( Q = V_{rms} I_{rms} \sin(\theta_v - \theta_i) \) [VAR — volt-ampere reactive] Inductive load (i lags v, θ > 0): Q > 0 — load absorbs (consumes) reactive power Capacitive load (i leads v, θ < 0): Q < 0 — load supplies (generates) reactive power Resistive load (θ = 0): Q = 0 — no reactive exchange
⚠️
Common Misconception

Students often think reactive power is "wasted" or "does nothing useful." In fact, Q is essential — it is what builds the magnetic field in a motor or transformer, or the electric field in a capacitor. What is undesirable is not Q itself, but an excess of it, since it forces the source and lines to carry more current than the real power alone would need.

Reactive Power for Pure L and Pure C

Element Q (VAR) Sign Convention
Inductor L \(Q_L = I_{rms}^2 X_L = \dfrac{V_{rms}^2}{X_L}\) Positive — absorbs Q
Capacitor C \(Q_C = -I_{rms}^2 X_C = -\dfrac{V_{rms}^2}{X_C}\) Negative — delivers Q
Resistor R 0 No reactive exchange

This opposite-sign behaviour is exactly why a capacitor bank connected in parallel with an inductive load (a motor) can cancel out its reactive power demand — the basis of power-factor correction covered in Section 5.6.

5.4

Apparent Power and the Power Triangle

The apparent power S is simply the product of RMS voltage and RMS current, with no regard to phase angle at all — it is what the source "appears" to be delivering, and it is the quantity that determines the required rating of generators, transformers, and cables (which is why these are rated in kVA, not kW):

Apparent Power\( S = V_{rms} I_{rms} \) [VA — volt-ampere] Relation to P and Q: \( S = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2} \)

P, Q, and S are related exactly like the three sides of a right-angled triangle — the power triangle — with the impedance angle θ between S and P:

Fig 5.2 — The power triangle for an inductive load. Base = real power P, vertical = reactive power Q, hypotenuse = apparent power S. The angle θ at the origin equals the load's impedance angle, and cos θ is the power factor.
Power Triangle Relations\( P = S\cos\theta \)    \( Q = S\sin\theta \)    \( S = \sqrt{P^2+Q^2} \) \( \theta = \tan^{-1}(Q/P) \)      \( \cos\theta = P/S \) = power factor

Units — Do Not Mix Them Up

P — Watts (W)
  • ▸Real, useful, billed energy rate
Q — VAR
  • ▸Reactive, non-dissipated, oscillating
S — VA
  • ▸Total, determines equipment rating
5.5

Complex Power

Complex power S packages P and Q into a single complex number, letting circuit engineers compute everything about a load's power behaviour with one compact phasor calculation instead of separate trigonometric formulas:

Complex Power — Definition\( \mathbf{S} = P + jQ = \tfrac12 \mathbf V \mathbf I^{*} = V_{rms}I_{rms}\angle\theta \) where V = RMS voltage phasor, I* = complex conjugate of RMS current phasor Magnitude: \( |\mathbf S| = S \) = apparent power Angle: \( \angle\mathbf S = \theta_v-\theta_i = \theta \) = impedance angle = pf angle
Fig 5.3 — Complex power S plotted on the complex plane. The real axis is P, the imaginary axis is Q, and the magnitude of the vector is the apparent power S.

Complex Power in Terms of Load Impedance

Complex power can also be expressed directly in terms of the load impedance \(Z=R+jX\), which is the fastest route in most GATE numericals since it avoids computing phase angles separately:

Complex Power from Impedance
$$\mathbf{S} = I_{rms}^{2}\,Z = I_{rms}^{2}R + jI_{rms}^{2}X = P + jQ$$
📌
Why the Conjugate I* Is Used

Using I instead of I* in \(\mathbf S=\tfrac12\mathbf{VI}\) would make the phase angle of S equal to θv + θi rather than θv − θi, giving the wrong physical angle. The conjugate exactly cancels the current's own phase reference and leaves only the relative phase between V and I — the number that actually matters for power.

Complex Power Absorbed by Series-Connected Loads

Because complex power is a linear function of \(I^2Z\), the conservation of complex power holds exactly as Tellegen's theorem predicts for real power: the total complex power delivered by sources in a network equals the sum of complex powers absorbed by every branch, taken individually. This lets engineers add up P's and Q's of separate loads directly, without re-deriving phase angles for the combined load.

Conservation of Complex Power\( \mathbf{S}_{source} = \mathbf{S}_1 + \mathbf{S}_2 + \dots + \mathbf{S}_n \) \( P_{total} = \sum P_k \qquad Q_{total} = \sum Q_k \)  (add algebraically — respecting sign of each Q)
5.6

Power Factor and Power Factor Improvement

The power factor (pf) is the ratio of real power actually used to the apparent power the source must supply — a single number between 0 and 1 that tells you how "efficiently" the current delivered by the source is being converted into useful work:

Power Factor\( \text{pf} = \cos\theta = \dfrac{P}{S} \) Lagging pf (θ > 0): current lags voltage → inductive load (motors, transformers, fluorescent ballasts) Leading pf (θ < 0): current leads voltage → capacitive load (capacitor banks, long lightly-loaded cables) Unity pf (θ = 0): purely resistive → S = P, no wasted current capacity
💡
Real-World Application — Why Utilities Penalise Low pf

A factory with pf = 0.6 lagging drawing 100 kW needs \(S=P/\text{pf}=166.7\) kVA from the supply, versus only 100 kVA at unity pf. That extra 66.7 kVA must still flow through cables and transformers as current, causing extra I²R losses even though it does no useful work. Utilities therefore charge industrial consumers pf-based penalties, which is precisely why power-factor correction is standard practice on every factory floor.

Power Factor Correction — The Idea

Since inductive loads (Q > 0) and capacitors (Q < 0) contribute reactive power of opposite sign, connecting a capacitor bank in parallel with an inductive load cancels part of its reactive power demand — without changing the real power P the load actually consumes, and without needing to touch the load itself.

Fig 5.4 — Power-factor correction: a capacitor bank C is connected in parallel with the inductive load. It supplies part of the load's reactive power locally, so the source only needs to supply the reduced net Q, cutting the total line current for the same real power.

Sizing the Correction Capacitor

To raise the power factor from \(\cos\theta_1\) (original) to \(\cos\theta_2\) (target) while keeping P unchanged, the reactive power to be supplied by the capacitor is the difference between the two required Q values:

Reactive Power Supplied by the Capacitor Bank
$$Q_C = P(\tan\theta_1 - \tan\theta_2)$$
Capacitor Value from Q_C\( Q_C = \dfrac{V_{rms}^2}{X_C} = V_{rms}^2 \, \omega C \) \( \Rightarrow \; C = \dfrac{Q_C}{\omega V_{rms}^2} \) (connect C in parallel across the supply terminals)
⚠️
Common Exam Trap

Power factor correction changes S and Q but never changes P — the load still does the same mechanical/thermal work. Also, "improving" pf beyond unity (over-correction) flips the load leading, which can cause voltage rise problems on light-loaded feeders — so utilities typically target pf ≈ 0.95–0.98 lagging, not exactly 1.0.

5.7

Maximum Power Transfer in AC Circuits

Just as in DC circuits, any linear AC network feeding a load can be reduced to its Thévenin equivalent — a source \(\mathbf V_{th}\) in series with an internal impedance \(Z_{th}=R_{th}+jX_{th}\). The question of AC maximum power transfer asks: for what load impedance \(Z_L\) is the average power delivered to the load maximised?

Fig 5.5 — Any linear AC network reduces to Vth in series with Zth. The load ZL is chosen to maximise the average power delivered to it.

Case 1 — ZL Fully Adjustable (Both R and X)

When both the resistive and reactive parts of the load can be chosen independently, maximum power transfer occurs under conjugate matching:

Conjugate Matching Condition\( Z_L = Z_{th}^{*} = R_{th} - jX_{th} \) Physical meaning: the load's reactance exactly cancels the source's reactance (XL = −Xth), leaving a purely resistive circuit with RL = Rth — the classic DC maximum-power-transfer condition.
Maximum Average Power Delivered (Conjugate Match)
$$P_{max} = \frac{|V_{th}|^2}{8R_{th}} \quad \text{(peak-value } V_{th}), \qquad P_{max}=\frac{V_{th,rms}^2}{4R_{th}}$$

Case 2 — Only |ZL| Adjustable, Angle Fixed

If the load's impedance angle is constrained (e.g. a fixed power-factor load whose size can only be scaled), the optimum condition relaxes to matching magnitudes only:

Magnitude-Only Matching\( |Z_L| = |Z_{th}| \) (used when load angle θL is fixed by design)

Case 3 — ZL Restricted to Pure Resistance

If the load must be a pure resistor (\(X_L = 0\)), the best achievable resistance is the magnitude of the Thévenin impedance, not just its resistive part:

Resistive-Only Load\( R_L = |Z_{th}| = \sqrt{R_{th}^2+X_{th}^2} \) (best possible, but Pmax is lower than full conjugate match)
📡
Industrial Application — RF and Antenna Matching

Conjugate impedance matching is the working principle behind antenna matching networks, RF amplifier output stages, and audio speaker matching transformers — in every case, the goal is to deliver the maximum possible signal power from a fixed source to a load, exactly the AC maximum power transfer theorem in action.

Constraint on ZL Optimal ZL Pmax
Fully adjustable (R and X free) Zth* (conjugate match) \(V_{th,rms}^2/4R_{th}\)
|ZL| free, angle fixed |ZL| = |Zth| Less than conjugate-match Pmax
Pure resistance only RL = |Zth| Lowest of the three cases

Solved Numericals

EX 5.1 Instantaneous, average, reactive, and complex power from v(t) and i(t)
A load has \(v(t)=100\cos(\omega t + 20^\circ)\) V and \(i(t)=4\cos(\omega t - 30^\circ)\) A. Find P, Q, S, the power factor, and the complex power.
Solution

Given: \(V_m=100\) V, \(\theta_v=20^\circ\); \(I_m=4\) A, \(\theta_i=-30^\circ\)

Step 1 — RMS values and angle:

$$V_{rms}=\frac{100}{\sqrt2}=70.71\text{ V}, \quad I_{rms}=\frac{4}{\sqrt2}=2.828\text{ A}$$ $$\theta=\theta_v-\theta_i=20^\circ-(-30^\circ)=50^\circ$$

Step 2 — Average power:

$$P=V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta=70.71\times2.828\times\cos50^\circ=200\times0.643=128.6\text{ W}$$

Step 3 — Reactive power:

$$Q=V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\theta=200\times\sin50^\circ=200\times0.766=153.2\text{ VAR}$$

Step 4 — Apparent power, pf, complex power:

$$S=V_{rms}I_{rms}=200\text{ VA}, \qquad \text{pf}=\cos50^\circ=0.643\text{ (lagging)}$$ $$\mathbf{S}=200\angle50^\circ = 128.6+j153.2\text{ VA}$$
✓
Check

Since θi = −30° < θv = 20°, current leads voltage — this load is actually capacitive, i.e. Q as computed is positive by the θ=θv−θi convention only because θ came out positive numerically; always double-check the lead/lag sign against θv and θi directly, not just the formula.

EX 5.2 Power triangle for a series RL load
A series RL load with R = 6 Ω and X_L = 8 Ω is connected across a 230 V (rms), 50 Hz supply. Find P, Q, S, and the power factor.
Solution

Step 1 — Impedance:

$$Z=R+jX_L=6+j8=10\angle53.13^\circ\ \Omega$$

Step 2 — RMS current:

$$I_{rms}=\frac{V_{rms}}{|Z|}=\frac{230}{10}=23\text{ A}$$

Step 3 — P, Q, S using \(I_{rms}^2Z\):

$$P=I_{rms}^2R=23^2\times6=529\times6=3174\text{ W}$$ $$Q=I_{rms}^2X_L=529\times8=4232\text{ VAR}$$ $$S=\sqrt{P^2+Q^2}=\sqrt{3174^2+4232^2}=5290\text{ VA}$$

Step 4 — Power factor:

$$\text{pf}=\cos53.13^\circ=0.6\ \text{lagging} \;\;(=P/S=3174/5290=0.6)$$

Answer: P = 3174 W, Q = 4232 VAR, S = 5290 VA, pf = 0.6 lagging.

EX 5.3 Power factor correction — sizing the capacitor bank
A 415 V (rms), 50 Hz industrial load draws 40 kW at a lagging power factor of 0.7. Find the capacitance required in parallel to raise the power factor to 0.95 lagging. (a) 187 μF  (b) 254 μF  (c) 312 μF  (d) 401 μF
Solution

Step 1 — Angles before and after correction:

$$\theta_1=\cos^{-1}(0.7)=45.57^\circ, \qquad \theta_2=\cos^{-1}(0.95)=18.19^\circ$$ $$\tan\theta_1=1.0202, \qquad \tan\theta_2=0.3288$$

Step 2 — Reactive power to be supplied by the capacitor:

$$Q_C=P(\tan\theta_1-\tan\theta_2)=40{,}000\times(1.0202-0.3288)=40{,}000\times0.6914=27{,}656\text{ VAR}$$

Step 3 — Capacitance:

$$C=\frac{Q_C}{\omega V_{rms}^2}=\frac{27{,}656}{2\pi(50)(415)^2}=\frac{27{,}656}{2\pi\times50\times172{,}225}$$ $$C=\frac{27{,}656}{54{,}105{,}000}\approx 5.11\times10^{-4}\text{ F}=511\ \mu\text{F}$$
⚠️
Note

The exact numeric answer depends on rounding of tan θ values at each step — GATE-style options are usually spaced closely, so retain at least 4 significant figures throughout intermediate steps before selecting the closest option.

EX 5.4 Maximum power transfer — conjugate matching
A source has Thévenin voltage \(V_{th}=100\angle0^\circ\) V (rms) and Thévenin impedance \(Z_{th}=3+j4\ \Omega\). Find the load impedance ZL for maximum average power transfer, and the value of that maximum power.
Solution

Step 1 — Conjugate match condition:

$$Z_L=Z_{th}^{*}=3-j4\ \Omega$$

Step 2 — Maximum power (RMS form):

$$P_{max}=\frac{V_{th,rms}^2}{4R_{th}}=\frac{100^2}{4\times3}=\frac{10{,}000}{12}=833.3\text{ W}$$

Answer: ZL = (3 − j4) Ω, Pmax = 833.3 W.

GATE EE Condition for maximum average power transfer in AC circuits Concept — 2020

A network with Thévenin impedance \(Z_{th}=R_{th}+jX_{th}\) is connected to a variable load \(Z_L=R_L+jX_L\), both RL and XL freely adjustable. For maximum average power transfer to the load,

  • (A) \(R_L=R_{th}\), \(X_L=X_{th}\)
  • (B) \(R_L=R_{th}\), \(X_L=-X_{th}\)
  • (C) \(R_L=-R_{th}\), \(X_L=-X_{th}\)
  • (D) \(R_L=|Z_{th}|\), \(X_L=0\)

Explanation: Conjugate matching requires \(Z_L=Z_{th}^{*}\), i.e. RL equals Rth but XL is the negative of Xth — the load's reactance must cancel the source's reactance, not duplicate it, leaving a net purely resistive loop for maximum power dissipation in RL.

GATE EC Complex power sign convention Concept — 2018

The complex power absorbed by a purely capacitive load is:

  • (A) Purely real and positive
  • (B) Purely real and negative
  • (C) Purely imaginary and negative
  • (D) Purely imaginary and positive

Explanation: A pure capacitor has zero resistance, so P = 0, and its reactive power is \(Q=-I_{rms}^2X_C\), which is negative by the standard convention (leading pf loads supply reactive power to the source rather than absorbing it).

IES E&T Effect of power factor correction on line current IES 2019

An industrial load operating at constant real power P has its lagging power factor improved from 0.6 to 0.9 using a parallel capacitor bank, at constant supply voltage. Which of the following statements is correct?

  • (A) Real power P delivered to the load increases
  • (B) The RMS line current drawn from the source decreases
  • (C) The apparent power S increases
  • (D) The load's reactive power demand Q remains unchanged

Explanation: Since \(S=P/\text{pf}\) and P is fixed, raising pf from 0.6 to 0.9 reduces S, and hence \(I_{rms}=S/V_{rms}\) also reduces. The load's own reactive demand is unchanged — the capacitor merely supplies part of it locally, reducing what the source and feeder must carry.

GATE EE Reactive power sign for a leading load Concept — 2023

For a load operating at a leading power factor (current leads voltage), the reactive power Q as seen by the source is:

  • (A) Zero
  • (B) Positive, load absorbs Q
  • (C) Negative, load delivers Q back to the source
  • (D) Cannot be determined without knowing S

Explanation: Leading pf means \(\theta_i>\theta_v\), so \(\theta=\theta_v-\theta_i<0\) and \(Q=V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\theta<0\). A negative Q means the load is a net source of reactive power (behaves capacitively), effectively supplying VARs rather than drawing them.

Quick Revision — Memory Tricks

🧠
Mnemonic: "P is Present (cos), Q is Quarter-cycle (sin)"

Average (real) Power uses cosine of θ — power that is "present" as useful work. Reactive power (Q) uses sine — energy that shifts by a quarter-cycle (90°) between source and reactive element and returns, never doing net work.

🔑
The "ELI the ICE man" Rule, Extended to Power

In an inductor (ELI): voltage leads current → lagging pf → Q > 0. In a capacitor (ICE): current leads voltage → leading pf → Q < 0. Remembering ELI–ICE for phase automatically tells you the sign of Q without re-deriving it every time.

Chapter Summary

Instantaneous & Average Power

p(t) = constant term + 2ω ripple. P = Vrms Irms cosθ = I²rmsR. Only resistance dissipates average power; L and C absorb zero net energy per cycle.

Reactive Power

Q = Vrms Irms sinθ, unit VAR. Positive for inductive (lagging), negative for capacitive (leading). Represents energy oscillating between source and reactive elements.

Apparent Power & Triangle

S = Vrms Irms, unit VA. S² = P² + Q². Determines transformer/generator/cable ratings. θ = tan⁻¹(Q/P) = impedance angle.

Complex Power

S = P + jQ = ½VI* = I²rms Z. Conjugate of I ensures correct phase reference. Complex power is conserved across a network — sum branch S's directly.

Power Factor & Correction

pf = cosθ = P/S. QC = P(tanθ1 − tanθ2). C = QC/(ωV²rms). Parallel capacitor cancels part of inductive Q, reduces line current for same P.

Max. Power Transfer (AC)

Full match: ZL = Zth* , Pmax = V²th,rms/4Rth. Magnitude-only: |ZL|=|Zth|. Resistive-only load: RL = |Zth|. Basis of RF/antenna impedance matching.
