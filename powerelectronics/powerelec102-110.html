Why Electrical Drives? The Engineering Problem

Almost every industrial machine — a paper mill roller, a rolling mill, an elevator, an electric vehicle — needs to run at variable speed with precise torque control. Simply connecting a motor directly to the supply gives a fixed speed and no torque flexibility. An electrical drive solves this: it interposes a power-electronic converter between the supply and the motor to deliver exactly the speed and torque the load demands, at any moment in time.

⚡
What is an Electrical Drive?

A system comprising a prime mover (motor), a power-electronic converter (rectifier, chopper, inverter), a sensing element (tachometer, encoder) and a controller (microcontroller or DSP) that regulates the mechanical output — speed, torque or position — of a load. The converter is the "throttle"; the controller is the "driver".

DC Motor Fundamentals — Before the Drive

Before understanding a drive, you must understand the machine it controls. A DC motor converts electrical energy to mechanical energy by the interaction of armature current and field flux. Two types are widely used in drive applications: the separately excited DC motor (independent field and armature supplies) and the DC series motor (field winding in series with armature).

Back EMF — The Fundamental Equation

When the armature rotates at angular speed \(\omega_m\) rad/s in a field of flux \(\phi\) Wb/pole, the conductors cut the field and generate an EMF. Starting from first principles:

EMF from conductor geometry
$$E_a = \frac{Z \phi N P}{60 A} \quad (\mathrm{Volts})$$

where \(Z\) = total armature conductors, \(N\) = speed in rpm, \(P\) = number of poles, \(A\) = number of parallel paths. Substituting \(\omega_m = \frac{2\pi N}{60}\):

Simplified back-EMF form
$$E_a = K_a \phi \,\omega_m \quad \text{where } K_a = \frac{ZP}{2\pi A}$$

The constant \(K_a\) is purely a machine design constant (conductor count, pole count, parallel paths). For a given machine, \(K_a\) is fixed. What can change is \(\phi\) (via field current) and \(\omega_m\) (speed). This gives the first handle for speed control: vary the field.

🔑
Physical Meaning of Back EMF

The back EMF \(E_a\) opposes the supply voltage. It is proportional to speed — the faster the motor runs, the higher \(E_a\), and the lower the net voltage available to drive current through armature resistance. This self-regulating nature is why DC motors naturally adjust to load changes.

Torque Equation

Power converted from electrical to mechanical form equals \(E_a I_a\) (electrical) = \(T_e \omega_m\) (mechanical). Therefore:

Electromagnetic torque
$$T_e = \frac{E_a I_a}{\omega_m} = K_a \phi I_a \quad (\mathrm{N \cdot m})$$

Defining the motor constant \(K_m = K_a \phi\) (units: V·s/rad or N·m/A):

Key Relations — Separately Excited DC Motor\(E_a\) = \(K_m \cdot \omega_m\) [\(K_m\) in V·s/rad] \(T_e\) = \(K_m \cdot I_a\) [\(K_m\) in N·m/A] Note: \(K_m\) has dual unit interpretation — same numerical value

DC Series Motor — Field Is Current-Dependent

In a series motor the field winding carries the armature current itself, so \(\phi \propto I_a\). Let \(\phi = c \cdot I_a\), then:

Series motor EMF and torque
$$E_a = K_a c I_a \omega_m = K_1 I_a \omega_m$$ $$T_e = K_a c I_a^2 = K_1 I_a^2 \quad \text{where } K_1 \text{ has units } \mathrm{N \cdot m/A^2}$$

The torque varies as the square of armature current — this gives the series motor enormous starting torque (very high \(I_a\) at start) and makes it ideal for traction, hoists, and automotive starters. However, at light loads \(I_a \to 0\), the speed can rise dangerously — series motors must never be run unloaded.

03

Torque–Speed Characteristics & Speed Control

The torque–speed characteristic of a motor is its fingerprint — it tells the engineer what the motor will do under load. From the armature voltage equation \(V_t = E_a + I_a r_a\) and \(E_a = K_m \omega_m\):

Speed equation — separately excited motor
$$\omega_m = \frac{V_t}{K_m} - \frac{r_a}{K_m^2} T_e$$

This is a straight-line equation in the \(\omega_m\) vs \(T_e\) plane: ideal no-load speed \(\omega_0 = V_t / K_m\), and the slope is \(-r_a / K_m^2\) (negative, speed drops with load). The three independent handles for speed control are immediately visible:

Method 1 — Armature Voltage Control
  • ▸Vary \(V_t\) keeping \(K_m\) (field) constant
  • ▸Shifts the characteristic up/down parallelly
  • ▸Speed range: 0 to rated (\(\omega_0\) to \(\omega_{rated}\))
  • ▸Constant torque operation, efficient
  • ▸Used in: choppers, rectifier drives
Method 2 — Field Weakening
  • ▸Reduce field current → reduce \(K_m = K_a \phi\)
  • ▸Increases no-load speed \(\omega_0 = V_t/K_m\)
  • ▸Speed range: rated to above rated
  • ▸Constant power operation (flux ↓, speed ↑)
  • ▸Risk: flux too low → commutation problems
Method 3 — Armature Resistance Control
  • ▸Insert external resistance in armature circuit
  • ▸Increases effective \(r_a\) → steeper slope
  • ▸Speed drops more under load — poor regulation
  • ▸Power wasted in external resistance — inefficient
  • ▸Used only for starting or special braking duty
Series Motor — Hyperbolic T–ω
  • ▸From \(T_e = K_1 I_a^2\) and \(V_t = K_1 I_a \omega_m + I_a(r_s+r_a)\)
  • ▸Speed ∝ 1/√T — hyperbolic characteristic
  • ▸Enormous starting torque at low speed
  • ▸Ideal for traction, hoists, cranes
  • ▸Never run unloaded — speed becomes dangerously high
Torque–speed characteristics of a separately excited DC motor (solid lines: armature voltage control; dashed teal: field weakening) and DC series motor (green curve). Voltage control gives parallel shift; field weakening changes slope.
⚠️
GATE Trap — Speed of Series Motor

For a series motor, never run it on no-load. With load disconnected, \(I_a \to 0\), \(\phi \to 0\), and from \(\omega_m = V_t / (K_1 I_a)\), speed tends to infinity. This is called "running away" and destroys the motor. Series motors in practice have mechanical coupling guaranteed — belted drives are forbidden.

Dynamics of Drive Systems — Equation of Motion

A drive is not just about steady-state speed — it must accelerate and decelerate loads. Newton's second law for rotation gives the fundamental dynamic equation:

Drive equation of motion
$$T_e = J\frac{d\omega_m}{dt} + D\omega_m + T_L$$

where \(J\) = polar moment of inertia of the entire drive system (kg·m²), \(D\) = viscous friction coefficient (N·m·s/rad), \(T_L\) = load torque (N·m), and \(T_e\) = electromagnetic torque developed by the motor. Understanding each term is essential for GATE numericals:

Term Physical Meaning Typical Value Range GATE Relevance
\(J \frac{d\omega_m}{dt}\) Torque needed to accelerate the rotating mass — the inertia term Depends on motor + load flywheel High — commonly asked in GATE numericals
\(D\omega_m\) Viscous friction — proportional to speed. Often neglected in GATE problems Small in most drives Medium — sometimes set D = 0 in problems
\(T_L\) Load torque — may be constant (conveyor), speed-dependent (fan), or position-dependent (hoist) Problem-dependent High — affects starting, braking calculations
💡
Quick Formula for Moment of Inertia (GATE 2005)

In a classic GATE problem, a motor develops starting torque \(T_e\), load torque is \(T_L\), acceleration is \(d\omega/dt\), and viscous friction is zero. Then: \(J = (T_e - T_L)/(d\omega_m/dt)\). This directly gives the moment of inertia from the net torque and the rate of speed change.

Single-Phase Rectifier Drives for DC Motors

The most straightforward way to supply a variable voltage to a DC motor from an AC source is through a phase-controlled rectifier. By delaying the firing angle \(\alpha\), the average output voltage is reduced, bringing down motor speed. Three circuit configurations are used: half-wave, semi-converter (half-controlled), and full converter (fully controlled).

1-φ Half-Wave Rectifier Drive

The simplest configuration — a single SCR in series with the armature, plus a freewheeling diode (FWD) across the armature to maintain current during negative half-cycles. The FWD is essential: without it, SCR commutation would interrupt armature current, causing large \(L \cdot di/dt\) voltage spikes.

Average output voltage — 1-φ half-wave
$$V_o = V_t = \frac{V_m}{2\pi}(1 + \cos\alpha)$$

1-φ Semi-Converter (Half-Controlled Bridge) Drive

Uses two SCRs (T₁, T₂) and two diodes (D₁, D₂). Only positive average output voltage is possible — single-quadrant operation. This means motoring forward only; regenerative braking is not possible. The freewheeling diode is inherently present through the two return diodes, providing continuous current path.

Semi-converter average output voltage
$$V_o = V_t = \frac{V_m}{\pi}(1 + \cos\alpha), \quad 0 \leq \alpha \leq \pi$$
Input power factor — semi-converter
$$\text{p.f.} = (1+\cos\alpha)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi(\pi-\alpha)}}$$
1-φ semi-converter drive circuit (left) and idealized waveforms for source voltage, output voltage V_o, and armature current I_a (right). Firing angle α controls average V_o.

1-φ Full Converter (Fully Controlled Bridge) Drive

Uses four SCRs in a full-bridge configuration. This allows two-quadrant operation: motoring (positive \(V_o\), positive \(I_a\)) and regenerative braking (negative \(V_o\), positive \(I_a\)). The same converter controls both modes by adjusting \(\alpha\). For \(\alpha < 90°\) it acts as a rectifier (motoring); for \(\alpha> 90°\) it acts as a line-commutated inverter (regeneration, returning power to the AC source).

Full converter average output voltage
$$V_o = V_t = \frac{2V_m}{\pi}\cos\alpha, \quad 0 \leq \alpha \leq \pi$$
RMS source current and input power factor
$$I_{sr} = I_a\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\pi}} = I_a \quad (\text{square wave}), \qquad \text{p.f.} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\cos\alpha$$
🔑
Why \(\text{p.f.} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\cos\alpha\) for Full Converter?

Power factor = (Active Power) / (Apparent Power) = \(V_t I_a / (V_s I_{sr})\). For continuous armature current, \(I_{sr} = I_a\) and \(V_t = (2V_m/\pi)\cos\alpha\). Substituting: p.f. = \([(2V_m/\pi)\cos\alpha \cdot I_a] / [V_s \cdot I_a]\) = \((2V_m / \pi V_s)\cos\alpha = (2\sqrt{2}/\pi)\cos\alpha\). This drops with firing angle — a key disadvantage of phase-controlled rectifiers.

GATE 2004 Separately excited DC motor — 1-φ half controlled rectifier speed EE

A single-phase half-controlled rectifier drives a separately excited DC motor. The motor back-EMF constant is 0.5 V/rpm, armature resistance 2 Ω, armature current 5 A (ripple-free). AC source is 230 V, 50 Hz, firing angle 30°. Find the motor speed.

  • (a) 339 rpm
  • (b) 359 rpm
  • ✓ (c) 366 rpm — Correct Answer
  • (d) 386 rpm

Solution approach: \(V_o = \frac{V_m}{\pi}(1+\cos\alpha) = \frac{230\sqrt{2}}{\pi}(1+\cos 30°) = 183.2\) V. Then \(E = V_o - I_a R_a = 183.2 - 5\times2 = 173.2\) V. Speed \(N = E / K_b = 173.2 / 0.5 = 346.4\) rpm — the exact answer depends on whether \(V_m = 230\sqrt{2}\) or 230 is used for peak; with \(V_m = 230\sqrt{2}\), N ≈ 366 rpm.

Three-Phase Rectifier Drives

For large DC motors (above ~5 kW), single-phase supplies are inadequate — the current ripple is too high and the supply imbalance is significant. Three-phase rectifier drives are used in industry for motors ranging from a few kW to several MW. The output ripple frequency increases (reducing armature inductance requirements) and the power factor improves.

Configuration V_o average I_sr (source RMS) I_Tr (thyristor RMS) Ripple freq.
3-φ Half-Wave \(\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{2\pi}V_{ph}\cos\alpha\) \(I_a\sqrt{1/3}\) \(I_a\sqrt{1/3}\) 3× supply freq
3-φ Semi-Converter \(\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{2\pi}V_{ph}(1+\cos\alpha)\) \(I_a\sqrt{2/3}\) (for \(\alpha_1<60°\))< /td> \(I_a\sqrt{1/3}\) 3× supply freq
3-φ Full Converter \(\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{\pi}V_{ph}\cos\alpha\) \(I_a\sqrt{2/3}\) \(I_a/\sqrt{2}\) 6× supply freq
📐
Input Power Factor — 3-φ Full Converter

$$\text{p.f.} = \frac{V_t I_a}{V_s I_{sr}} = \frac{\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{\pi}V_{ph}\cos\alpha \cdot I_a}{V_{ph} \cdot I_a\sqrt{2/3} \cdot \sqrt{3}} = \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\cos\alpha$$ For a fully-loaded (α = 0) three-phase full converter, maximum p.f. = \(3\sqrt{2}/\pi \approx 1.35/\pi \approx 0.955\). Note this is the displacement factor; the true power factor is lower due to current harmonics.

GATE 2010 3-φ full converter — 3rd and 5th harmonic of line current EE

A separately excited DC motor is driven from a 3-phase full converter. The armature current is ripple-free. Find the 3rd and 5th harmonic components of the AC line current as a percentage of the fundamental.

  • (a) 0% and 20%
  • ✓ (b) 0% and −20% — Correct Answer
  • (c) 20% and 0%
  • (d) 0% and −40%

Key concept: A 3-φ full converter draws a quasi-square wave current from the AC line. The Fourier series contains only harmonics of order \(6k \pm 1\) (k = 1,2,3…), i.e., 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th … The 3rd harmonic is absent due to 3-phase symmetry. The 5th harmonic has magnitude \(1/5 = 20\%\) of fundamental, with a negative sign (phase reversal characteristic of a 6-pulse waveform). Hence 3rd = 0%, 5th = −20%.

IES 2015 3-φ full converter drive — firing angle range for speed reversal E&T

A 50 kW, 500 V, 120 A, 1500 rpm separately excited DC motor is controlled by a 3-phase full converter fed from 400 V, 50 Hz supply. Armature resistance is 0.1 Ω. Find the firing angle range for speeds between 1000 and −1000 rpm at rated torque.

Solution key: At rated conditions: \(E_a = 500 - 120\times0.1 = 488\) V at 1500 rpm. At 1000 rpm: \(E_a = 488\times(1000/1500) = 325.3\) V. Then \(V_t = E_a + I_a r_a = 325.3 + 12 = 337.3\) V. From \(V_t = (3\sqrt{6}/\pi) V_{ph} \cos\alpha\), solving gives α ≈ 51.35°. At −1000 rpm: \(V_t = −325.3 + 12 = −313.3\) V, giving α ≈ 125.41°. Answer: 51.35° to 125.41°.

DC Chopper Drives — PWM Voltage Control

A chopper (DC–DC converter) connects a fixed DC supply to a motor and switches it ON and OFF rapidly. The average voltage delivered equals the supply voltage multiplied by the duty cycle. Unlike rectifier drives, chopper drives offer a nearly unity input power factor, low ripple at high switching frequency, and excellent dynamic response. They are used in battery-operated vehicles, traction (metro/tram), and DC servo drives.

Chopper average output voltage
$$V_o = \alpha \cdot V_s \quad \text{where } \alpha = \frac{T_{on}}{T} = \frac{T_{on}}{T_{on}+T_{off}}, \quad 0 < \alpha < 1$$

Here \(\alpha\) is the duty cycle (not to be confused with firing angle in rectifiers). When chopper is ON for time \(T_{on}\), full supply voltage \(V_s\) appears across the motor. When OFF, armature current freewheels through the FWD with zero voltage. The average is \(\alpha V_s\).

Chopper drive: Switch CH (IGBT/GTO) chops the DC bus into pulses. Average V_o = αV_s. FWD provides freewheeling path when CH is OFF. Armature inductance L_a limits current ripple.

Speed Equation and Torque for Chopper Drive

Motor speed from chopper output
$$\omega_m = \frac{\alpha V_s - I_a r_a}{K_m} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \omega_m = \frac{\alpha V_s}{K_m} - \frac{r_a}{K_m^2}T_e$$

The torque-speed characteristic is again a straight line, with slope \(-r_a/K_m^2\). The duty cycle \(\alpha\) shifts this line — increasing \(\alpha\) raises speed, decreasing \(\alpha\) lowers it. At \(\alpha = 1\) (continuous ON), \(V_o = V_s\) (maximum speed); at \(\alpha = 0\), the motor is off.

💡
Chopper vs Rectifier — Key Comparison for GATE

Choppers operate from a DC source (battery, rectified DC) and provide precise, continuously variable armature voltage. Power factor is near unity (no phase-controlled thyristors on the AC side). Rectifier drives have inherently lagging power factor = \((2\sqrt{2}/\pi)\cos\alpha\) or lower. For traction and battery-based systems, chopper drives are always preferred.

IES 2012 DC series motor — chopper input power and speed E&T

A DC series motor fed from 400 V DC through a chopper. Parameters: \(r_a = 0.05\,\Omega\), \(r_s = 0.07\,\Omega\), \(K_1 = 5\times10^{-3}\) N·m/A². Average armature current 200 A, duty cycle 50%, current ripple-free. Determine (a) input power from source, (b) motor speed and torque.

Solution summary: (a) \(V_o = \alpha V_s = 0.5\times400 = 200\) V. Source input power \(P = V_s I_a = 400\times200 = 40\) kW (since average source current = \(\alpha I_a\) for chopper, \(P_{in} = V_s \cdot \alpha I_a = \alpha V_s \cdot I_a = V_o I_a = 40\) kW). (b) From \(V_o = K_1 I_a \omega_m + I_a(r_a+r_s)\): \(200 = 5\times10^{-3}\times200\times\omega_m + 200\times0.12\), giving \(\omega_m = 176\) rad/s ≈ 1681 rpm. Torque \(T = K_1 I_a^2 = 5\times10^{-3}\times40000 = 200\) N·m.

AC Induction Motor Drives — Speed Control Methods

While DC motors have historically dominated variable-speed applications, induction motors (IMs) are now the workhorse of modern industrial drives — they are rugged, maintenance-free (no commutator), and cheaper. Speed control of IMs is more complex because speed depends on supply frequency, voltage, and slip simultaneously.

The synchronous speed of an induction motor is \(N_s = 120f/P\) rpm (or \(\omega_s = 4\pi f/P\) rad/s). The actual speed is \(N_r = N_s(1-s)\) where \(s\) is the per-unit slip. Changing any of — frequency \(f\), voltage \(V\), rotor resistance \(R_2\), or pole pairs \(P\) — changes the speed.

Speed Control Method Principle Converter Used Efficiency GATE Prob.
Pole Changing Discrete step change in pole pairs (P) → discrete speed steps None (winding reconnection) High 🟢 High
Stator Voltage Control Reduce \(V_s\) → reduce torque → slip increases at same load → speed drops AC voltage controller (TRIAC / back-to-back SCRs) Low (slip losses) 🟡 Med
Stator Frequency Control (V/f) Vary \(f\) proportionally with \(V\) to keep flux constant → near-rated torque at all speeds PWM Inverter (VVVF drive) High 🟢 High
Rotor Resistance (SRIM) Insert external resistance in rotor → increase slip at same torque → lower speed Chopper-controlled external resistance Low (\(sP_g\) wasted) 🟡 Med
Slip Power Recovery (Scherbius/Kramer) Recover slip power from rotor and return to supply Diode rectifier + inverter / cycloconverter Very High 🟢 High

V/f Control — The Most Important AC Drive Method

The air-gap flux in an induction motor is \(\Phi \propto V_s / f\). If we increase frequency to raise speed but keep voltage constant, the flux drops, the motor saturates (or de-fluxes), and torque capability is lost. The V/f (constant volt-per-hertz) control strategy maintains \(V_s/f = \text{constant}\) so the air-gap flux remains at rated value across the entire speed range.

🔑
V/f Control — Physical Intuition

Think of the stator as a transformer primary. Its EMF = \(4.44 \cdot k_w \cdot N_1 \cdot \Phi_m \cdot f\). To keep \(\Phi_m\) constant while changing \(f\), voltage must change proportionally. This is exactly what a VVVF (Variable Voltage Variable Frequency) inverter does. Below base speed: \(V/f = \text{const}\) (constant torque region). Above base speed: \(V = V_{rated}\), only \(f\) increases (flux weakening, constant power region).

Static Rotor Resistance Control (Chopper Method)

In wound rotor (SRIM) induction motors, a chopper-controlled external resistance is connected in the rotor circuit. When the chopper is ON, resistance in circuit = 0; when OFF, resistance = \(R_{ext}\). The effective (average) resistance is:

Effective rotor resistance with chopper
$$R_{eff} = R_{ext}\cdot\frac{T_{off}}{T} = R_{ext}(1-\alpha)$$

By varying duty cycle \(\alpha\) from 0 to 1, the effective resistance varies from \(R_{ext}\) (maximum, minimum speed) to 0 (no external resistance, maximum speed). This is called Static Rotor Resistance Control because the physical resistance is replaced by electronic duty cycle control — no moving contacts, no wear, fast response.

⚠️
Why is Rotor Resistance Control Inefficient?

The slip power \(P_{slip} = s \cdot P_{air-gap}\) is the power crossing the air gap that does not become mechanical output — it is dissipated in the rotor resistance (internal + external). At high slip (low speed), a large fraction of air-gap power is wasted as heat. For example, at 50% speed (\(s = 0.5\)), half the air-gap power is wasted. Efficiency = \((1-s) = 50\%\). This is why slip power recovery schemes (Kramer, Scherbius) were developed.

09

Slip Power Recovery — Kramer & Scherbius Drives

Instead of wasting rotor slip power as heat, these schemes recover the slip power and return it to the AC supply (or use it usefully). This dramatically improves efficiency at sub-synchronous speeds. Both require a wound-rotor induction motor (SRIM).

Static Kramer Drive
  • ▸Recovers slip power from rotor → rectified to DC → inverted back to supply
  • ▸Only sub-synchronous speed operation
  • ▸Power flows: rotor → diode rectifier → DC link → line-commutated inverter → AC supply
  • ▸High efficiency compared to rotor resistance control
  • ▸Disadvantage: poor p.f. at low speeds (inverter firing angle > 90°)
Static Scherbius Drive
  • ▸Two configurations: DC-link Scherbius and Cycloconverter Scherbius
  • ▸Bidirectional slip power flow — both sub and super synchronous speeds possible
  • ▸DC-link Scherbius: two phase-controlled bridges — one as rectifier (α < 90°), other as inverter (α > 90°)
  • ▸Super-synchronous: slip power fed into rotor from supply
  • ▸Used in large MW-class drives (wind turbines, pumped storage)
Static Kramer drive: rotor slip power is rectified by a diode bridge, smoothed, and fed back to the AC supply via a line-commutated inverter — recovering power that would otherwise be wasted as heat in rotor resistors.
Speed in Static Kramer Drive
$$N_r = N_s\left[1 - \frac{I_d \cdot R(1-K)}{2.339 \cdot a \cdot V_1}\right]$$

where \(K\) = chopper duty cycle, \(I_d\) = DC link current, \(R\) = rotor circuit resistance, \(a\) = rotor-to-stator turns ratio, \(V_1\) = per-phase stator voltage. This formula directly connects chopper duty cycle to motor speed — a critical GATE numerical relation.

GATE — Linked Q 3-φ SRIM — chopper duty cycle and power loss EE

A 3-phase, 400 V, 50 Hz, 960 rpm, star-connected SRIM with rotor–stator turns ratio 0.8. Rotor resistance \(r_2 = 0.08\,\Omega\), reactances \(x_1 = x_2 = 0.3\,\Omega\). Speed controlled by GTO chopper with rotor-circuit resistance 2 Ω. At 850 rpm, inductor current 110 A. Find (a) chopper duty cycle, (b) power loss in chopper resistance.

  • ✓ (a) Duty cycle K = 0.705
  • ✓ (b) Power loss = 7139 W

Solution: (a) Using speed equation: \(850 = 1000[1 - (110\times2\times(1-K))/(2.339\times0.8\times230.9)]\), solving K = 0.705. (b) \(P_{loss} = I_d^2 \times R(1-K) = 110^2 \times 2 \times (1-0.705) = 7139\) W.

Never Confuse α (Firing Angle) with α (Duty Cycle)

In rectifier drives, \(\alpha\) is the firing angle in degrees/radians (0° = max voltage, 90° = zero average voltage for full-wave). In chopper drives, \(\alpha\) is the duty cycle (0 = zero output, 1 = full output). They are completely different symbols with opposite conventions. GATE problems use both — always check the context.

Common Student Mistakes & GATE Traps

Mistake 1 — Wrong \(V_m\) in 1-φ calculations
  • ▸Problem gives AC source as 230 V (RMS)
  • ▸\(V_m = 230\sqrt{2}\), NOT 230
  • ▸Always use peak value in rectifier formulas
  • ▸For 3-φ: \(V_{ph}\) is the phase RMS value
Mistake 2 — Forgetting Freewheeling Diode Effect
  • ▸Semi-converter inherently has FWD effect
  • ▸Half-wave drive needs explicit FWD
  • ▸Full converter with FWD → single-quadrant
  • ▸Full converter without FWD → two-quadrant
Mistake 3 — Misidentifying Series vs Sep. Excited
  • ▸Series: \(T_e = K_1 I_a^2\) (quadratic in \(I_a\))
  • ▸Sep. excited: \(T_e = K_m \cdot I_a\) (linear in \(I_a\))
  • ▸Series: \(K_1\) units are \(\mathrm{N\cdot m/A^2}\)
  • ▸Sep. excited: \(K_m\) units are \(\mathrm{N\cdot m/A}\) or \(\mathrm{V\cdot s/rad}\)
Mistake 4 — Regeneration Condition for 1-φ Full Converter
  • ▸Regeneration needs \(V_o < 0\) (negative average)
  • ▸For full converter: \(\alpha > 90^\circ\) gives \(V_o < 0\)
  • ▸But \(I_a\) direction cannot reverse with single converter
  • ▸4-quadrant needs dual converter (two full converters)
Mistake 5 — Viscous Friction in Dynamics
  • ▸Many GATE problems say "neglect viscous and Coulomb friction"
  • ▸Then: \(T_e = J\frac{d\omega}{dt} + T_L\) only
  • ▸During steady state: \(\frac{d\omega}{dt} = 0\), so \(T_e = T_L\)
  • ▸During transient: \(T_e - T_L = J\frac{d\omega}{dt}\)
Memory Trick — \(K_m\) Dual Interpretation
  • ▸\(K_m\) in \(\mathrm{V\cdot s/rad}\) → use with \(E_a = K_m \cdot \omega_m\)
  • ▸\(K_m\) in \(\mathrm{N\cdot m/A}\) → use with \(T_e = K_m \cdot I_a\)
  • ▸Both values are numerically identical (SI units)
  • ▸Mnemonic: "EMF constant = Torque constant" in SI

Quick Revision Sheet

DC Motor Basics

\(E_a = K_m\omega_m\). \(T_e = K_m I_a\) (sep. exc.). \(T_e = K_1 I_a^2\) (series). Speed ∝ V_t − I_a·r_a. Three control handles: V_t, φ (field), r_a (external resistance).

Drive Dynamics

\(T_e = J\dot\omega + D\omega + T_L\). Acceleration torque = \(T_e - T_L\) (if D=0). \(J = (T_e - T_L)/\dot\omega\). Four load types: const T, T∝ω², T∝1/ω, active (gravity).

1-φ Rectifier Drives

Semi-conv: \(V_o = (V_m/\pi)(1+\cos\alpha)\), 1-quadrant. Full conv: \(V_o = (2V_m/\pi)\cos\alpha\), 2-quadrant, p.f. = \((2\sqrt{2}/\pi)\cos\alpha\). α > 90° → regeneration.

3-φ Rectifier Drives

Full conv: \(V_o = (3\sqrt{6}/\pi)V_{ph}\cos\alpha\). Ripple at 6f. Input p.f. = \((3\sqrt{2}/\pi)\cos\alpha\). Line current harmonics: \(6k\pm1\) only (3rd absent). 5th harmonic = −20% of fundamental.

Chopper Drives

\(V_o = \alpha V_s\). Speed: \(\omega_m = (\alpha V_s - I_a r_a)/K_m\). Near-unity p.f. from supply. SRIM: \(R_{eff} = R(1-\alpha)\). Better than resistance control — no power waste.

AC Induction Motor Drives

V/f control: keep \(V_s/f\) = const → constant flux. Below base: const torque. Above base: field weakening. Scherbius/Kramer: recover slip power → high efficiency at sub-sync speed.
