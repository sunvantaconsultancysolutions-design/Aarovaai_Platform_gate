2.1

Why Starting Is a Problem in Induction Motors

When rated voltage is applied across the stator of an induction motor at standstill (N = 0), the slip s = 1. Under this condition, the rotor behaves exactly like the secondary of a short-circuited transformer — the rotor EMF is at its maximum, and since the rotor circuit impedance is low, an extremely large rotor current flows.

The stator must supply this rotor current through mutual flux, so the stator also draws a very large current from the supply — typically 5 to 8 times the full-load current. This inrush is momentary (lasts only until the motor accelerates), but it causes:

Problems
  • ▸Severe voltage dip in the supply lines, disturbing other loads
  • ▸Excessive heating in stator windings during start
  • ▸High mechanical stress on motor shaft & coupling
  • ▸Tripping of overload protection devices
Key Insight
  • ▸Motors are rugged and can withstand starting current
  • ▸Problem is the effect on the supply system
  • ▸Small motors (<5 kW) may start directly (DOL)
  • ▸Larger motors need reduced voltage / rotor resistance methods
📐
Core Relationship

The rotor current at standstill: \(I_2 = \dfrac{E_2}{R_2 + jX_2}\). Since \(E_2\) is maximum at s=1 and \((R_2 + jX_2)\) is fixed, \(I_2\) is very large. All starting methods work by either reducing \(E_2\) (reduced voltage) or increasing \(R_2\) (rotor resistance starting).

2.2

Direct Online (DOL) Starting

In DOL starting, the stator is directly connected to the full supply voltage using a push-on / push-off switch. The motor experiences full rated voltage at the moment of starting, so the starting current is maximum but the starting torque is also maximum.

Fig 2.1 — DOL starter circuit with contactor, bimetallic overload relay, and undervoltage protection. No current-limiting element in series.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §6.11; Chapman [1], §6.8
Advantages
  • ✓Simplest and cheapest starter
  • ✓Maximum starting torque
  • ✓Push ON / Push OFF operation
  • ✓Two protections built-in
Disadvantages
  • ✗Starting current 5–8× full-load current
  • ✗Severe voltage dip in supply lines
  • ✗Only suitable for small motors (<5 kW single-phase, <5 kW three-phase)
  • ✗Not a reduced voltage starter
⚡
Two Protections in DOL

Overload protection: Thermal bimetallic elements trip on overcurrent. Undervoltage protection: Contactor coil de-energises when power fails — prevents unintentional restart when power is restored. This is a critical safety feature.

2.3

Stator Resistance / Reactance Starting

External resistors (or reactors) are inserted in series with each stator phase at starting. This creates a voltage drop across the external impedance, so the voltage appearing across the motor stator terminals is reduced — reducing both starting current and starting EMF.

Let the fraction of full voltage applied be x (where x < 1). Since torque ∝ (voltage)², the starting torque is reduced by x², while starting current is reduced only by x (linearly).

Fig 2.2 — Stator resistance starting: external resistors in series reduce the terminal voltage to xV. Bypassed by contactor after motor picks up speed.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §6.12; Bimbhra [3], §5.11
Key Formulae — Stator Resistance / Reactance Startingx = fraction of full supply voltage applied (x < 1) Starting current : Ist = x × If_DOL Starting torque : Tst/Tf = x² × (Ist_DOL/If)² × sF Torque penalty is SQUARE of current reduction. Reactors: efficiency (η) not greatly affected but power factor is low lagging.
⚠️
Important Distinction

With stator resistance: supply current = motor current (series circuit) → current from supply is reduced by x. With autotransformer: supply current is reduced by x², which is a greater benefit — hence autotransformer is preferred for large motors despite higher cost.

2.4

Autotransformer Starting

An autotransformer with tapping ratio x (x < 1) steps down the supply voltage to xV at the motor terminals. The motor starting current from the motor side is x times the DOL starting current. However, because the autotransformer is a transformer, the supply-side current is further reduced by x — making the supply current x² times the DOL starting current.

Fig 2.3 — Autotransformer starter with selectable tappings (60%, 70%, 80%, 90%). Supply current reduced by x², motor current reduced by x. Used for large cage motors.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §6.13; Chapman [1], §6.8
Autotransformer — Key RelationsTapping ratio: x (e.g. 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9) Motor terminal volt: V_motor = x × V_supply Motor start current: I_st_motor = x × I_DOL (at motor terminals) Supply-side current: I_st_supply = x² × I_DOL (transformer action!) Torque ratio: Tst/Tf = x² × (Ist_DOL/If)² × sF Compare with stator resistance: same torque formula x², but supply current with AT = x²×I_DOL vs SR = x×I_DOL → AT is more effective in reducing supply disturbance.
💡
GATE Trap: Why AT Reduces Supply Current by x² not x

Motor draws x·I_DOL at motor voltage xV. The autotransformer secondary current is x·I_DOL. Primary current = (secondary turns/primary turns) × secondary current = x × (x·I_DOL) = x²·I_DOL. This "double reduction" makes AT far superior for large motors.

Advantages
  • ✓Flexible tapping for optimum torque/current
  • ✓Supply current reduced by x² (best of all reduced-voltage methods)
  • ✓Good η during starting (transformer losses only)
  • ✓Preferred for large motors (>25 kW)
Disadvantages
  • ✗Most expensive of the cage motor starters
  • ✗Requires a suitable 3-phase autotransformer
  • ✗Bulkier than other starters
2.5

Star-Delta (Y-Δ) Starting

Star-delta starting is the most common and economical reduced-voltage starting method for squirrel cage motors in the 5–30 kW range. It requires only a single TPDT (Triple Pole Double Throw) switch — no external resistors or transformers.

Principle: The motor stator is designed for delta (Δ) connection at rated speed. During starting, it is connected in star (Y), which reduces the phase voltage to V/√3. When the motor accelerates to about 70–90% of synchronous speed, the switch throws to delta, applying full phase voltage V.

Fig 2.4 — Star (Y) connection during starting reduces phase voltage to V/√3. Switch throws to delta (Δ) when motor reaches ≈80% of synchronous speed for normal rated operation. Uses TPDT switch only.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §6.14; Chapman [1], §6.8
Star-Delta — Quantitative AnalysisDuring START (Y connection): V_phase(Y) = V_L / √3 I_st(Y) = (V_L/√3) / Z_ph = I_st_DOL / 3 [current ratio = 1/3] During RUN (Δ connection): V_phase(Δ) = V_L (full phase voltage) Starting Torque Ratio: Tst/Tf = (1/3) × (Ist_DOL/If)² × sF Both starting current AND torque are reduced to 1/3 of DOL values. Current from supply = 1/3 × I_DOL (line current in star)
⚠️
Limitation: Voltage Surge at Switchover

When switching from Y to Δ, there is a momentary transient (voltage surge) because the motor EMF and supply are not in phase at the instant of switching. This can cause a current spike. For this reason, star-delta is not suitable for motors >3.3 kV (those motors are usually designed for star connection anyway).

Comparison: Why Star-Delta vs Autotransformer at x = 1/√3?

An autotransformer at x = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577 gives x² = 1/3, exactly the same torque and supply current as star-delta. The difference: with AT the motor sees xV throughout starting, with Y-Δ there is a hard switchover. AT is more controllable but more expensive. Star-delta is preferred for the 5–30 kW range.

2.6

Rotor Resistance Starting (Slip-Ring / Wound Rotor IM)

External resistance is inserted into the rotor circuit via slip rings and brushes. This method is only applicable to wound-rotor (slip-ring) induction motors. The stator receives full rated voltage — so starting current from the supply is not as severely large as in DOL cage motors, and the starting torque is dramatically increased.

Fig 2.5 — Rotor resistance starter: external resistance in rotor circuit via slip rings. Stator gets full voltage. Resistance stepped out as motor accelerates. Improves starting torque dramatically.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §6.15; Bimbhra [3], §5.13
Rotor Resistance — PhysicsTorque: T ∝ (E₂)² × R₂ / (R₂² + s²X₂²) At standstill (s=1): T ∝ E₂² × R₂ / (R₂² + X₂²) Maximum torque when: R₂(total) = X₂ ⟹ T_max at s=1 (standstill!) This is why rotor resistance starting is the ONLY method that can give maximum torque at starting. Rotor copper loss = s × Power transferred to rotor At high s: more loss → not suitable for long-duration or wide-range speed control.
Advantages
  • ✓Technically best starting method
  • ✓Starting torque can equal maximum torque
  • ✓Smooth acceleration as resistance is cut out
  • ✓Reduces starting current while increasing torque
  • ✓Preferred for high-inertia loads (electric traction)
Disadvantages
  • ✗Only for wound-rotor (slip-ring) motors
  • ✗Slip rings, brushes need maintenance
  • ✗High copper loss in external resistance
  • ✗Motor is more expensive than cage type
2.7

Speed Control — Stator Side Methods

Induction motors are less popular than DC shunt motors for speed control because even though the T-s characteristics are identical in shape, DC motors offer far more effective and efficient control. Despite this, speed control of IMs is important for variable-speed drives.

Speed: \(N = N_s(1-s) = \dfrac{120f}{P}(1-s)\). Control can be from stator (supply voltage, frequency, or pole changing) or rotor side (resistance, slip power recovery, cascading).

Method 1 — Supply Voltage Control

The supply voltage is varied (using an autotransformer or thyristor controller). Since \(T \propto V^2\), reducing voltage shifts the T-s curve down. For constant load torque, the motor reacts by increasing its slip — so speed reduces.

Fig 2.6 — Supply voltage control: reducing V shifts T-s curve down (T ∝ V²). Motor operates at higher slip for same load torque. Inefficient — more I²R loss at lower speed.
⚠️
Inefficiency of Voltage Control

At reduced voltage, for the same load torque, the motor runs at higher slip. Rotor copper loss = s × air-gap power → higher slip means more copper loss. The motor draws more current for the same mechanical output, becoming inefficient. Hence voltage control is not preferred for wide-range or long-duration speed control.

Method 2 — Variable Frequency Control (V/f Control)

Frequency is varied by a power electronic converter (inverter/VFD). Since synchronous speed \(N_s = 120f/P\), varying f directly controls speed. To avoid core saturation, the V/f ratio must be kept constant:

V/f (Variable Frequency Drive) — Key RelationsStator EMF: V₁ ≈ E ≈ 4.44 f N₁ Bm A Maintain flux: Bm ∝ V/f = constant Max torque: Tmax ∝ (1/Ns) × E₂²/(2X₂) = (3×60/2πNs) × E₂²/(2X₂) At reduced f: Ns decreases → Ns1 = (f/f1)×Ns E2 decreases → E21 = (f/f1)×E2 X2 decreases → X21 = (f/f1)×X2 Therefore: Tmax ∝ E₂²/(Ns×X₂) = constant! Within V/f control range: maximum and breakdown torque remain the same.
💡
V/f Control — GATE Key Point

When V/f is kept constant, the slip at maximum torque \(s_m = R_2/X_2\) changes (since X₂ = 2πfL₂ varies with f, while R₂ is constant). At lower frequencies, \(s_m\) increases. This means the T-s curves shift but the maximum torque is preserved — a key advantage over voltage control. Requires an expensive power electronic converter.

Method 3 — Pole Changing

Only gives discrete speed steps. Two methods: (a) Multiple windings — two independent sets of windings in the stator slots, designed for different pole numbers (e.g. 2-pole and 4-pole). Depending on speed requirement, one is connected to supply, other is disconnected. Increases motor size and cost.

(b) Consequent pole changing — a single winding is reconnected externally to produce different pole formations. Only squirrel cage rotors are suitable since the rotor automatically adjusts to any pole number on the stator. Gives only 2:1 speed ratio per winding set.

Fig 2.7 — Consequent pole changing: same winding reconnected to produce 2× poles → half the synchronous speed. Only squirrel cage motors support this method.

Method 4 — Stator Resistance Speed Control

Same principle as stator resistance starting — resistors inserted in series to reduce voltage. However, as a speed control method it is disadvantageous: motor draws large current for same torque at lower speed → poor efficiency. Preferred only for small motors and for short duration control because of its low cost.

2.8

Speed Control — Rotor Side Methods

Method 1 — Rotor Resistance Control

External resistance is inserted in the rotor circuit of a slip-ring motor. Torque equation: \(T \propto \dfrac{s}{R_2}\). For constant torque operation, if R₂ increases, s must increase proportionally — so speed decreases. The speed can be varied smoothly.

Fig 2.8 — Rotor resistance control: increasing R₂ shifts sm to higher slip values. For constant load torque, motor operates at higher slip → lower speed. Max torque remains constant (only sm changes).
🔴
Why Rotor Resistance Control is Inefficient

All slip power = \(s \times P_{air-gap}\) is dissipated as heat in the external rotor resistance. At low speed (high s), this loss is enormous. Therefore rotor resistance control is not suitable for wide-range or continuous speed control. It is used only for starting or brief speed adjustment in traction applications.

Method 2 — Slip Power Recovery (Kramer / Scherbius)

Instead of wasting slip power as heat, it is recovered and fed back to the supply. External voltage at slip frequency is injected into the rotor circuit through slip rings. Depending on the phase of injected voltage:

  • Add to rotor EMF (in phase with sE₂): increases rotor current → torque ↑ → slip ↓ → speed ↑ (supersynchronous)
  • Oppose rotor EMF (antiphase): reduces rotor current → torque ↓ → motor reacts by increasing slip → speed ↓ (subsynchronous)

This method is expensive and does not give a wide speed range. Modern implementation uses static Kramer scheme (diode rectifier + inverter) — commonly used in large pump and fan drives.

Method 3 — Cascading / Tandem Connection

Two slip-ring induction motors are mechanically coupled (same shaft) and electrically connected — the rotor output of Motor 1 (at slip frequency) feeds the stator of Motor 2. This gives multiple discrete speed options.

Cascading — Speed EquationsSynchronous speeds: Motor 1: N₁ = (120f/P₁)(1−s₁) Motor 2: N₂ = (120×s₁f/P₂)(1−s₂) Since both shafts are coupled: N₁ = N₂ = N Setting s₁s₂ → 0: N = 120f / (P₁ ± P₂) + sign: Cumulative cascading (same phase sequence for M2) − sign: Differential cascading (reversed phase sequence for M2) Four speed options with cascading: N₃ = 120f/P₁ (M1 alone) N₄ = 120f/P₂ (M2 alone) N₁ = 120f/(P₁+P₂) (cumulative cascade) N₂ = 120f/(P₁−P₂) (differential cascade)
🔑
GATE Concept: Cascading Requirements

Both motors must be slip-ring type. The phase sequence of rotor output of Motor 1 determines whether the cascade is cumulative (+) or differential (−). Differential cascading requires the poles of the two motors to be different (especially for differential). This drive provides 4 speed steps including independent operation of each motor.

2.9

Braking of Induction Motors

Braking is done intentionally to stop the motor rapidly at a required instant. It is essential in manufacturing processes and drive systems. Braking in induction motors is analogous to braking in DC motors — three fundamental methods exist.

Method 1 — Plugging (Counter-Current Braking)

The phase sequence of the supply is reversed (by interchanging any two phase terminals across the stator) while the motor is running. The magnetic field immediately reverses direction, but the rotor continues rotating in the original direction due to inertia.

This means the relative speed between field and rotor = N_s + N (very large!), so a very large braking torque is developed. The motor tries to rotate in the reverse direction — it must be disconnected from supply before reaching zero speed using a speed-sensing relay (zero-speed switch), otherwise it will reverse.

Fig 2.9 — Plugging: reversed phase sequence creates s ≈ 2 (sum of normal slip + rotor slip). Extremely effective braking. High rotor temperature — motor must be designed for this condition.

Method 2 — Dynamic (Rheostatic) Braking

The 3-phase supply is disconnected and a DC voltage is applied across any two stator phases. This creates a stationary (non-rotating) magnetic flux in the air gap. The rotating rotor cuts this stationary flux, generating currents in the rotor and producing a braking torque.

Fig 2.10 — Dynamic braking: 3φ AC disconnected; DC applied to 2 stator phases (star or delta connection). Creates stationary flux. Rotating rotor generates braking EMF, current, and torque. No danger of speed reversal.
💡
Advantage Over Plugging

Dynamic braking has no danger of speed reversal — unlike plugging, the motor naturally comes to rest without reversing. However, induced rotor currents must be controlled (from stator or rotor side if slip-ring) to avoid overheating. This method produces a constant stationary time-varying flux — it is similar to regenerative braking but the energy is dissipated rather than recovered.

Method 3 — Regenerative Braking

Regenerative braking is not intentional braking — it occurs naturally when the motor speed exceeds synchronous speed (N > N_s). In this condition, the slip becomes negative (s < 0), and the motor acts as an induction generator, feeding real power back into the supply lines.

Regenerative Braking — ConditionNormal motoring: N < Ns, s = (Ns − N)/Ns > 0 Regenerative: N > Ns, s = (Ns − N)/Ns < 0 When s < 0: E₂ reverses sign → I₂ reverses → T reverses (braking torque) Rotor component of stator current reverses → IM feeds active power to supply During regeneration: Magnetising current I₀ still drawn from supply (lagging) → VAR consumed Active power fed back → only magnetising current from supply
📌
Regenerative Braking — Practical Significance

This is not intentional braking and will not stop the motor. It occurs naturally with overhauling loads (descending load on a hoist) or during frequency reduction in VFD drives. It is particularly useful in mountain railways where slip-ring motors are preferred — they feed power back to the grid while descending, saving energy. During this braking, the motor still draws magnetising (reactive) current from the supply.

GATE & IES Exam Concepts — Worked Numericals

EXAMPLE 2.1 Autotransformer Starting — Find tapping ratio for given starting torque
A 3-phase squirrel cage IM draws 6 times full-load current at full voltage starting (DOL). Full-load slip = 0.05. An autotransformer starter is used. Find the tapping ratio x if the starting torque must be 1.5 times full-load torque.
Solution

Given: I_st (DOL) / I_f = 6, s_f = 0.05, T_st / T_f = 1.5

Using autotransformer torque formula: \[\frac{T_{st}}{T_f} = x^2 \left(\frac{I_{st\_DOL}}{I_f}\right)^2 s_f\]

Substituting: \[1.5 = x^2 \times (6)^2 \times 0.05 = x^2 \times 1.8\]

\[x^2 = \frac{1.5}{1.8} = 0.833 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = 0.913\]

Answer: Tapping ratio x = 0.913 (approximately 91.3% tap). Note: standard tappings are 60%, 70%, 80%, 90% — use 90% tap.

EXAMPLE 2.2 Star-Delta Starting — Torque ratio with given full-load parameters
For a 3-phase IM, I_st(DOL)/I_f = 7 and s_f = 0.04. Find T_st/T_f for (a) DOL starting, (b) Star-delta starting.
Solution

(a) DOL starting: \[\frac{T_{st}}{T_f} = \left(\frac{I_{st}}{I_f}\right)^2 \times s_f = (7)^2 \times 0.04 = 49 \times 0.04 = \mathbf{1.96}\]

(b) Star-delta starting: Starting torque is 1/3 of DOL starting torque: \[\frac{T_{st}}{T_f} = \frac{1}{3} \times 1.96 = \mathbf{0.653}\]

Also using formula directly: \[\frac{T_{st}(Y)}{T_f} = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{I_{st\_DOL}}{I_f}\right)^2 \times s_f = \frac{1}{3} \times 49 \times 0.04 = 0.653\]

EXAMPLE 2.3 Cascading Connection — Find operating speeds
A 50 Hz supply feeds a cascaded set of two slip-ring induction motors. Motor 1 has P₁ = 4 poles, Motor 2 has P₂ = 6 poles. Both are mechanically coupled. Find all four possible operating speeds.
Solution

\[N_s = \frac{120f}{P}\]

M1 alone: \(N_{s1} = \dfrac{120 \times 50}{4} = \mathbf{1500 \text{ rpm}}\)

M2 alone: \(N_{s2} = \dfrac{120 \times 50}{6} = \mathbf{1000 \text{ rpm}}\)

Cumulative cascade (P₁ + P₂): \(N = \dfrac{120 \times 50}{4+6} = \dfrac{6000}{10} = \mathbf{600 \text{ rpm}}\)

Differential cascade (P₁ − P₂): \(N = \dfrac{120 \times 50}{|4-6|} = \dfrac{6000}{2} = \mathbf{3000 \text{ rpm}}\) (Note: 3000 rpm is supersynchronous — valid only if phase sequence of M2's rotor output is reversed relative to M1)

EXAMPLE 2.4 Slip Power Recovery — Frequency of injected voltage
A 50 Hz 4-pole induction motor runs at 1200 rpm. What should be the frequency of voltage injected into the rotor for obtaining steady torque?
Solution

Synchronous speed: \(N_s = \dfrac{120 \times 50}{4} = 1500\text{ rpm}\)

Slip: \(s = \dfrac{N_s - N}{N_s} = \dfrac{1500 - 1200}{1500} = \dfrac{300}{1500} = 0.2\)

Rotor frequency = slip frequency: \(f_{rotor} = s \times f = 0.2 \times 50 = \mathbf{10\text{ Hz}}\)

The injected voltage must be at rotor frequency (10 Hz) — i.e., at slip frequency — to maintain steady torque. This is the principle of slip power recovery (Kramer scheme).

GATE EE Starting torque of an IM with reduced voltage starting 2019

In a 3-phase squirrel cage induction motor, when the supply voltage is reduced to half, the starting torque becomes:

  • A) Half of original starting torque
  • B) One-fourth of original starting torque
  • C) Twice the original starting torque
  • D) Remains unchanged

Explanation: Starting torque \(T_{st} \propto V^2\). If V → V/2, then \(T_{st} \propto (V/2)^2 = V^2/4\). So T_st becomes 1/4 of original. This is why reduced voltage starting significantly reduces starting torque — the main trade-off.

GATE EE Star-Delta vs DOL — comparison of starting current 2017

A 3-phase IM has a full-load current of 100 A. In star-delta starting, the line current drawn during starting as a fraction of DOL starting current is:

  • A) 1/√3
  • B) 1/2
  • C) 1/3
  • D) 1/√6

Explanation: In star connection, phase voltage = V/√3. Phase current = (V/√3)/Z_ph = I_DOL_phase/√3. In star, I_line = I_phase. In delta (DOL), I_line = √3 × I_phase. So I_line(Y)/I_line(DOL) = (I_DOL_phase/√3)/(√3 × I_DOL_phase) = 1/3.

IES Rotor resistance starting — which motor type? 2018

Rotor resistance starting is applicable to which type of induction motor?

  • A) Squirrel cage induction motor only
  • B) Single-phase induction motor only
  • C) Slip-ring (wound rotor) induction motor only
  • D) Both squirrel cage and slip-ring motors

Explanation: Rotor resistance starting requires access to rotor circuit through slip rings and brushes. Squirrel cage motors have short-circuited rotor bars — no external connection possible. Only slip-ring (wound rotor) motors allow external resistance to be inserted in the rotor circuit.

GATE EE Regenerative braking condition 2020

An induction motor operates in regenerative braking mode when:

  • A) Rotor speed is zero and stator is energised
  • B) Motor is connected in star configuration
  • C) Rotor speed exceeds synchronous speed (N > Ns), giving negative slip
  • D) DC voltage is applied to stator windings

Explanation: Regenerative braking occurs when s < 0, i.e., N > Ns. The slip becomes negative, torque reverses (becomes braking torque), and the machine acts as an induction generator feeding real power back to the supply. Option C is correct.

Chapter Summary

DOL Starting

Full voltage. I_st = 5–8× I_FL. Suitable only for small motors (<5 kW). Two protections: overload (bimetallic) + undervoltage. Simplest, cheapest.

Star-Delta

TPDT switch only. V_phase(Y) = V/√3. Tst = 1/3 of DOL. I_line = 1/3 of DOL. Switchover transient at ~80% Ns. Best for 5–30 kW range.

Autotransformer

Tapping x < 1. I_supply = x²·I_DOL. Tst/Tf = x²(Ist/If)²sF. Best reduced-voltage method (supply current = x²). Expensive. Best for large cage motors.

Rotor Resistance

Slip-ring motors only. Tst maximised when R₂=X₂. Technically the best method. Used in traction. Speed also controllable but inefficient for wide range.

V/f Speed Control

Bm ∝ V/f = const. Tmax constant in range. Requires power electronic inverter (VFD). Sm changes but Tmax preserved. Most popular modern method.

Braking

Plugging: s≈2, fastest but energy wasted, reversal risk. Dynamic: DC to stator, no reversal. Regenerative: N>Ns (s<0), energy returned, mountain railways.
