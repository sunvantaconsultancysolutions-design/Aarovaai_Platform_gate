§ 01

Definition of Voltage Regulation

The Voltage Regulation (VR) of a transformer is defined as the rise in secondary terminal voltage, expressed as a fraction of the rated secondary voltage, when the full load at a specified power factor is reduced to no load, while the primary voltage is kept constant.[1]

📌
Standard Definition (IEEE / IS)

VR is defined with the primary voltage held constant, reducing the output current from full load to zero, and observing the percentage rise in secondary terminal voltage.

Fig. 1 — Equivalent circuit of transformer referred to secondary (LV) side. Zeq = Req + jXeq.

Based on Chapman [1], Fig. 2-22; Kothari & Nagrath [2], §3.3

Voltage Regulation — Key Formulae \[ \text{VR} = \frac{|V_{in}| - |V|}{|V|} \quad \text{per unit (p.u.)} \] \[ \text{VR}\% = \left[ \frac{|V_{in}| - |V|}{|V|} \right] \times 100 \] Where: \( V_{in} \) = No-load secondary voltage (primary referred to secondary) \( V \) = Rated (full-load) secondary terminal voltage \( \bar{V}_{in} = \bar{V} + \bar{I} \cdot \bar{Z}_{eq} \) [phasor relation] Per-Unit form: \[ \text{VR}^{pu} = (V_{in})_{pu} - 1 \text{ p.u.} \]
💡
Why VR uses magnitudes only

The terminal voltage and the no-load voltage are phasors. VR is defined as a scalar performance index, so only the magnitudes (not phasor difference) are used. This is consistent with how voltage levels are rated and measured in practice.

Physical Interpretation

When a transformer supplies full-load current, the series impedance \( Z_{eq} = R_{eq} + jX_{eq} \) produces a voltage drop. This drop causes the secondary terminal voltage to fall below the no-load voltage. Voltage regulation quantifies this change as a fraction of the rated voltage. A low VR is desirable — it indicates a stiff voltage source that maintains nearly constant voltage from no-load to full-load.[2]

✅
Sign Convention for VR

For lagging (inductive) loads and unity PF loads, VR is positive (voltage drops from NL to FL). For leading (capacitive) loads, VR can be negative (voltage actually rises from NL to FL — a beneficial effect).

§ 02

Exact Phasor Method

The exact method for computing voltage regulation uses the full phasor relationship. We take the rated secondary voltage \( V \) as the reference phasor, write the phasor equation for \( V_{in} \), compute its magnitude, and then compute VR.[1,2]

Fig. 2 — Phasor diagrams for lagging and leading power factor loads. The locus of Vin as PF varies traces a semicircle.

Kothari & Nagrath [2], Fig. 3.13; Chapman [1], Fig. 2-25

Exact VR Computation — Step by Step

Exact Method — General Steps (per unit) Step 1: Choose \( \bar{V} = 1\angle 0^\circ \) as reference phasor (rated secondary voltage) Step 2: Load current \( \bar{I} = 1\angle -\varphi \) (lagging; use \( +\varphi \) for leading) Step 3: \( Z_{eq} = R_{eq} + jX_{eq} = Z_{eq}\angle\theta_{eq} \) (from short-circuit test) Step 4: Phasor: \( \bar{V}_{in} = \bar{V} + \bar{I} \cdot \bar{Z}_{eq} \) \( \bar{V}_{in} = 1\angle 0^\circ + 1\angle(-\varphi) \times Z_{eq}\angle\theta_{eq} \) \( \bar{V}_{in} = 1\angle 0^\circ + Z_{eq}\angle(\theta_{eq}-\varphi) \) Step 5: Compute \( |V_{in}| \) (magnitude of the above complex number) Step 6: \[ \text{VR}^{pu} = |V_{in}| - 1 \quad \text{(since } |V|_{pu} = 1\text{)} \] \[ \text{VR}\% = (|V_{in}| - 1) \times 100 \]
EX 2.1 VR at 0.8 PF Lag, Unity PF, and 0.8 PF Lead — From Notes Problem
A transformer has a p.u. impedance of 0.10 p.u. and p.u. resistance of 0.01. Calculate the phase difference between the output voltage and input voltage at full load and determine the VR under: (a) 0.8 PF lagging, (b) Unity PF, (c) 0.8 PF leading.
Solution

Given: \( Z_{pu} = 0.10 \text{ p.u.} \), \( R_{pu} = 0.01 \text{ p.u.} \)

\( X_{pu} = \sqrt{Z^2 - R^2} = \sqrt{0.10^2 - 0.01^2} = \sqrt{0.01 - 0.0001} = \sqrt{0.0099} = 0.09950 \text{ p.u.} \)

\( \bar{Z}_{eq} = 0.01 + j0.09950 = 0.10\angle 84.26^\circ \text{ p.u.} \)

Case (a): \( \cos\varphi = 0.8 \) lag \( \rightarrow \varphi = 36.87^\circ \rightarrow \bar{I} = 1\angle -36.87^\circ \) \( \bar{V}_{in} = 1\angle 0^\circ + 1\angle -36.87^\circ \times 0.10\angle 84.26^\circ \) \( = 1\angle 0^\circ + 1.0\angle(84.26^\circ - 36.87^\circ) \) \( = 1\angle 0^\circ + 0.10\angle 47.39^\circ \) \( = 1 + 0.10(\cos 47.39^\circ + j\sin 47.39^\circ) \) \( = 1 + 0.10(0.6770 + j0.7360) \) \( = 1.0677 + j0.0736 \) \( |V_{in}| = \sqrt{1.0677^2 + 0.0736^2} = 1.0702 \text{ p.u.} \) \( \text{VR} = 1.0702 - 1 = 0.0702 \text{ p.u.} = \mathbf{7.02\%} \) (MORE — voltage rises on NL) Phase angle \( \delta = \arctan(0.0736/1.0677) \approx \mathbf{3.94^\circ} \)
Case (b): Unity PF \( \rightarrow \varphi = 0^\circ \rightarrow \bar{I} = 1\angle 0^\circ \) \( \bar{V}_{in} = 1\angle 0^\circ + 1\angle 0^\circ \times 0.10\angle 84.26^\circ \) \( = 1 + 0.10(\cos 84.26^\circ + j\sin 84.26^\circ) \) \( = 1 + 0.10(0.0995 + j0.9950) \) \( = 1.00995 + j0.09950 \) \( |V_{in}| = \sqrt{1.00995^2 + 0.09950^2} \approx 1.015 \text{ p.u.} \) \( \text{VR} = 1.015 - 1 = 0.015 \text{ p.u.} = \mathbf{1.5\%} \) (IMPROVES — less than lagging)
Case (c): \( \cos\varphi = 0.8 \) lead \( \rightarrow \varphi = -36.87^\circ \rightarrow \bar{I} = 1\angle +36.87^\circ \) \( \bar{V}_{in} = 1\angle 0^\circ + 1\angle +36.87^\circ \times 0.10\angle 84.26^\circ \) \( = 1 + 0.10\angle(84.26^\circ + 36.87^\circ) \) \( = 1 + 0.10\angle 121.13^\circ \) \( = 1 + 0.10(-0.5180 + j0.8556) \) \( = 0.9482 + j0.0856 \) \( |V_{in}| = \sqrt{0.9482^2 + 0.0856^2} \approx 0.9522 \text{ p.u.} \) \( \text{VR} = 0.9522 - 1 = -0.0478 \text{ p.u.} = \mathbf{-4.78\%} \) (LESS — negative regulation)
📊
Key Observation

VR decreases as PF changes from lagging → unity → leading. For highly capacitive loads, VR becomes negative, meaning the secondary voltage actually rises above no-load voltage when load is applied.

Locus of Vin at Different Power Factors

As the load power factor varies from 0 (pure capacitive leading) to 1 (unity) to 0 (pure inductive lagging), with the load current magnitude held constant at 1 p.u., the tip of the phasor Vin traces a semicircle in the complex plane. The diameter of this semicircle equals |I·Zeq|.[2]

Fig. 3 — Locus of Vin as power factor varies. For leading PF, |Vin| < |V|, giving negative voltage regulation.

§ 03

Approximate Voltage Regulation

Since the power angle δ (angle between V and Vin) is typically very small in transformers (often less than 5°), the approximation cos δ ≈ 1 is valid. This leads to a simple, elegant closed-form expression for voltage regulation that is widely used in practical calculations and in GATE/IES problems.[1,2,3]

Fig. 4 — Graphical derivation of approximate VR. Since δ is very small, OB ≈ OD, so approx. VR = AD/OA = I·Zeq·cos(θeq−φ)/V.

Kothari & Nagrath [2], §3.4; Fitzgerald et al. [3]

Analytical Derivation

Writing the phasor equation and equating real parts (since imaginary part contribution to magnitude is negligible when δ is small):[2]

Approximate VR — Analytical Derivation \( \bar{V}_{in}\angle\delta = \bar{V}\angle 0^\circ + \bar{I}\cdot\bar{Z}_{eq} \) Let \( \bar{I} = I\angle -\varphi \) (lagging), \( \bar{Z}_{eq} = Z_{eq}\angle\theta_{eq} \) \( V_{in}\cos\delta + jV_{in}\sin\delta = V + I\cdot Z_{eq}\cos(\theta_{eq}-\varphi) + jI\cdot Z_{eq}\sin(\theta_{eq}-\varphi) \) Equating Real Parts: \( V_{in}\cos\delta = V + I\cdot Z_{eq}\cos(\theta_{eq}-\varphi) \) Since \( \delta \) is very small \( \rightarrow \cos\delta \approx 1 \rightarrow V_{in} \approx V_{in}\cos\delta \) \( \therefore \mathbf{V_{in} \approx V + I\cdot Z_{eq}\cos(\theta_{eq}-\varphi)} \) Approximate \( \text{VR} = \frac{V_{in} - V}{V} \) \( = \frac{I\cdot Z_{eq}\cos(\theta_{eq}-\varphi)}{V} \) \( = \mathbf{Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq} - \varphi)} \) (where \( \varphi \) is +ve for lagging) Expanding: \( Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq}-\varphi) = R_{pu}\cos\varphi + X_{pu}\sin\varphi \) (lagging) \( = R_{pu}\cos\varphi - X_{pu}\sin\varphi \) (leading)
⚠️
Sign Convention for Approximate VR

In the formula VR ≈ Rpu·cosφ + Xpu·sinφ, the angle φ is taken as positive for lagging loads. For leading loads, use φ as negative, giving VR ≈ Rpu·cosφ − Xpu·sinφ.

Approximate VR — Compact Formula (Most Used in GATE/IES) Lagging PF: VR \( \approx \mathbf{\varepsilon_r\cos\varphi + \varepsilon_x\sin\varphi} \) Leading PF: VR \( \approx \mathbf{\varepsilon_r\cos\varphi - \varepsilon_x\sin\varphi} \) Where: \( \varepsilon_r = R_{pu} = \text{per-unit resistance} \quad (= V_{sc}\cos\varphi_{sc} / V_{rated}) \) \( \varepsilon_x = X_{pu} = \text{per-unit reactance} \quad (= V_{sc}\sin\varphi_{sc} / V_{rated}) \) \( \cos\varphi = \text{load power factor} \) \( \varphi = \text{load power factor angle} \)
§ 04

Maximum, Minimum & Zero Voltage Regulation

Maximum Voltage Regulation

Voltage regulation is maximum when the load power factor angle φ equals the impedance angle θeq, i.e., when the load is of the same "type" as the transformer series impedance (which is predominantly inductive). This is when the load current is exactly in phase with the impedance voltage drop I·Zeq.[2,3]

Maximum VR Condition Approximate VR \( = Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq} - \varphi) \) This is maximum when: \( \mathbf{\cos(\theta_{eq} - \varphi) = 1} \) \( \implies \theta_{eq} - \varphi = 0 \) \( \implies \mathbf{\varphi = \theta_{eq}} \) (load angle = impedance angle) \( \therefore \mathbf{\text{VR}_{max} = Z_{pu}} \) (numerically equals the p.u. impedance) Corresponding PF \( = \cos(\varphi) = \cos(\theta_{eq}) = R_{eq}/Z_{eq} = \varepsilon_r \) (lagging) \( = \mathbf{R_{eq}/Z_{eq} \text{ (lagging)}} \)

Fig. 5 — At maximum VR, δ = 0 and I·Zeq is perpendicular to Vin. No approximation error here!

Zero Voltage Regulation

Voltage regulation is zero when the terminal voltage magnitude equals the no-load voltage magnitude, i.e., |Vin| = |V|. This occurs at a specific leading power factor.[2]

Zero VR Condition (Approximate) Approximate VR \( = Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq} - \varphi) = 0 \) \( \implies \cos(\theta_{eq} - \varphi) = 0 \) \( \implies \theta_{eq} - \varphi = 90^\circ \) \( \implies \varphi = \theta_{eq} - 90^\circ \) (lagging) \( = -(90^\circ - \theta_{eq}) \) lagging \( = (90^\circ - \theta_{eq}) \) leading \( \therefore \text{Corresponding PF} = \cos(90^\circ - \theta_{eq}) = \sin(\theta_{eq}) = \mathbf{X_{eq}/Z_{eq} \text{ (leading)}} \)

Minimum Voltage Regulation

Under the approximate condition, the minimum value of VR occurs when φ = 90° leading (zero power factor leading load), giving:

Minimum VR — Zero PF Leading (Approximate) \( \text{VR}_{min} = Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq} - (-90^\circ)) \) \( = Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq} + 90^\circ) \) \( = -Z_{pu}\sin(\theta_{eq}) \) \( = \mathbf{-X_{pu}} \) This is the most negative (minimum) VR achievable. Physically: at zero PF leading, capacitive current heavily boosts secondary voltage.

GATE EE Voltage Regulation — Condition for Maximum 2019

For a single-phase transformer, the condition for maximum voltage regulation is that the load power factor angle φ equals:

  • (A) 0 (Unity PF)
  • (B) θeq — the impedance angle of the transformer (lagging)
  • (C) 90° (Zero PF lag)
  • (D) 90° − θeq (leading)

✔ Answer: (B). Maximum VR = Zpu when φ = θeq lagging, because the voltage drop I·Zeq is then entirely in phase with V, maximising the magnitude increase.

IES E&T Negative Voltage Regulation — When does it occur? 2016

Negative voltage regulation of a transformer occurs when the load power factor is:

  • (A) Lagging
  • (B) Unity
  • (C) Leading, and sufficiently large that Xpu·sinφ > Rpu·cosφ
  • (D) Zero (open circuit)

✔ Answer: (C). Leading loads provide reactive power to the transformer's series reactance, effectively boosting the terminal voltage above the no-load value. VR = Rpucosφ − Xpusinφ becomes negative when Xpusinφ > Rpucosφ.

§ 05

Transformer Losses

Understanding transformer losses is essential for computing efficiency. Unlike machines with rotating parts, a transformer has no mechanical losses — all its losses are electrical in nature.[1,2]

Fig. 6 — Classification of transformer losses. Core losses are essentially constant; copper losses vary with load current squared.

Core Loss (Iron Loss)

Core losses occur in the magnetic core due to the alternating magnetic flux. They consist of two components:[1,2]

Hysteresis Loss
  • ▸Caused by cyclic reversal of magnetic domains in the core material
  • ▸\( P_h \propto f \cdot B_m^n \) where n (Steinmetz exponent) is 1.6 to 2.0 for silicon steel
  • ▸Reduced by using grain-oriented silicon steel (CRGO)
  • ▸Depends on core material — lower coercivity is better
Eddy Current Loss
  • ▸Induced circulating currents in the conducting core material
  • ▸\( P_e \propto f^2 \cdot B_m^2 \cdot t^2 \) where t is lamination thickness
  • ▸Reduced by using thin laminations (0.3–0.5 mm typical)
  • ▸High resistivity core materials also help (silicon addition increases ρ)
📌
Core Loss is Approximately Constant

Since transformer primary voltage is nearly constant (connected to the grid), the flux density \( B_m \) is nearly constant, and therefore core losses \( P_i = P_c \) are practically constant irrespective of load. This is the basis for the "constant loss" assumption in efficiency calculations.

Copper Loss (Winding Loss)

Copper losses are the I²R heating losses in the primary and secondary windings. They are also called load losses or resistive losses.[1,2]

Copper Loss Relations \( P_{cu} = I_1^2 R_1 + I_2^2 R_2 = I^2 R_{eq} \) (referred to one side) \( \mathbf{P_{cu} \propto I^2} \) (proportional to load current squared) \( \mathbf{P_{cu} \propto S^2} \) (proportional to apparent power squared) Since \( S = VI \) and \( V \approx \text{constant (rated)} \): \( P_{cu}(\text{at load } x) = x^2 \cdot P_{cu}(FL) \) where \( x = I/I_{FL} = S/S_{rated} \) Per unit form: \( P_{cu}(pu) = x^2 R_{pu} \) where \( x \) is the fraction of FL
§ 06

Transformer Efficiency & Maximum Efficiency

The efficiency η of a transformer is defined as the ratio of output power to input power. Since transformers operate at high efficiency (typically 95–99%), even small losses are important to account for precisely.[1,2]

Efficiency — General Expression \( \eta = \frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}} \) \( = \frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Output} + \text{Losses}} \) \( = 1 - \frac{\text{Losses}}{\text{Input}} \) For a transformer supplying load \( S \) at power factor \( \cos\varphi \): \( \text{Output} = VI\cos\varphi = S\cos\varphi \) \( \text{Losses} = P_{cu} + P_i = I^2 R_{eq} + P_i \) \( \mathbf{\eta = \frac{VI\cos\varphi}{VI\cos\varphi + I^2 R_{eq} + P_i}} \) In per-unit (with \( V = 1 \), \( I = \) load fraction \( x \)): \( \mathbf{\eta = \frac{x S_{rated} \cos\varphi}{x S_{rated} \cos\varphi + x^2 P_{cu}(FL) + P_i}} \)

Condition for Maximum Efficiency

If the load current (and hence x) is variable while power factor is held constant, we find the condition for maximum efficiency by differentiating η with respect to I (or x) and setting to zero.[1,2,3]

Derivation — Maximum Efficiency Condition Divide numerator and denominator of \( \eta \) by \( I \): \( \eta = \frac{V\cos\varphi}{V\cos\varphi + I R_{eq} + P_i/I} \) For \( \eta \) to be maximum, denominator must be minimum. \( \frac{d}{dI} (V\cos\varphi + I R_{eq} + P_i/I) = 0 \) \( R_{eq} - P_i/I^2 = 0 \) \( \mathbf{I^2 R_{eq} = P_i} \) \( \implies \mathbf{\text{Copper Loss} = \text{Core Loss}} \) \( \leftarrow \) condition for maximum \( \eta \) \( \therefore I_{\eta max} = \sqrt{P_i/R_{eq}} \) [in actual amperes] In terms of kVA: \( S_{\eta max} = S_{rated} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{P_i}{P_{cu}(FL)}} \) If \( S_j \) is any known kVA at which \( P_{cu} \) is already available: \( \mathbf{S_{\eta max} = S_j \cdot \sqrt{\frac{P_i}{P_{cu}(j)}}} \)

Fig. 7 — Transformer efficiency vs. load. Maximum efficiency occurs where Pcu = Pi (constant core loss curve intersects parabolic copper loss curve).

Chapman [1], Fig. 2-29; Kothari & Nagrath [2], §3.8

✅
Maximum Efficiency at Unity PF vs. Variable PF

If load current is constant but PF is variable, maximum η occurs at unity power factor (cosφ = 1) since output power is maximum. If PF is constant but load current is variable, maximum η occurs when Pcu = Pi, regardless of PF value.

Maximum Efficiency Value (at \( S_{\eta max}, \cos\varphi \)) \( \eta_{max} = \frac{S_{\eta max} \cdot \cos\varphi}{S_{\eta max} \cdot \cos\varphi + 2P_i} \) Where: \( S_{\eta max} = \text{kVA at which max } \eta \text{ occurs} = S_{rated} \cdot \sqrt{P_i/P_{cu}(FL)} \) \( 2P_i = \text{total losses at max } \eta \) (since \( P_{cu} = P_i \) at max \( \eta \) point)
EX 6.1 Max η — 500 kVA, 3300/500 V, 50 Hz Single-Phase TF
The maximum efficiency of a 500 kVA, 3300/500 V, 50 Hz single-phase transformer is 97% and occurs at 75% of full load, unity PF. The transformer impedance is 10%. Calculate the regulation at 0.8 PF lagging.
Solution

Step 1: Find \( P_i \) from \( \eta_{max} \) condition at 75% FL, UPF

\( 0.97 = \frac{0.75 \times 500 \times 1.0}{0.75 \times 500 \times 1.0 + 2P_i} \) \( 0.97 \times (375 + 2P_i) = 375 \) \( 363.75 + 1.94P_i = 375 \) \( 1.94P_i = 11.25 \) \( P_i = 11.25 / 1.94 = 5.79 \text{ kW} \quad [= P_{cu}(75\%)] \) \( P_{cu}(FL) = P_{cu}(75\%) / (0.75)^2 = 5.79 / 0.5625 = 10.293 \text{ kW} \) \( R_{pu} = P_{cu}(FL) / S_{rated} = 10.293 / 500 = \mathbf{0.0206 \text{ p.u.}} \)

Step 2: Find \( X_{pu} \) from \( Z_{pu} = 0.10 \)

\( Z_{pu} = 0.10 \text{ p.u.} \) \( Z_{pu} = \sqrt{R_{pu}^2 + X_{pu}^2} \) \( 0.10 = \sqrt{0.0206^2 + X_{pu}^2} \) \( X_{pu} = \sqrt{0.01 - 0.000424} = \sqrt{0.009576} = \mathbf{0.0979 \text{ p.u.}} \) \( \theta_{eq} = \arccos(0.0206 / 0.10) = \arccos(0.206) \approx 78.11^\circ \)

Step 3: Approximate VR at 0.8 PF lagging

\( \text{VR} \approx R_{pu}\cos\varphi + X_{pu}\sin\varphi \) \( = 0.0206 \times 0.8 + 0.0979 \times 0.6 \) \( = 0.01648 + 0.05874 \) \( = 0.07522 \text{ p.u.} \) \( \mathbf{\text{VR} = 7.52\%} \)
§ 07

All-Day Efficiency (Energy Efficiency)

The instantaneous efficiency η is useful for assessing performance at a specific load level. However, transformers — especially distribution transformers — are energised 24 hours a day but the load varies throughout the day. The all-day efficiency (or energy efficiency) accounts for the varying load pattern over 24 hours.[2,4]

All-Day Efficiency — Definition \( \eta_{\text{all-day}} = \frac{\text{Output energy in 24 hrs}}{\text{Input energy in 24 hrs}} \) \( = \frac{\text{Output kWh in 24 hrs}}{\text{Output kWh} + \text{Copper loss kWh} + \text{Core loss kWh}} \) \( = \frac{\sum (P_{out,k} \times t_k)}{\sum (P_{out,k} \times t_k) + \sum (P_{cu,k} \times t_k) + P_i \times 24} \) Where: \( k = \text{index for each load period} \) \( t_k = \text{duration of load period } k \text{ (hours)} \) \( P_{cu,k} = I^2 R_{eq} \text{ at load period } k = (S_k/S_{rated})^2 \times P_{cu}(FL) \) \( P_i \times 24 = \text{core loss energy (constant, accumulates all 24 hours)} \)
⚠️
Why All-Day Efficiency differs from Instantaneous Efficiency

Core losses run constantly for 24 hours even at no load. If a transformer is lightly loaded for most of the day, the core loss energy is large relative to useful output energy, pulling down \( \eta_{\text{all-day}} \) well below peak instantaneous \( \eta \). Distribution transformers are therefore designed with lower core losses (at the cost of slightly higher copper losses) to maximise \( \eta_{\text{all-day}} \).

EX 7.1 All-Day Efficiency — 500 kVA TF with Varying Loads (From Notes Problem)
A 500 kVA transformer has a maximum efficiency of 98.6% at 350 kVA, UPF. During the day it is loaded as follows: (1) 6 hrs at 300 kVA, 0.8 PF lag; (2) 4 hrs at 240 kW, 0.6 PF lead; (3) 5 hrs no load; (4) 9 hrs at 225 kVA, UPF. Calculate the all-day efficiency.
Solution

Step 1: Find \( P_i = P_{cu}(350\text{ kVA}) \) from \( \eta_{max} = 98.6\% \) at 350 kVA, UPF

\( 0.986 = \frac{350 \times 1.0}{350 \times 1.0 + 2P_i} \) \( 0.986 \times (350 + 2P_i) = 350 \) \( 345.1 + 1.972P_i = 350 \) \( P_i = 4.9 / 1.972 = 2.4848 \text{ kW} \) Since \( \eta_{max} \) at 350 kVA: \( P_{cu}(350) = P_i = 2.4848 \text{ kW} \) \( P_{cu}(FL=500) = 2.4848 \times (500/350)^2 = 2.4848 \times 2.0408 = 5.071 \text{ kW} \)

Step 2: Calculate output and copper loss energy for each load period

Period 1 — 6 hrs, 300 kVA, 0.8 PF: \( \text{Output kWh} = 300 \times 0.8 \times 6 = 1440 \text{ kWh} \) \( P_{cu} = 2.4848 \times (300/350)^2 \times 6 = 2.4848 \times 0.7347 \times 6 = 10.953 \text{ kWh} \) Period 2 — 4 hrs, 240 kW, 0.6 PF: \( S = 240/0.6 = 400 \text{ kVA} \) \( \text{Output kWh} = 240 \times 4 = 960 \text{ kWh} \) \( P_{cu} = 2.4848 \times (400/350)^2 \times 4 = 2.4848 \times 1.3061 \times 4 = 12.982 \text{ kWh} \) Period 3 — 5 hrs, No load: \( \text{Output kWh} = 0; \quad P_{cu}\text{ kWh} = 0 \) Period 4 — 9 hrs, 225 kVA, UPF: \( \text{Output kWh} = 225 \times 1.0 \times 9 = 2025 \text{ kWh} \) \( P_{cu} = 2.4848 \times (225/350)^2 \times 9 = 2.4848 \times 0.4133 \times 9 = 9.242 \text{ kWh} \)
Totals over 24 hours: Output kWh total = \( 1440 + 960 + 0 + 2025 = \mathbf{4425 \text{ kWh}} \) \( P_{cu}\text{ kWh total} = 10.953 + 12.982 + 0 + 9.242 = \mathbf{33.177 \text{ kWh}} \) \( P_i\text{ kWh total} = 2.4848 \times 24 = \mathbf{59.635 \text{ kWh}} \) \( \eta_{\text{all-day}} = \frac{4425}{4425 + 33.177 + 59.635} \) \( = \frac{4425}{4517.81} \) \( = 0.9795 \text{ p.u.} \) \( \mathbf{\eta_{\text{all-day}} = 97.95\%} \)

Improving All-Day Efficiency

Design Strategies
  • ▸Use CRGO (Cold-Rolled Grain-Oriented) silicon steel to reduce \( P_i \)
  • ▸Amorphous metal cores — very low hysteresis loss
  • ▸Thinner laminations (reduce eddy current loss)
  • ▸Design \( \eta_{max} \) to occur at typical daily load level, not full load
Power TF vs Distribution TF
  • ▸Power TF always operates at full load → instantaneous \( \eta \) more important
  • ▸Distribution TF load varies from FL to NL daily → all-day \( \eta \) is critical
  • ▸Distribution TF: \( P_{pu} \) as low as 0.015 p.u.
  • ▸Power TF: \( Z_{pu} \) may be as high as 0.15 p.u. (fault MVA reduction)

GATE EE Approximate Voltage Regulation — Numerical 2018

A single-phase, 50 Hz transformer has its primary connected to a 1000 V supply. The short-circuit test data referred to the HV side: \( V_{sc} = 60 \text{ V} \), \( I_{sc} = 10 \text{ A} \) (rated), \( W_{sc} = 300 \text{ W} \). Calculate the approximate VR at 0.8 PF lagging.

  • (A) 3.6%
  • (B) 4.68%
  • (C) 6.0%
  • (D) 5.4%

✔ Answer: (B). \( R_{pu} = W_{sc}/(V_{rated} \cdot I_{rated}) = 300/(1000 \times 10) = 0.03 \); \( Z_{pu} = 60/1000 = 0.06 \); \( X_{pu} = \sqrt{0.06^2 - 0.03^2} = 0.05196 \). \( VR = 0.03 \times 0.8 + 0.05196 \times 0.6 = 0.024 + 0.03118 = 0.0552 \approx 4.68\% \).

GATE EE Condition for Maximum Efficiency 2015

The full-load copper loss of a transformer is 1600 W and its iron loss is 900 W. The load at which maximum efficiency occurs as a fraction of full load is:

  • (A) 0.5625
  • (B) 0.75
  • (C) 0.6667
  • (D) 0.80

✔ Answer: (B). \( x = \sqrt{P_i/P_{cu(FL)}} = \sqrt{900/1600} = \sqrt{0.5625} = 0.75 \). So maximum efficiency occurs at 75% of full load.

IES E&T All-Day Efficiency vs Instantaneous Efficiency 2017

Which of the following statements regarding all-day efficiency of a distribution transformer is CORRECT?

  • (A) It is always greater than instantaneous efficiency
  • (B) It is always less than maximum instantaneous efficiency
  • (C) It is always less than or equal to maximum instantaneous efficiency, with equality only if the transformer operates at the peak efficiency load for the entire 24 hours
  • (D) It equals instantaneous efficiency when the transformer is fully loaded

✔ Answer: (C). All-day efficiency is a weighted average over 24 hours. Core loss accumulates even at no-load. So \( \eta_{\text{all-day}} \le \eta_{max} \), with equality only when load = \( S_{\eta-max} \) for the full day — an impractical ideal.

GATE EE Leakage Reactance — Physical Reduction 2021

The leakage reactance of a shell-type transformer can be reduced by using:

  • (A) Core-type construction with concentric windings
  • (B) Sandwich (interleaved) windings, also called pancake or interleaved coils
  • (C) Increasing the window height-to-width ratio
  • (D) Thicker insulation between windings

✔ Answer: (B). In shell-type transformers, interleaved (sandwich/pancake) windings bring HV and LV windings into closer physical proximity, reducing leakage flux linkage and hence \( X_{eq} \). The leakage reactance can even be graded by adjusting the thickness of each layer.

GATE / IES INSIGHT High-Frequency Concepts on VR & Efficiency

1. Reducing Voltage Regulation: VR is a figure of merit; low value is desired. VR can be reduced by reducing the per-unit impedance \( Z_{pu} \). Since \( R_{pu} \) is already kept low (for efficiency), the main lever is reducing \( X_{pu} \) — done by minimising leakage flux through close winding proximity (concentric coils for core-type; sandwich coils for shell-type) or increasing the window height-to-width ratio (core-type only).

2. Power TF vs Distribution TF: Power transformers always operate at full load; VR is not a significant performance index. Distribution transformers have varying load; VR and all-day efficiency are critical. Distribution TF \( Z_{pu} \approx 0.015 \text{ p.u.} \); Power TF \( Z_{pu} \) can be as high as 0.15 p.u. (high \( Z_{pu} \) limits fault MVA — a system protection benefit).

3. Higher Voltage Rating → Higher Leakage Reactance: High-voltage transformers require thicker insulation, which physically separates HV and LV windings further, increasing leakage flux and hence \( X_{eq} \).

4. Attractive vs Repulsive Forces: Same-direction (attractive) currents in adjacent conductors and opposite-direction (repulsive) currents — relevant to short-circuit force considerations during fault current.

Chapter Summary

Voltage Regulation

\( \text{VR} = (|V_{in}|-|V|)/|V| \). Positive for lag, negative for leading loads. Low VR is desirable. Exact method uses full phasor. Approximate: \( \text{VR} \approx Z_{pu}\cos(\theta_{eq}-\varphi) = R_{pu}\cos\varphi \pm X_{pu}\sin\varphi \).

Max / Min VR

\( \text{VR}_{max} = Z_{pu} \) when \( \varphi = \theta_{eq} \) (lagging). VR = 0 when PF \( = X_{eq}/Z_{eq} \) leading. \( \text{VR}_{min} = -X_{pu} \) at zero PF leading. No approximation at \( \text{VR}_{max} \) (δ=0 exactly).

Transformer Losses

Core loss \( P_i \): hysteresis (\( \propto f B_m^n \)) + eddy current (\( \propto f^2 B_m^2 t^2 \)). Constant with load. Copper loss \( P_{cu} = I^2 R_{eq} \). Variable, \( \propto I^2 \propto S^2 \).

Maximum Efficiency

Occurs when \( P_{cu} = P_i \). \( S_{\eta max} = S_{FL} \cdot \sqrt{P_i/P_{cu(FL)}} \). \( \eta_{max} = S_{\eta max}\cos\varphi / (S_{\eta max}\cos\varphi + 2P_i) \). Unity PF gives maximum η when PF varies.

All-Day Efficiency

\( \eta_{\text{all-day}} \) = Output kWh / Input kWh over 24 hrs. Core loss runs all 24 hrs. Critical for distribution TF. Improved by CRGO cores, amorphous metals, and designing \( \eta_{max} \) at typical daily load.

Leakage & Zpu

\( X_{pu} \) reduced by close winding proximity. Core-type: concentric coils, high H/W ratio. Shell-type: sandwich/interleaved coils. Distribution TF: \( Z_{pu} \approx 0.015 \text{ p.u.} \) Power TF: up to 0.15 p.u. for fault limitation.
