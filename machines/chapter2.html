01

What is Armature Reaction?

When a DC machine operates under load, the armature conductors carry current and produce their own magnetic field — the armature MMF (magnetomotive force). This armature flux interacts with the main field flux produced by the field poles, distorting and weakening the resultant air-gap flux. This phenomenon is called armature reaction.[1]

🔑
Core Definition

Armature reaction = the effect of armature flux on the distribution of main field flux when the machine carries load. It causes (a) distortion of the flux density waveform, (b) shift of the Magnetic Neutral Axis (MNA), and (c) a slight net reduction in total flux due to magnetic saturation.

Geometric Neutral Axis (GNA) vs Magnetic Neutral Axis (MNA)

The Geometric Neutral Axis (GNA) is the axis perpendicular to the main field axis — it is fixed regardless of load. The Magnetic Neutral Axis (MNA) is the axis where the resultant air-gap flux density is zero; it is where the brushes must be placed for sparkless commutation. Under no-load, MNA ≡ GNA. Under load, the armature current shifts the MNA away from the GNA.

Fig. 1 — Left: No-load DC generator; GNA = MNA; brushes at correct position. Right: Loaded generator; armature current produces φa which distorts the resultant flux and shifts the MNA by angle α in the direction of rotation.

Nature of Armature MMF

The armature winding, when carrying current \(I_a\), produces a distributed MMF whose axis lies along the GNA (i.e., perpendicular to the field axis). This MMF is cross-magnetising — it neither adds nor subtracts from the field MMF directly, but it is at right angles and causes distortion. However, due to magnetic saturation, the leading pole tip (where armature flux aids field flux) saturates, while the trailing pole tip only partially aids — leading to a net demagnetisation effect.

⚠️
Saturation Effect — Net Demagnetisation

Even though armature reaction is theoretically purely cross-magnetising (no net change in flux), in practice magnetic saturation causes the increase in flux under the leading pole tip to be less than the decrease under the trailing pole tip. The net result is a slight reduction in total flux (ΔΦ), reducing both EMF and torque capability.

02

Armature Reaction in Generators vs Motors

The physics of armature reaction is identical in generators and motors — it is the armature current flowing in the armature winding that produces the disturbing MMF. However, since the direction of armature current reverses between generator and motor action (even for the same rotation), the direction of MNA shift is opposite.[2]

Fig. 2 — MNA shifts forward (in direction of rotation) in a generator, and backward (opposite to rotation) in a motor, for the same direction of mechanical rotation.

Key Memory Aid for GATE

Interpole polarity in a generator = same as the next main pole in the direction of rotation. Interpole polarity in a motor = same as the previous main pole (behind the direction of rotation). Equivalently: for motor, interpole polarity = same as main pole behind the direction of rotation.

03

Demagnetisation & Cross-Magnetisation AT/Pole

When brushes are shifted by an angle \(\theta\) from the GNA (to the actual MNA), the total armature ampere-turns per pole \(\left(\frac{I_a \cdot Z}{2 \cdot A \cdot 2P}\right) = \frac{I_a Z}{4AP}\) can be split into two components:[2]

Demagnetising AT/pole (ATd/p) — directly opposes the main field MMF. This is the component of armature MMF that lies along the field axis.

Cross-magnetising AT/pole (ATc/p) — acts perpendicular to the field, causing flux distortion without direct demagnetisation.

Armature Reaction Formulae — AT/Pole Total AT/pole: \(\text{AT/p} = \frac{I_a \times Z}{2 \times A \times 2P} = \frac{I_a Z}{4AP}\)

Brush shift (electrical): \(\theta_e = \frac{P}{2} \times \theta_m \quad \text{[\(\theta\) in degrees]}\)

Demagnetising AT/pole:
  \(\text{ATd/p} = \frac{2\theta}{180} \times \frac{I_a Z}{4AP} \quad \text{[\(\theta\) in elec. degrees]}\)

Cross-magnetising AT/pole:
  \(\text{ATc/p} = \left(1 - \frac{2\theta}{180}\right) \times \frac{I_a Z}{4AP}\)

Check: \(\text{ATd/p} + \text{ATc/p} = \text{AT/p}\) ✓

For brushes on GNA (\(\theta = 0\)): \(\text{ATd/p} = 0\), \(\text{ATc/p} = \text{AT/p}\) (purely cross-magnetising)

Where \(Z\) = total armature conductors, \(A\) = number of parallel paths, \(P\) = number of poles, \(I_a\) = armature current, \(\theta\) = brush shift angle in electrical degrees.

✅
Physical Interpretation

If brushes are on the GNA (θ = 0), there is no demagnetisation — armature reaction is purely cross-magnetising. Only when brushes are shifted does a demagnetising component arise. This is why brush position is critical in practice.

Conductors Under Commutating Zone (Zc)

The number of armature conductors under the commutating zone (pole arc ≈ brush shift zone) per pole is given by:

Conductors under commutating zone \(Z_c = \frac{Z}{A \cdot P} \times \frac{\text{Pole arc}}{\text{Pole pitch}}\)

These conductors carry current that neither helps nor hurts commutation — they are in the "neutral" zone. In practice, compensating windings and interpoles neutralise this zone's effect.

04

Remedies — Interpoles & Compensating Windings

Interpoles (Commutating Poles)

Interpoles are small auxiliary poles placed between main poles. They carry the full armature current (series-connected with the armature winding) and produce a flux that exactly neutralises the armature flux in the commutating zone. The result: the coil undergoing commutation has zero resultant flux, so current reversal is smooth and sparkless.[1]

🔑
Interpole Polarity Rules

Generator: Interpole must have the same polarity as the next main pole in the direction of rotation.
Motor: Interpole must have the same polarity as the preceding main pole (behind the direction of rotation).

Fig. 3 — 4-pole DC machine with 4 interpoles (yellow) placed between main poles. Each interpole carries the full armature current \(I_a\) to neutralise armature flux in the commutating zone.

Compensating Windings

While interpoles correct armature reaction only in the commutating zone, compensating windings (CW) eliminate armature reaction across the entire pole face. They are embedded in slots cut into the pole faces and carry the armature current in the opposite direction, producing an MMF that exactly cancels the armature MMF at every point under the pole.[3]

Interpoles — Summary
  • ▸Placed between main poles (in air gap)
  • ▸Series with armature winding
  • ▸Neutralises armature flux in commutating zone only
  • ▸Suitable for medium-duty machines
  • ▸Cannot eliminate flux distortion under the pole arc
Compensating Winding — Summary
  • ▸Embedded in pole-face slots
  • ▸Series with armature winding
  • ▸Neutralises armature MMF across entire pole arc
  • ▸Used in heavy-duty machines with rapid load changes
  • ▸Used with interpoles for complete neutralisation
📐
Required turns for CW and Interpole

Compensating winding AT/pole = \(\frac{I_a Z}{2AP} \times \frac{\text{pole arc}}{\text{pole pitch}}\). Interpole AT/pole must overcome armature AT in commutating zone plus provide a small reversing flux. Since both carry \(I_a\), required turns can be directly computed.

05

Universal Motor — Why Series Motors Work on AC

A DC series motor can operate on AC supply — it is then called a universal motor. The key reason is that when AC supply is given, both the field flux \(\phi\) and the armature current \(I_a\) reverse simultaneously (since they are in series), so the torque \(T \propto \phi \cdot I_a\) remains always positive. This means a net unidirectional torque is produced.

💡
Why Shunt Motor Cannot Run on AC

In a DC shunt motor, the field and armature are in parallel. On AC, the field winding has large inductance, causing the field current to lag far behind the armature current. Since \(\phi\) and \(I_a\) are no longer in phase, the torque \(T \propto \phi \cdot I_a\) alternates in direction — producing oscillating torque with zero average. Additionally, laminated cores would be required. Hence shunt (and separately excited) DC motors cannot operate on AC.

Series Motor Torque Equation T ∝ φ · Ia (DC series: φ ∝ Ia ∴ T ∝ Ia²) On AC: φ(t) = Φm sin(ωt) Ia(t) = Im sin(ωt) [both reverse together — series circuit] Instantaneous torque: T(t) ∝ Φm Im sin²(ωt) ≥ 0 always Average torque: Tavg ∝ Φm Im / 2 > 0 ✓

Solved Numericals — EMF & Armature Resistance

EX 6.1 Induced EMF of a 24-Slot, 2-Pole DC Generator (Double Layer Winding)
A 24-slot, 2-pole DC generator has 18 turns/coil with double-layer winding. Flux density = 1 Tesla/pole, armature length = 20 cm, radius = 10 cm. Poles designed to cover 80% of pole pitch. Armature angular velocity = 183.2 rad/s. Determine the induced EMF.
Solution
2P = 2 | S = 24 slots | Double layer | T/coil = 18 | B = 1 T/pole ℓ = 20 cm = 0.2 m | r = 10 cm = 0.1 m | Pole coverage = 80% | ω = 183.2 rad/s
STEP 1
Calculate flux per pole (φ)
Total armature surface area \(= 2\pi r \ell = 2\pi \times 0.1 \times 0.2 = 0.1256 \text{ m}^2\)
Area per pole \(= \dfrac{2\pi r\ell}{P} = \dfrac{0.1256}{1} = 0.1256 \text{ m}^2\) (for 1 pole pair, P=1 each side)
Effective area under one pole \(= 0.8 \times \dfrac{2\pi r \ell}{P} = 0.8 \times 0.0628 = 0.05024 \approx 0.05 \text{ m}^2\)
\[\phi = B \times A_{\text{eff}} = 1 \times 0.05 = 0.05 \text{ Wb}\]
STEP 2
Calculate total conductors (Z)
Number of coils = 24 (double layer → one coil per slot)
Total turns = \(18 \times 24 = 432\)
\[Z = 432 \times 2 = 864 \text{ conductors}\]
STEP 3
Calculate speed (N) in rpm
\[N = \frac{60 \times \omega}{2\pi} = \frac{60 \times 183.2}{2\pi} = 1750 \text{ rpm}\]
STEP 4
Apply EMF equation (lap winding, A = 2P = 2 for 2-pole)
\[E_g = \frac{\phi Z N P}{60 A} = \frac{0.05 \times 864 \times 1750 \times 1}{60 \times 2}\] \[E_g = \frac{0.05 \times 864 \times 1750}{120} = 1260 \text{ V}\]
✓ Total Induced EMF = 1260 V (across all parallel paths)

EMF per coil and per conductor: With 2 parallel paths, 12 coils are in each path (24 total). Each path generates 1260 V. Voltage per coil = 1260/12 = 105 V. Each coil has 18 turns, so voltage per turn = 105/18 = 5.83 V. Each turn has 2 conductors, so voltage per conductor = 5.83/2 = 2.9 V.

EX 6.2 Armature Resistance of a 4-Pole Lap-Wound DC Shunt Generator
A 4-pole lap-wound DC shunt generator armature winding consists of 220 turns, each of resistance 0.004 Ω. Find the armature resistance.
Solution
P = 4 | Lap winding → A = P = 4 | Total turns = 220 | Resistance per turn = 0.004 Ω
STEP 1
Find total conductors
220 turns = 440 conductors, so total resistance of all conductors in series = \(0.002 \times 440 = 0.88 \Omega\) (resistance per conductor = 0.004/2 = 0.002 Ω)
STEP 2
Apply armature resistance formula
For a lap winding, the armature resistance is: \[R_a = \frac{R_c \cdot Z}{A^2}\] where \(R_c\) = resistance per conductor = 0.002 Ω, \(Z = 440\), \(A = 4\) \[R_a = \frac{0.002 \times 440}{4^2} = \frac{0.88}{16} = 0.055 \Omega\]
✓ Armature Resistance Ra = 0.055 Ω
🔑
General Formula

For any winding: \(R_a = \dfrac{r_c \cdot Z}{A^2}\) where \(r_c\) is resistance per conductor, \(Z\) = total conductors, \(A\) = parallel paths. This comes from the fact that \(A\) parallel paths each have \(Z/A\) conductors in series.

Solved Numericals — Compound Generator

EX 7.1 Compound Generator — Generated EMF (Long & Short Shunt)
A compound generator is to supply 250 lamps each rated 100 W at 250 V. Armature resistance = 0.06 Ω, series resistance = 0.04 Ω, Rsh = 50 Ω. BCD (brush contact drop) = 1 V/brush. Determine the generated EMF when the machine is connected as (a) long shunt and (b) short shunt.
Solution
250 lamps × 100 W @ 250 V | Total load = 25000 W | V = 250 V R_a = 0.06 Ω | R_se = 0.04 Ω | R_sh = 50 Ω | BCD = 1 V/brush = 2 V total
STEP 1
Find load current and shunt current
\[I_L = \frac{P_{\text{load}}}{V} = \frac{25000}{250} = 100 \text{ A}\] For long shunt: \(I_{sh} = \dfrac{V}{R_{sh}} = \dfrac{250}{50} = 5 \text{ A}\)
STEP 2a
(a) Long Shunt Connection
In long shunt, the shunt field is across the terminal voltage. Series resistance carries \(I_a = I_L + I_{sh} = 100 + 5 = 105 \text{ A}\) \[E_g = V + I_a(R_a + R_{se}) + \text{BCD}\] \[E_g = 250 + 105(0.06 + 0.04) + 2 = 250 + 10.5 + 2 = \boxed{262.5 \text{ V}}\]
STEP 2b
(b) Short Shunt Connection
In short shunt, shunt field is across armature only. Load current flows through series resistance first: \[I_{sh} = \frac{V + I_L R_{se}}{R_{sh}} = \frac{250 + 100 \times 0.04}{50} = \frac{254}{50} = 5.08 \text{ A}\] \[I_a = I_L + I_{sh} = 100 + 5.08 = 105.08 \text{ A}\] \[E_g = V + I_a R_a + I_L R_{se} + \text{BCD}\] \[E_g = 250 + 105.08 \times 0.06 + 100 \times 0.04 + 2 = 250 + 6.305 + 4 + 2 \approx \boxed{262.3 \text{ V}}\]
✓ Long shunt EMF = 262.5 V  |  Short shunt EMF = 262.3 V
EX 7.2 Long Shunt Compound Generator — Power Generated & Distribution
In a long shunt compound generator, terminal voltage = 230 V, delivers 150 A. Rsh = 92 Ω, Ra = 0.032 Ω, Rse = 0.015 Ω, Rd (diverter) = 0.03 Ω. Determine the power generated and its distribution.
Solution
V = 230 V | I_L = 150 A | R_sh = 92 Ω | R_a = 0.032 Ω R_se = 0.015 Ω | R_d = 0.03 Ω (parallel with R_se)
STEP 1
Find equivalent series resistance (diverter parallel with series field)
\[R_{eq} = \frac{R_{se} \times R_d}{R_{se} + R_d} = \frac{0.015 \times 0.03}{0.015 + 0.03} = \frac{0.00045}{0.045} = 0.01 \Omega\]
STEP 2
Find shunt and armature current (long shunt)
\[I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}} = \frac{230}{92} = 2.5 \text{ A}\] \[I_a = I_L + I_{sh} = 150 + 2.5 = 152.5 \text{ A}\]
STEP 3
Find generated EMF
\[E_g = V + I_a(R_a + R_{eq}) = 230 + 152.5(0.032 + 0.01) = 230 + 152.5 \times 0.042\] \[E_g = 230 + 6.405 = 236.405 \text{ V}\]
STEP 4
Power generated in armature
\[P_g = E_g \times I_a = 236.405 \times 152.5 = 36051.76 \text{ W} \approx 36.05 \text{ kW}\]
STEP 5
Power distribution (losses)
① Armature copper loss: \(I_a^2 R_a = (152.5)^2 \times 0.032 = 744.2 \text{ W}\)
② Shunt field loss: \(I_{sh}^2 R_{sh} = (2.5)^2 \times 92 = 575 \text{ W}\)
③ Series field + diverter: \(I_a^2 R_{eq} = (152.5)^2 \times 0.01 = 232.6 \text{ W}\)
Total copper loss = 744.2 + 575 + 232.6 = 1551.8 W
Power delivered to load: \(P_d = V \times I_L = 230 \times 150 = 34500 \text{ W}\)
Check: \(P_g - P_{cu} = 36051.76 - 1551.8 \approx 34500 \text{ W}\) ✓
✓ Power generated = 36.05 kW  |  Total Cu loss = 1551.8 W  |  Power delivered = 34.5 kW

Solved Numericals — DC Shunt Motor

EX 8.1 220 V DC Shunt Motor — Cu Loss, Torque, Shaft Torque, Efficiency
A 220 V DC shunt motor takes a line current of 80 A and runs at 800 rpm. Rsh = 50 Ω, Ra = 0.1 Ω, rotational loss = 1600 W. Find: (1) Cu loss, (2) Torque developed (Tq), (3) Shaft torque (Tsh), (4) Efficiency (η).
Solution
V = 220 V | I_L = 80 A | N = 800 rpm | R_sh = 50 Ω | R_a = 0.1 Ω | Rot. loss = 1600 W
STEP 1
Find armature current
\[I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}} = \frac{220}{50} = 4.4 \text{ A}\] \[I_a = I_L - I_{sh} = 80 - 4.4 = 75.6 \text{ A}\]
STEP 2
(1) Copper losses
Armature Cu loss = \(I_a^2 R_a = (75.6)^2 \times 0.1 = 571.5 \text{ W}\)
Shunt field loss = \(I_{sh}^2 R_{sh} = (4.4)^2 \times 50 = 968 \text{ W}\)
Total Cu loss = \(571.5 + 968 = \boxed{1539.5 \text{ W}}\)
STEP 3
(2) Torque developed (Tq)
Back EMF: \(E_b = V - I_a R_a = 220 - 75.6 \times 0.1 = 212.44 \text{ V}\)
\[T_q = \frac{60}{2\pi N} \cdot E_b I_a = \frac{60}{2\pi \times 800} \times 212.44 \times 75.6\] \[T_q = \frac{60 \times 16060.46}{5026.55} = \boxed{191.7 \text{ N·m}}\]
STEP 4
(3) Shaft torque (Tsh)
\[T_{sh} = \frac{60}{2\pi N}(E_b I_a - P_{\text{rot}}) = \frac{60}{2\pi \times 800}(16060.46 - 1600)\] \[T_{sh} = \frac{60 \times 14460.46}{5026.55} = \boxed{172.6 \text{ N·m}}\]
STEP 5
(4) Efficiency
Power input: \(P_{in} = V \times I_L = 220 \times 80 = 17600 \text{ W}\)
Power output: \(P_{out} = E_b I_a - P_{\text{rot}} = 16060.46 - 1600 = 14460.5 \text{ W}\)
\[\eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} = \frac{14460.5}{17600} = 0.8216 = \boxed{82.16\%}\]
✓ Cu loss = 1539.5 W  |  Tq = 191.7 N·m  |  Tsh = 172.6 N·m  |  η = 82.16%
EX 8.2 DC Motor — Speed and Torque with 10% Flux Reduction
A DC shunt motor runs at 1000 rpm, draws 10 A from a 220 V supply (Ra = 1 Ω). The supply drops to 200 V. Initially current goes to zero, then motor settles at 10 A. Find the new speed. Also, if the flux is reduced to 90% (armature reaction), find the new speed and torque ratio (armature current = 10 A, initial torque reference).
Solution
PART A
Initial back EMF
\[E_{b1} = V_1 - I_a R_a = 220 - 10 \times 1 = 210 \text{ V}\] When supply drops to 200 V: \(E_{b} = 200\) V (if current is zero, \(E_b = V\) momentarily = 200 V, but 210 V of back EMF means the motor decelerates until \(E_b\) settles). At new equilibrium with 10 A: \[E_{b2} = 200 - 10 \times 1 = 190 \text{ V}\] \[\frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{E_{b2}}{E_{b1}} = \frac{190}{210} \Rightarrow N_2 = 1000 \times \frac{190}{210} \approx 905 \text{ rpm}\]
PART B
With flux reduced to 90% (armature reaction), torque same
Since \(T \propto \phi I_a\), for same torque with \(\phi_2 = 0.9\phi_1\): \[\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{\phi_2 I_{a2}}{\phi_1 I_{a1}} = 1 \Rightarrow I_{a2} = \frac{\phi_1}{\phi_2} I_{a1} = \frac{I_{a1}}{0.9} = 11.11 \text{ A}\] New \(E_{b2} = V - I_{a2} R_a = 220 - 11.11 \times (4-Rext) \)... (solve for \(R_{ext}\) from context = 1.792 Ω per notes) \[N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi}: \quad \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{E_{b2}/\phi_2}{E_{b1}/\phi_1} = \frac{E_{b2}}{E_{b1}} \times \frac{\phi_1}{\phi_2}\]
✓ With 10% flux reduction and same torque: Ia increases to ≈ 11.11 A; Speed increases due to reduced flux (N ∝ 1/φ effect dominates)
📖
Shunt Motor Speed Regulation Note

In a shunt motor with armature reaction: flux decreases slightly (ΔΦ↓) → back EMF tends to drop → motor accelerates → armature current drops → new equilibrium at higher speed. Hence in a shunt motor, armature reaction may actually increase the no-load speed. The speed may increase with load — a slight rising speed characteristic.

Solved: Armature Reaction AT/p & Compensating Winding Turns

EX 9.1 8-Pole DC Generator — Armature Reaction AT/p with Brush Shift
An 8-pole DC generator has 480 armature conductors with wave winding. Armature current = 200 A. Find armature reaction AT/p and cross-magnetising and demagnetising AT/p for: (1) Brushes on GNA, (2) Brushes shifted 6° electrical from GNA, (3) Brushes shifted 6° mechanical from GNA.
Solution
P = 8 | Wave winding → A = 2 | Z = 480 | I_a = 200 A
BASE
Total AT/pole
\[\text{AT/p} = \frac{I_a \times Z}{2 \times A \times 2P} = \frac{200 \times 480}{2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 8} = \frac{96000}{32} = 3000 \text{ AT/p}\]
CASE 1
θ = 0 (Brushes on GNA)
\[\text{ATd/p} = \frac{2\theta}{180} \times 3000 = \frac{0}{180} \times 3000 = 0\] \[\text{ATc/p} = 3000 - 0 = 3000 \text{ AT/p}\] Purely cross-magnetising — no demagnetisation.
CASE 2
θ = 6° electrical
\[\text{ATd/p} = \frac{2 \times 6}{180} \times 3000 = \frac{12}{180} \times 3000 = 200 \text{ AT/p}\] \[\text{ATc/p} = \frac{180 - 12}{180} \times 3000 = \frac{168}{180} \times 3000 = 2800 \text{ AT/p}\]
CASE 3
θ = 6° mechanical → convert to electrical
\[\theta_e = \frac{P}{2} \times \theta_m = \frac{8}{2} \times 6 = 24°\text{ electrical}\] \[\text{ATd/p} = \frac{2 \times 24}{180} \times 3000 = \frac{48}{180} \times 3000 = 800 \text{ AT/p}\] \[\text{ATc/p} = \frac{180 - 48}{180} \times 3000 = \frac{132}{180} \times 3000 = 2200 \text{ AT/p}\]
✓ Case 1: ATd=0, ATc=3000  |  Case 2: ATd=200, ATc=2800  |  Case 3: ATd=800, ATc=2200 (all in AT/p)
EX 9.2 DC Machine — Compensating Winding & Interpole Turns/Pole
A DC machine has armature amp-turns/pole = 15000, ratio of pole arc to pole pitch = 0.68, interpolar air-gap length = 1 cm, additional flux density required = 0.25 T/p. Rated armature current = 850 A. Calculate: (a) CW turns/pole, (b) IPW turns/pole.
Solution
AT/p = 15000 | Pole arc/pole pitch = 0.68 | Lg = 0.01 m | ΔB = 0.25 T | I_a = 850 A
CW
Compensating winding turns/pole
CW must produce AT/p = pole arc fraction × armature AT/p
\[\text{CW AT/p} = 0.68 \times 15000 = 10200 \text{ AT/p}\] \[\text{CW turns/p} = \frac{10200}{I_a} = \frac{10200}{850} = 12 \text{ turns/pole}\]
IPW Base
Interpole base AT/p
IPW must neutralise armature AT in remaining zone (1 − 0.68) = 0.32 of pole pitch: \[\text{IPW base AT/p} = 0.32 \times 15000 = 4800 \text{ AT/p}\] \[\text{Base turns} = \frac{4800}{850} = 5.65 \approx 6 \text{ turns}\]
IPW Extra
Additional AT for reversing flux in interpolar gap
\[\text{Additional AT} = \frac{\Delta B \times L_g}{\mu_0} = \frac{0.25 \times 0.01}{4\pi \times 10^{-7}} = \frac{0.0025}{1.2566 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 1989 \text{ AT/p}\] \[\text{Total IPW AT/p} = 4800 + 1989 = 6789 \text{ AT/p}\] \[\text{IPW turns/p} = \frac{6789}{850} \approx 8 \text{ turns/pole}\]
✓ CW turns/pole = 12  |  IPW turns/pole = 8

GATE & IES Previous Year Questions

GATE EE Effect of armature reaction on resultant flux in a DC generator 2019

In a DC generator, the effect of armature reaction on the resultant flux under the leading pole tip is to:

  • (A) Increase flux under leading pole tip and decrease under trailing pole tip
  • ✓ (B) Increase under leading tip, decrease under trailing tip — net reduction due to saturation
  • (C) Increase flux uniformly under both pole tips
  • (D) No change in flux distribution
📖
Explanation

Armature flux adds to main flux under the leading pole tip and opposes under the trailing tip. However, saturation means the gain under the leading tip is less than the loss under the trailing tip → net flux decreases.

GATE EE Interpole polarity in a DC motor 2016

In a 4-pole DC motor rotating clockwise, the polarity of the interpole (located between the main poles N–S in the direction of rotation) should be:

  • (A) Same as the next N pole in direction of rotation
  • ✓ (B) Same as the preceding N pole (behind the direction of rotation)
  • (C) Opposite to both adjacent main poles
  • (D) Same as both adjacent main poles
📖
Explanation

In a motor, MNA shifts backward (opposite to rotation). The interpole must oppose the armature flux in the commutating zone. Since armature MMF is in the direction the rotor is coming from, the interpole polarity must match the preceding main pole — i.e., same as main pole behind the direction of rotation.

IES E&T Compensating winding vs interpole — correct statement 2018

Which of the following statements about compensating windings in DC machines is correct?

  • (A) Connected in parallel with armature; used only in motors
  • ✓ (B) Embedded in pole-face slots, series with armature; neutralises armature MMF across entire pole arc
  • (C) Used only in generators; not required in motors
  • (D) Replaces interpoles in low-speed machines only
📖
Explanation

Compensating windings are embedded in slots cut into the pole shoes and connected in series with the armature. They produce an MMF that cancels the armature MMF under the full pole arc. They are used in heavy-duty machines prone to flashover and always used together with interpoles for complete compensation.

GATE EE Shunt motor — effect of armature reaction on speed 2021

In a DC shunt motor, the effect of armature reaction (assuming flux is reduced by 5%) on the no-load speed is to:

  • (A) Decrease the speed proportionally
  • ✓ (B) Increase the speed — since N ∝ Eb/φ, reduced φ raises N
  • (C) Keep the speed unchanged since armature reaction is cross-magnetising
  • (D) Cause the motor to stall
📖
Explanation

Since \(N \propto E_b/\phi\), if φ decreases (due to armature reaction saturation effect), and \(E_b \approx V\) remains approximately constant, the speed increases. This is why in a shunt motor under heavy load with armature reaction, the speed may actually rise — a phenomenon that can lead to unstable operation at light loads.

IES E&T Numerical — Series motor torque ratio with 20% current increase 2017

In a DC series motor, if current increases from 10 A to 12 A (unsaturated), the torque increases by approximately:

  • (A) 20%
  • (B) 44%
  • ✓ (C) 44% — since T ∝ Ia², T2/T1 = (12/10)² = 1.44
  • (D) 40%
📖
Explanation

In a DC series motor (unsaturated): \(T \propto \phi I_a \propto I_a^2\). So \(\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{I_{a2}}{I_{a1}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{12}{10}\right)^2 = 1.44\). This is a 44% increase. For a shunt motor (φ = const): \(T \propto I_a\) → only 20% increase.

Chapter Summary

Armature Reaction

Armature current produces MMF along GNA axis. Causes MNA shift, flux distortion, and net demagnetisation (due to saturation). Forward in generator, backward in motor.

AT/p Components

Total AT/p = IaZ/(4AP). Split into ATd/p (demagnetising, along field axis) and ATc/p (cross-mag, ⊥ to field). Only when brushes are shifted does ATd appear.

Interpoles

Series-connected. Neutralise armature flux in commutating zone only. Generator: same as next pole. Motor: same as preceding pole. Prevents sparking at brushes.

Compensating Winding

Embedded in pole-face slots. Series with armature. Cancels armature MMF across entire pole arc. Used with interpoles for complete neutralisation in heavy-duty machines.

Shunt Motor Speed

N ∝ Eb/φ. Armature reaction reduces φ → speed may rise with load. In extreme cases, speed characteristic becomes rising — potentially unstable.

Series Motor Torque

T ∝ Ia² (unsaturated). Universal motor: both φ and Ia reverse on AC → T always positive. Shunt motor cannot run on AC (phase difference kills average torque).
