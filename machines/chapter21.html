4.1

Introduction — Single Phase Induction Motors

Single phase induction motors (1φ IM) operate on a single-phase AC supply and are by far the most widely used fractional horsepower motors in domestic and light-industrial applications. They cover ratings from 1 W to 1 kW and run on the same 1φ supply available in homes and offices.[1]

💡
Why 1φ Induction Motors are So Popular

If the fuse blows off in a 3φ motor during light-load running, it continues to run as a 1φ motor with reduced speed and increased current (single-phasing). 1φ motors are popular because of their small ratings (1 W to 1 kW) and because domestic supply is 1φ. They are used in fans, refrigerators, washing machines, pumps, hair dryers, and small appliances worldwide.[1]

Fig 4.1 — Cross-section of single phase induction motor. Construction essentially same as 3φ IM, but stator carries 1φ winding. Rotor is always squirrel cage type with skewing.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.1; Chapman [1], §10.1
Key Constructional Features
  • →Stator carries 1φ winding for 2, 4, or 6 poles
  • →Rotor is essentially squirrel cage type with skewing
  • →Starting winding differs based on starting method adopted
  • →Construction otherwise identical to 3φ motors
  • →Ratings: 1 W (toys) to 1 kW (sump pumps, ACs)
Typical Applications
  • →Ceiling fans, table fans, exhaust fans
  • →Refrigerators, washing machines, AC compressors
  • →Sump pumps, centrifugal pumps, grinders
  • →Hair dryers, Xerox machines, advertisement displays
  • →Small workshop tools (drills, lathes, blowers)
4.2

Double Revolving Field Theory

When a single-phase supply is applied across a stator containing one winding, a pulsating (alternating) magnetic field is produced — not a rotating field. This pulsating field does not produce starting torque. The complete analysis and equivalent circuit representation is based on the Double Field Revolving Theory.[2]

🔑
Core Principle of Double Revolving Field Theory

According to this theory, a pulsating magnetic field can be resolved into two rotating magnetic fields of equal magnitude (φm/2) running at synchronous speed in opposite directions. The net effect of these two fields on the rotor determines motor behaviour. At standstill, the net torque is zero. Once externally started, the motor develops net torque and accelerates.[2]

Fig 4.2 — Double revolving field theory: a pulsating field φ = φₘsin(ωt) resolves into forward field (φₘ/2 at +Ns) and backward field (φₘ/2 at −Ns). At standstill T_f = T_b, net torque = 0.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.2, p.452; Chapman [1], §10.2

Slip for Forward and Backward Fields

As the rotor rotates at speed N in the direction of the forward field (same direction as conventional rotation), the slip values for the two fields differ.

Slip Expressions — Forward and Backward Fields
FORWARD FIELD (φ_f): Rotates at +Ns, Rotor at N S_f = (Ns − N) / Ns = S (same as standard 3φ slip) BACKWARD FIELD (φ_b): Rotates at −Ns, Rotor at N (opposite direction) S_b = (Ns − (−N)) / Ns = (Ns + N) / Ns = (2Ns − (Ns−N)) / Ns = 2 − S (backward slip = 2 − forward slip) BOUNDARY CONDITIONS — Motoring Region (S = 0 to 1, S_b = 2 to 1): At Start (S=1): S_f = 1, S_b = 1 At Running (S=0): S_f = 0, S_b = 2
⚠️
Why 1φ IM is NOT Self-Starting

At standstill (S = 1), both S_f = 1 and S_b = 1. The forward and backward torques are equal and opposite, giving net torque = 0. Therefore, the motor will not start on its own. However, if given an initial push in either direction, the motor will accelerate in that direction because the torque in that direction becomes dominant. The double revolving field theory fully explains this behaviour.[2]

GATE CONCEPTRotor Speed and Slip in 1φ IM — The Non-Uniform Field Effect

In a 1φ IM, the backward field produces a backward torque at all rotor speeds (except at synchronous speed where backward torque → 0). This backward torque reduces the net useful torque and efficiency compared to a 3φ motor of the same rating. The backward-field effect also causes noise (uneven magnetic pull) and lower power factor — important distinctions tested in GATE.

4.3

Torque-Speed Characteristics of 1φ IM

The torque-speed characteristic of a single phase IM is derived from the superposition of the forward and backward torque components. The entire operating region is between the boundary conditions S = 0 and S = 2 (equivalent to motoring region for the 1φ case).[2]

Fig 4.3 — T-S characteristics of 1φ IM. Forward torque T_f (solid green), backward torque T_b (dashed red), net torque T_net = T_f − T_b (orange). At S=1, T_f = T_b, net torque = 0 — motor not self-starting.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.2, p.455; Chapman [1], §10.2
Torque Expressions — Forward and Backward Components
FORWARD TORQUE: T_f ∝ I²R₂ / S_f (rotor resistance / forward slip) BACKWARD TORQUE: T_b ∝ I²R₂ / S_b (rotor resistance / backward slip) GROSS TORQUE (Air gap torque): Tg = (60 / 2πNs) × (P_f − P_b) where P_f = power input to forward rotor, P_b = power input to backward rotor

The torque-speed characteristics explain why 1φ motors have lower efficiency and higher noise compared to 3φ motors — the backward torque continuously opposes motion during running, wasting energy in the backward rotor circuit.

4.4

Rotating Magnetic Field with 2-Phase Supply

While a single-phase supply produces only a pulsating field, a two-phase supply creates a true Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF), identical in nature to the RMF produced by a 3φ supply. This principle is the basis of all starting methods for 1φ motors.[2]

Fig 4.4 — RMF with two-phase supply: φ_A = φₘcosωt (horizontal) and φ_B = φₘsinωt (vertical, 90° apart). At each instant, the resultant field is φₘ rotating at Ns — same as 3φ RMF.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.3; Chapman [1], §10.3
✅
Key Insight — 2φ vs 3φ RMF

Like a 3φ supply, a 2φ supply also produces an RMF which rotates at synchronous speed, but with reduced strength φₘ only (compared to (3/2)φₘ for balanced 3φ). The phase displacement must be 90° (not 120° like 3φ). This is why adding a 90°-displaced auxiliary winding converts a 1φ IM into an effective 2φ motor during starting.

4.5

Starting Methods for Single Phase Induction Motors

Depending on the starting method adopted, 1φ induction motors are classified into different types. All starting methods work on the principle of creating a phase displacement between the main winding current (I_m) and auxiliary/starting winding current (I_s) to produce a net starting torque \(T_{st} \propto I_m I_s \sin\alpha\).[2]

TypePhase Displacement αStarting TorqueRating RangeApplications
Resistance Split-Phase~30°LowUp to 250 WDomestic fans, blowers, sump pumps
Capacitor Start≥90°High250–750 WCompressors, high-inertia loads
Capacitor Start & Run~90°High750 W–1 kWCeiling fans, AC motors, pumps
Shaded PoleVery smallVery Low1–50 WToys, small fans, Xerox machines

(1) Resistance Split-Phase Motor

The stator contains two windings connected in parallel: (i) Main winding (Running winding) — highly inductive with large number of turns; (ii) Starting (Auxiliary) winding — intentionally made highly resistive with fewer, thinner turns. This introduces a phase angle difference α ≈ 30° between I_m and I_s.[1]

Fig 4.5 — Resistance split-phase motor: two windings in parallel. Starting winding (high R, low L) carries Is leading Im by ~30°. Motor starts when α≠0. Centrifugal switch disconnects starting winding at ~80% Ns.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.4; Chapman [1], §10.4
💡
Resistance Split-Phase — Important Points

This is most popular for motors rating around 250 W only. Once the motor reaches ~80% of rated speed, the centrifugal switch opens to isolate starting winding from supply, and the motor continues to run with main winding only. This is not suitable for frequent starting/stopping because low current drawn is high (temperature rise in the resistor). Applications: domestic/sump motors, centrifugal motors, fans/blowers up to 250 W.[1]

(2) Capacitor Start Motor

A capacitor (electrolytic type) is connected in series with the starting winding through a centrifugal switch. This creates a phase angle α ≥ 90° between I_m and I_s, giving high starting torque. The starting current is comparatively low because α is large.[1]

Fig 4.6 — Capacitor start motor: electrolytic capacitor in series with starting winding creates α ≥ 90°, giving maximum T_st ∝ I_m I_s sin(α). CS disconnects at rated speed. Used for 250–750 W, high-inertia loads.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.4.2; Chapman [1], §10.4

(3) Capacitor Start and Capacitor Run Motor

In this type, there is no centrifugal switch. The capacitor (electrolytic) is continuously connected to supply along with the starting winding. The design cost is high comparatively because the starting winding must be efficient for continuous duty. It starts and runs as a near-perfect 2φ motor (capacitor run motors start as 2φ but run as 1φ motor with low power factor).[2]

🔑
Capacitor Run Motors — Why They're So Popular (Ceiling Fans, AC)

Capacitor run motors are popular because of their silent operation with smooth T-S characteristics and good starting as well as running torques. The continuously connected capacitor keeps the phase displacement high during running too, improving power factor and efficiency. All ceiling fans, AC indoor units, and washing machine motors of ratings 750 W–1 kW use this type. Two small-range capacitors may be used in parallel, with external switching to optimise starting and running torque separately.[2]

Fig 4.7 — Capacitor Start & Run motor: No CS switch. Capacitor continuously connected gives near-2φ operation throughout. Silent running, smooth T-S, good PF. Most popular for ceiling fans, AC, pumps.
Basis: Chapman [1], §10.4; Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.4.3

Reversing Direction of Split-Phase Motors

To reverse the direction of rotation of a split-phase motor, either the main winding terminals or the starting winding terminals must be reversed — but not both. Reversing both (or the supply terminals) does not change direction.

Direction Reversal — Split Phase Motors
To REVERSE direction → (do ONE of these): (1) Reverse main winding (Mw) terminals (2) Reverse starting winding (Sw) terminals (3) Interchanging capacitor from starting wdg to main wdg Does NOT reverse direction: (4) Reversing both Mw AND Sw terminals (5) Reversing supply terminals (same as reversing both)
4.6

Shaded Pole Motor

The shaded pole motor is the simplest, most compact single-phase induction motor. It has a totally different stator construction from split-phase types. It contains projected poles with a split and a short-circuited copper strip (shading coil) solidly closed in one region of the pole called the shaded region. Ratings are 1–50 W, mostly used in toys and compact appliances.[1]

Fig 4.8 — Shaded pole motor: salient poles with split/shading coil (Cu strip). Flux sweeps from unshaded to shaded region creating a moving MMF that produces starting torque. Direction fixed by shading coil position.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.5; Chapman [1], §10.5
⚠️
Shaded Pole Motor — Unique Characteristics

Starting torques are very low and non-uniform magnetic field results in non-uniform speed and torque. Direction of rotation is always from unshaded to shaded region — this cannot be changed without physically rotating or rewinding the stator. To reverse direction, two shading coils are needed which can be externally switched. Despite low efficiency and fixed direction, it is popular for its compactness and silent operation in toys, small fans, Xerox machines, hair dryers, advertisement displays, compact appliances like hair dryers.[1]

Shaded Pole — Working (Instants A,B,C,D)
  • A→BFlux increasing (+ve), shading coil EMF opposes → flux crowded in unshaded region
  • B→CFlux nearly at peak, rate of change ≈ 0, shading coil EMF ≈ 0 → flux uniform throughout pole
  • C→DFlux decreasing, shading coil opposes in +ve manner → flux crowded in shaded region
  • →Net effect: moving/creeping MMF from unshaded to shaded → creates start torque
Shaded Pole — Applications
  • →Toys (very small ratings)
  • →Small fans and desk fans
  • →Cooling fans in laptops
  • →Xerox machines, advertisement displays
  • →Compact appliances like hair dryers
  • →Popular for compactness, 1–50 W ratings
4.7

Equivalent Circuit of Single Phase Induction Motor

According to the double field revolving theory, the 1φ IM rotor reacts to both the forward and backward fields. Therefore, its equivalent circuit has one stator and two rotor representations — one for the forward field and one for the backward field, each carrying half of the magnetising reactance and rotor parameters.[2]

Fig 4.9 — Equivalent circuit of 1φ IM based on double revolving field theory. Forward branch (top): R₂'/2S_f in series with jX₂'/2, shunted by jXm/2. Backward branch (bottom): identical but R₂'/2(2−S). Z_eq = Z₁ + Z_f + Z_b.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §9.6, p.463; Chapman [1], §10.6
Equivalent Circuit — Key Impedance Expressions
STATOR IMPEDANCE: Z₁ = R₁ + jX₁ FORWARD IMPEDANCE (Z_f): Z_f = jXm/2 ∥ (R₂'/2S + jX₂'/2) = R_f + jX_f (where S = forward slip) BACKWARD IMPEDANCE (Z_b): Z_b = jXm/2 ∥ (R₂'/2(2−S) + jX₂'/2) = R_b + jX_b (where 2−S = backward slip S_b) TOTAL EQUIVALENT IMPEDANCE: Z_eq = Z₁ + Z_f + Z_b SUPPLY CURRENT: I = V / Z_eq Power factor: PF = cos∅ (lagging)
4.8

Performance Analysis — Power and Torque

The performance of a single-phase induction motor is determined from the equivalent circuit. For accurate analysis, core losses (iron, friction, windage) should be included while eliminating shunt branch resistance R_c from the actual equivalent circuit.[2]

Power Flow and Torque Equations — 1φ IM
POWER INPUT TO FORWARD ROTOR (Air gap power, forward): P_f = I² × R_f (I = stator current) POWER INPUT TO BACKWARD ROTOR: P_b = I² × R_b NET AIR GAP POWER (to rotor): P_net = P_f − P_b GROSS (AIR GAP) TORQUE: Tg = (60 / 2πNs) × (P_f − P_b) MECHANICAL POWER OUTPUT (Gross): P_mech = (1−S)(P_f − P_b) SHAFT POWER (Net mechanical output): P_shaft = (1−S)(P_f − P_b) − P_rotational where P_rotational = friction + windage + iron losses EFFICIENCY: η = P_shaft / (V × I × cos∅) = [(1−S)(P_f−P_b) − P_rot] / (V × I × cos∅) SHAFT TORQUE: T_shaft = (60 / 2πN) × P_shaft
✅
Important Accuracy Note — NL Test Correction

In the NL test, wattmeter readings represent iron, friction, and windage losses combined. But iron losses are separated to represent them as shunt branch R_c in the actual equivalent circuit. From the stator resistance value, rotational loss = \(W_0 - I_0^2 R_1\). Net shaft output then correctly excludes rotational losses from mechanical output.[2]

EXAMPLE 4.1Performance Calculation from Equivalent Circuit
A 1φ, 230 V, 50 Hz induction motor has the following equivalent circuit parameters: R₁ = 1.5 Ω, X₁ = 2.4 Ω, R₂' = 4 Ω, X₂' = 2.4 Ω, Xm = 80 Ω. At slip S = 0.04, find: (i) supply current, (ii) power factor, (iii) gross torque, (iv) gross mechanical power output.
Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Find S_f and S_b
\(S_f = S = 0.04\)  \(S_b = 2 - S = 2 - 0.04 = 1.96\)

Step 2: Forward branch impedance Z_f
\(Z_{r,f} = \frac{R_2'}{2S_f} + j\frac{X_2'}{2} = \frac{4}{2 \times 0.04} + j\frac{2.4}{2} = 50 + j1.2 \,\Omega\)
\(Z_f = \frac{j\frac{X_m}{2} \times Z_{r,f}}{j\frac{X_m}{2} + Z_{r,f}} = \frac{j40 \times (50+j1.2)}{j40 + 50+j1.2}\)
\(= \frac{j40 \times (50+j1.2)}{50 + j41.2}\)

Numerator: \(j40(50+j1.2) = j2000 - 48 = -48 + j2000\)
Denominator magnitude: \(|50+j41.2| = 64.9\angle 39.5°\)
\(Z_f \approx 30.7\angle 50.5° = 19.6 + j23.7 \,\Omega\)

Step 3: Backward branch impedance Z_b
\(Z_{r,b} = \frac{R_2'}{2S_b} + j\frac{X_2'}{2} = \frac{4}{2 \times 1.96} + j1.2 = 1.02 + j1.2 \,\Omega\)
\(Z_b = \frac{j40(1.02+j1.2)}{j40+1.02+j1.2} = \frac{j40.8-48}{1.02+j41.2}\)
\(\approx 0.98 + j1.18 \,\Omega\)

Step 4: Total Z_eq
\(Z_{eq} = Z_1 + Z_f + Z_b = (1.5+j2.4) + (19.6+j23.7) + (0.98+j1.18)\)
\(= 22.08 + j27.28 \,\Omega\)
\(|Z_{eq}| = \sqrt{22.08^2 + 27.28^2} = 35.2 \,\Omega\)

Step 5: Supply current and power factor
\(I = \frac{V}{|Z_{eq}|} = \frac{230}{35.2} = 6.53 \,\text{A}\)
\(\cos\phi = \frac{22.08}{35.2} = 0.627 \text{ (lagging)}\)

Step 6: Powers and Torque
\(P_f = I^2 R_f = 6.53^2 \times 19.6 = 836 \,\text{W}\) \(P_b = I^2 R_b = 6.53^2 \times 0.98 = 41.7 \,\text{W}\)
Synchronous speed: \(N_s = \frac{120 \times 50}{4} = 1500 \,\text{rpm}\) (assuming 4-pole)
\(T_g = \frac{60}{2\pi \times 1500}(836 - 41.7) = \frac{60 \times 794.3}{2\pi \times 1500} = 5.06 \,\text{N·m}\)
\(P_{mech} = (1-S)(P_f - P_b) = 0.96 \times 794.3 = 762.5 \,\text{W}\)

4.9

Determination of Equivalent Circuit Parameters — Tests

Equivalent circuit parameters of a 1φ IM are determined from three tests, similar in principle to 3φ IM tests:[2]

(1) No-Load Test

Apply rated voltage across the given 1φ main winding and allow it to run freely on no-load (no output). Since the motor runs nearly at synchronous speed, S ≈ 0.

No-Load Test — Key Relations
S → 0: R₂'/2S_f → ∞ (open circuit) S_b = 2 − 0 = 2: R₂'/2(2−S) → R₂'/4 (finite, NOT open) Measurements: V₀ (rated), I₀ (NL current), W₀ (NL power) Rotational losses = W₀ − I₀²R₁ (subtract stator Cu loss) V_AB = I₀ × (Xm/2) → find Xm from no-load test Xm = 2V_AB / I₀ NOTE: LPF wattmeter is required to measure NL power (because NL power factor is very low)

(2) DC Test

Apply small DC voltage across the stator by connecting a voltmeter and ammeter. The ratio V/I gives DC resistance. Convert to AC value:

DC Test — Stator Resistance
R_dc = V_dc / I_dc R_ac = R₁_AC = 1.5 × R_dc (AC resistance ≈ 1.5 × DC resistance)

(3) Blocked Rotor Test

Rotor is initially blocked (N = 0, S = 1). Small voltage is applied through an auto-transformer while ensuring rated current flows through the stator. Since S = 1, Xm/2 has high value → shunt branch is essentially open (neglected). Wattmeter reading gives only Cu losses (stator + rotor).

Blocked Rotor Test — Parameter Extraction
At S = 1: (Shunt branch neglected) W_BR = I_BR² × R_BR (stator + rotor Cu losses) R_BR = W_BR / I_BR² Z_BR = V_BR / I_BR X_BR = √(Z_BR² − R_BR²) Parameter split: X₁ = X₂' = X_BR / 2 (equal split — standard assumption) R₂' = R_BR − R₁ (rotor resistance referred to stator)

🔑
Why 1φ Motors Are Costlier for Same Rating

For the same power rating, 1φ motors are comparatively having more size, cost, and lower torques, and run with noise due to the backward field. Cost is also comparatively more in 1φ because of the auxiliary winding, centrifugal switch, and capacitor required. In contrast, 3φ motors have higher power capacity and are more efficient per unit size. Despite this, 1φ motors dominate domestic markets because 3φ supply is unavailable to most households.[2]

GATE & IES — Previous Year Questions

GATE EEA single-phase induction motor is NOT self-starting becauseGATE 2014
  • (a) the rotor has high resistance
  • ✓ (b) the pulsating field produces equal and opposite torques, giving zero net starting torque
  • (c) the stator resistance is too low
  • (d) the reactance of the motor is too high

Explanation: According to double revolving field theory, a 1φ pulsating field resolves into two RMFs of equal magnitude φₘ/2 rotating in opposite directions at Ns. At standstill (S=1), both produce equal but opposite torques — \(T_f = T_b\) — so net torque = 0. Hence not self-starting.

GATE EEIn a single phase induction motor, the backward slip S_b when forward slip S_f = 0.04 isGATE 2016
  • (a) 0.04
  • (b) 0.96
  • ✓ (c) 1.96
  • (d) 2.04

Explanation: \(S_b = 2 - S_f = 2 - 0.04 = 1.96\). This is the standard relation: forward slip S, backward slip = 2 − S. At running condition (S = 0.04), the backward field rotates at −Ns but rotor is at +N, so relative slip is (Ns + N)/Ns = 2 − S.

IES 2015Which single-phase motor type is most suitable for loads requiring high starting torque with ratings around 250–750 W?
  • (a) Shaded pole motor
  • (b) Resistance split-phase motor
  • ✓ (c) Capacitor start motor
  • (d) Universal motor

Explanation: Capacitor start motors have α ≥ 90° between main and starting winding currents (due to electrolytic capacitor), giving maximum starting torque \(T_{st} \propto I_m I_s \sin\alpha\). Used for 250–750 W high-inertia loads like compressors. Resistance split-phase: low T_st (α ≈ 30°). Shaded pole: very low T_st, 1–50 W only.

GATE EEThe direction of rotation of a shaded pole motor is always fromGATE 2018
  • (a) shaded region to unshaded region
  • ✓ (b) unshaded region to shaded region
  • (c) depends on supply frequency
  • (d) can be either direction based on winding

Explanation: The flux always moves from unshaded to shaded region (in the cycle: at AB the flux is increasing, shading coil opposes and crowds flux in unshaded region; at CD flux is decreasing, shading coil effect crowds flux in shaded region). This creates a sweeping MMF from unshaded → shaded, so rotation is always unshaded → shaded.

IES 2017What happens to the backward slip S_b at the instant when the 1φ IM starts from standstill?
  • (a) S_b = 0
  • ✓ (b) S_b = 1
  • (c) S_b = 2
  • (d) S_b = ∞

Explanation: At start (standstill), N = 0, so S_f = 1. Then S_b = 2 − S_f = 2 − 1 = 1. Both slips equal 1 at standstill. At full running (S_f ≈ 0), S_b ≈ 2. The complete motoring region for 1φ IM is S = 0 to 1 (S_b = 2 to 1).

GATE EETo reverse the direction of a capacitor-start motor, one shouldGATE 2019
  • (a) Reverse both main winding and starting winding terminals
  • (b) Reverse supply terminals only
  • ✓ (c) Reverse either main winding terminals OR starting winding terminals (not both)
  • (d) Interchange the capacitor from starting to main winding

Explanation: Reversing only ONE winding (main OR starting, not both) reverses the phase sequence of the two currents, reversing the direction of the resultant rotating field and hence rotation. Reversing BOTH windings (or supply terminals) is equivalent to reversing neither — the relative phase between the currents is unchanged.

Chapter Summary

Double Revolving Field

Pulsating 1φ field = two RMFs of φₘ/2 in opposite directions at Ns. At standstill T_f = T_b, net T = 0. NOT self-starting. Once started, net torque ≠ 0 in direction of rotation.

Slip Relations

Forward slip: S_f = S. Backward slip: S_b = 2 − S. At start: S_f = S_b = 1. At running: S_f → 0, S_b → 2. Motoring region: S = 0 to 1.

Starting Methods

Resistance split-phase: α ≈ 30°, low T_st, ≤250 W. Capacitor start: α ≥ 90°, high T_st, 250–750 W. Cap start & run: always on, 2φ operation, 750–1000 W. Shaded pole: 1–50 W, compactness.

Equivalent Circuit

Z_eq = Z₁ + Z_f + Z_b. Forward: jXm/2 ‖ (R₂'/2S + jX₂'/2). Backward: jXm/2 ‖ (R₂'/2(2−S) + jX₂'/2). Tg = (60/2πNs)(P_f − P_b).

Starting Torque

T_st ∝ I_m · I_s · sinα. Max at α = 90°. Capacitor creates α ≥ 90° — highest T_st. Direction reversal: reverse ONLY one winding (Mw or Sw). Reversing both = no change.

1φ vs 3φ Comparison

3φ: self-starting, higher efficiency, smaller size for same rating, smooth torque. 1φ: backward field causes noise, lower efficiency, needs auxiliary components, costlier per kW.
