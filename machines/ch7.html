2.1

The Speed Equation — Foundation of Speed Control

For any DC motor, the back-EMF (\( E_b \)) is given by \( E_b \) = V − \( I_a \)\( R_a \), and the motor speed is directly proportional to the back-EMF and inversely proportional to the flux per pole. This fundamental relationship defines every speed control strategy available for DC motors.[1]

DC Motor Speed Equation — Master Formula
Back-EMF: \( E_b = V - I_a R_a \) Speed equation: \( N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \propto \frac{V - I_a R_a}{\phi} \) Three ways to vary \( N \): (1) Flux control (\( \phi \)): Vary field current → flux → Field weakening (2) Resistance control (\( R_a \)): Add external resistance in armature circuit (3) Voltage control (\( V \)): Change supply voltage to armature Classification of motor: A → Differential (unstable — not used in practice) B → Shunt (constant flux) C → Cumulative (most commonly used series/compound)
📐
Key Insight — Three Handles on Speed

The speed equation gives exactly three independent variables that can be manipulated: flux (\( \phi \)), armature circuit resistance (\( R_a \) + \( R_{ext} \)), and terminal voltage (V). Each handle produces a different characteristic, suitable for different load types. GATE frequently asks which method gives speeds above rated and which below rated.

Fig 2.1 — Three independent handles for DC motor speed control. Field weakening gives above-rated speeds; armature resistance gives below-rated; voltage control covers both ranges.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.7, p.354; Chapman [1], §8.4
2.2

Shunt Motor — Field Weakening (Field Current Control)

In a shunt motor, the field winding is connected in parallel with the armature. The field current (\( I_{sh} \)) determines the flux (\( \phi \)). By connecting an external resistance (rheostat) in series with the field winding, the field current can be reduced, which weakens the flux and thereby increases the speed above rated value. This is the most efficient speed control method for shunt motors.[2]

Field Weakening — Circuit Equations
Normal condition: \( I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}} \) \( \phi = \phi_{sh\_rated} \) With added \( R_{ext} \): \( I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh} + R_{ext}} \) \( \phi = \phi_{sh\_new} < \phi_{rated} \) Speed relation: \( N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \) Since \( E_b \approx V \) (\( I_a R_a \) is small), \( N \propto \frac{1}{\phi} \) Result: ↑ \( R_{ext} \) → ↓ \( I_{sh} \) → ↓ \( \phi \) → ↑ \( N \) (above rated) Minimum speed: Rated speed (at \( R_{ext} = 0 \)) Maximum speed: Limited by commutation & mechanical stability
Fig 2.2 — Field weakening circuit (left): \( R_{ext} \) in series with field winding reduces \( I_{sh} \) → \( \phi \) → raises speed above rated. Speed-torque curve (right): Field weakening = variable torque, constant power zone above rated speed.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.7 p.354; Chapman [1], §8.4
Advantages of Field Weakening
  • →Minimum speed is rated speed only
  • →Highly efficient — field circuit current is small, so copper loss in \( R_{ext} \) is negligible
  • →No need of large rheostat (field current ≈ 2–5% of armature current)
  • →No need for additional cooling
  • →Also called Variable Torque Constant Power speed control
Limitations & Precautions
  • →Speed control only above rated — cannot go below rated
  • →Requires 4-point starter for wide range speed control
  • →Additional armature reaction as main flux is reduced → needs interpoles & compensating winding
  • →Field resistance must be at minimum position during starting
  • →Excessive field weakening → dangerously high speed → mechanical failure
⚠️
4-Point Starter — Why Required

In a standard 3-point starter, the hold-in coil is in series with the field circuit. If the field rheostat is increased to weaken the flux for speed control, the hold-in coil also loses current and may release the starter arm, disconnecting the supply — a dangerous situation. The 4-point starter connects the hold-in coil directly across the supply (independent of the field circuit), preventing this problem.[2]

2.3

Shunt Motor — Armature Resistance (Rheostatic) Control

By inserting an external resistance (\( R_{ext} \)) in series with the armature circuit, the back-EMF drops, which reduces the speed. This method gives speeds only below rated. Because flux (\( \phi \)) remains constant (shunt field is unaffected), the torque equation T = k\( \phi \)\( I_a \) means torque capacity stays the same — this is essentially voltage control without varying supply voltage.[1]

Armature Resistance Control — Equations
With \( R_{ext} \) in armature circuit: \( E_b = V - I_a(R_a + R_{ext}) \) ↓ \( E_b \) ↓ \( N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \) → since \( \phi = \text{const} \), \( N \propto [V - I_a(R_a + R_{ext})] \) Speed range: From rated speed (\( R_{ext}=0 \)) down to near zero Torque: Constant torque (\( \phi = \text{constant} \)) Power: Variable (\( P = T \cdot \omega \) → power decreases with speed) Efficiency: Poor — large copper loss in \( R_{ext} = I_a^2 \times R_{ext} \)
Fig 2.3 — Armature resistance control: \( R_{ext} \) inserted in series with armature. Flux (\( \phi \)sh) remains constant. Speed decreases as \( R_{ext} \) increases — below rated only. Less efficient due to I²R loss in \( R_{ext} \).
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.7, p.356; Chapman [1], §8.4
💡
Why ARCC is Called Constant Torque / Variable Power

In armature resistance control, flux (\( \phi \)) remains constant at rated value (the shunt field is unaffected). Since torque T = k\( \phi \)\( I_a \), and if \( I_a \) is held constant (same load torque), the torque remains constant regardless of speed. Power = T × ω therefore decreases as speed decreases. This is why ARCC is called Constant Torque, Variable Power speed control. In contrast, field weakening (FLOSC) is Variable Torque, Constant Power.

Advantages
  • →Simple — no need of 4-point starter
  • →No additional armature reaction problem
  • →Smooth control from rated speed down to zero
  • →Suitable for traction-type loads
Disadvantages
  • →Large copper loss in external resistance → poor efficiency
  • →Requires additional cooling for external resistor
  • →Voltage regulation poor — speed varies with load
  • →Arm. rheostat must be at maximum position during starting
2.4

Shunt Motor — Voltage Control & Ward–Leonard Method

In voltage control, the terminal voltage supplied to the armature is varied while maintaining the field excitation (and flux) at rated value. Since N ∝ \( E_b \) ∝ V (when \( I_a \)\( R_a \) is small), varying V gives smooth, stepless speed control in both directions — above and below rated — without the losses associated with armature resistance control.[2]

The most important practical implementation is the Ward–Leonard method (also called the Ward–Leonard–Ilgner method when a flywheel is added). A separately excited DC generator driven by an AC or DC motor provides a variable DC voltage to the main motor. This method is used in steel rolling mills, paper mills, and mine hoists where precise bidirectional speed control is essential.[3]

Multiple Voltage Control & Ward–Leonard — Key Points
Principle: \( N \propto \frac{V}{\phi} \) → vary \( V \) while \( \phi = \phi_{rated} \) (constant) → Speed proportional to supply voltage Requirements: Self-excited shunt motor must be separately excited Requires a multiple-voltage DC source OR: a variable-output DC generator (Ward–Leonard) Ward–Leonard system components: 1. AC/DC motor (prime mover) — drives the DC generator 2. Separately excited DC generator (G) — variable voltage output 3. Cross-over switch + variable resistance — controls generator excitation 4. DC main motor (M) — whose speed is to be controlled 5. Flywheel (optional, Ilgner method) — smooths speed fluctuations Advantages: - Smooth speed control from rated to below AND above rated - Control in both directions (motoring & braking) - No resistance losses in armature circuit — highly efficient - Used for large drives: steel rolling, mine winding, cranes Disadvantages: - Very expensive (3 machines: prime mover + gen + motor) - Large floor space & maintenance required - Low overall efficiency due to cascaded machines
Fig 2.4 — Ward–Leonard system: AC/DC prime mover drives a separately excited generator whose output voltage is varied by a cross-over switch and field rheostat. Motor speed tracks generator output voltage — no resistance losses.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.7 p.358; Fitzgerald [3], §7.6
🔑
Ward–Leonard vs Ward–Leonard–Ilgner

Ward–Leonard: Basic system with three machines. Ward–Leonard–Ilgner: A flywheel is connected on the shaft of the generator-prime mover set. When load suddenly increases, the flywheel releases kinetic energy, smoothing out speed fluctuations and reducing peak demand from the supply. This improves overall energy efficiency, especially for loads with sudden torque demands (mine winders, rolling mills).[2]

EX 1Voltage Control — Speed vs Flux Dependence
A shunt motor is running at rated speed 1000 rpm with rated voltage \( V \). If the supply voltage is halved to \( V/2 \), determine the new speed for two cases: (1) Flux \( \phi \) is proportional to field current (linear magnetic circuit), and (2) Flux \( \phi \) remains constant due to a separate fixed excitation.
Solution
From the speed equation, \( N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \approx \frac{V}{\phi} \) (assuming \( I_a R_a \) drop is negligible). Let initial state be \( N_1 = 1000 \text{ rpm} \), \( V_1 = V \), \( \phi_1 = \phi \). Case 1: Flux proportional to field current (Standard Shunt Motor) If \( V_2 = V/2 \), then field current \( I_{sh2} = \frac{V_2}{R_{sh}} = \frac{I_{sh1}}{2} \). Since \( \phi \propto I_{sh} \), new flux \( \phi_2 = \phi/2 \). \( \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{V_2}{V_1} \times \frac{\phi_1}{\phi_2} = \frac{V/2}{V} \times \frac{\phi}{\phi/2} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 = 1 \) \( N_2 = 1000 \text{ rpm} \) (Speed remains unchanged) Case 2: Flux is constant (Separately Excited / Ward-Leonard) Here, \( V_2 = V/2 \) but \( \phi_2 = \phi_1 = \phi \) (constant). \( \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{V_2}{V_1} \times \frac{\phi_1}{\phi_2} = \frac{V/2}{V} \times 1 = \frac{1}{2} \). \( N_2 = 1000 \times 0.5 = 500 \text{ rpm} \) (Speed becomes half) This proves why Ward-Leonard requires a separately excited motor!
2.5

Speed Control of DC Series Motors — Methods Overview

In a DC series motor, the field winding is connected in series with the armature. The series field current equals the armature (line) current: \( I_{se} \) = \( I_L \) = \( I_a \). Therefore \( \phi \) ∝ \( I_a \), and the speed-torque characteristic is hyperbolic. Speed control methods for series motors exploit this unique characteristic.[1]

📋
Six Methods for Series Motor Speed Control

The six standard methods are: (1) Field Diverter — resistance in parallel with field winding; (2) Armature Diverter — resistance in parallel with armature; (3) Tapped Field — change number of series turns; (4) Paralleling Field Coils — switch between series/parallel arrangements; (5) Armature Resistance Control — \( R_{ext} \) in series; (6) Multiple Voltage Control — vary supply voltage.

① Field Diverter Method

A diverter resistance (\( R_d \)) is connected in parallel with the series field winding. Part of the armature current bypasses the field winding, reducing field current (\( I_{se} \) < \( I_L \)), which weakens the flux and increases speed above normal value. The diverter resistance should never be reduced below a minimum value, as the speed becomes dangerously high.[2]

Fig 2.5 — Field diverter: \( R_d \) in parallel with series field. Increasing \( R_d \) from min → field current decreases → flux weakens → speed rises above normal. Never reduce \( R_d \) to zero (speed runaway).
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.8, p.360; Chapman [1], §8.5
2.6

Series Motor — Armature Diverter

A diverter resistance is connected in parallel with the armature. When the diverter is connected, part of the line current is diverted away from the armature, reducing armature current (\( I_a \) < \( I_L \)). To maintain the torque (T = k\( \phi \)\( I_a \)), the motor draws more current from the supply, which now flows through the field winding — increasing field current (and flux). The increased flux reduces speed. This method gives speeds below rated.[2]

Armature Diverter — Analysis
Without diverter: \( I_{se} = I_L = I_a \) \( T = k\phi I_a \) With diverter \( R_d \): \( I_a < I_L \) \( I_{se} = I_L > I_a \) → \( \phi \) ↑ Torque maintenance: Motor draws more \( I_L \) from supply → More current through field → \( \phi \) ↑ → speed ↓ Note: \( T \propto \phi \times I_a \) — both change when diverter is used Speed control is BELOW rated; opposite of field diverter
Fig 2.6 — Armature diverter: \( R_d \) in parallel with armature. Motor torque balance forces more supply current through field → flux increases → speed decreases (below rated).
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.8, p.360
2.7

Series Motor — Tapped Field & Paralleling Field Coils

In the tapped field method, the series field winding has tapping points at intermediate positions. By changing the tap connection, the number of active field turns (\( N_{se} \)) changes. More turns → more flux → slower speed; fewer turns → less flux → higher speed. This gives discrete speed steps only, with a diverter used to obtain intermediate speeds.[2]

Fig 2.7 — Tapped field: intermediate taps on field winding change \( N_{se} \). Fewer active turns → less flux → higher speed. Discrete speed steps only; diverter used for intermediate speeds.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.8, p.361; Chapman [1], §8.5

④ Paralleling Field Coils

The series field winding is wound in multiple sections (coils). By switching between series and parallel arrangements of these coil sections, the effective number of MMF-producing turns per ampere changes, altering the flux. When coils are in series (all 4 in series, each with \( N_{se} \)/4 turns carrying \( I_a \)), flux = \( \phi \) ∝ \( N_{se} \)·\( I_a \). When switched to parallel (2 series + 2 series, each group in parallel), each branch carries \( I_a \)/2 with doubled turns — the net MMF changes, providing different speed steps.[2]

💡
Paralleling Field Coils — Concept

All coils in series: \(\phi\) ∝ \( N_{se} \)·\( I_a \) (maximum flux → minimum speed). When coils are switched to parallel groups, the effective MMF per unit current changes — this reduces flux and raises speed. The field winding is arranged so coils can be externally switched between series and parallel combinations. A diverter may be added to get speeds between the discrete steps.

2.8

Series Motor — Armature Resistance & Multiple Voltage

⑤ Armature Resistance Control (Series Motor)

Similar to the shunt motor case — an external resistance (\( R_{ext} \)) is inserted in series with the armature circuit. The back-EMF drops, reducing speed below rated. However, this method involves large power dissipation in \( R_{ext} \) (\( I_a \)² × \( R_{ext} \)) and requires an additional multiple voltage source. As a result, it is rarely used in practice and is considered highly inefficient.[1]

Series Motor — Armature Resistance Control
Circuit: Supply → \( R_{se} \) (field) → \( R_{ext} \) (external) → \( R_a \) → \( E_b \) → return Effect: \( E_b = V - I_a(R_{se} + R_{ext} + R_a) \) ↓ \( E_b \) → ↓ \( N \) Problem: High copper loss (\( I_a \) is large in series motor) Requires multiple voltage source Therefore: rarely used — large \( I^2 R \) dissipation

⑥ Multiple Voltage Control (Series Motor)

By changing the supply voltage from a set of multiple DC sources, speed can be controlled in discrete steps. This method avoids the resistance losses of ARCC but requires a multiple-voltage DC supply infrastructure, which is expensive. It is most practically implemented as traction motors in series and parallel — used extensively in electric railway locomotives and trams.[3]

Fig 2.8 — Traction motor speed control: Series connection → each motor gets V/2 → lower speed (for starting, acceleration). Parallel connection → each motor gets full V → higher running speed. This is how electric trams and older train locomotives controlled speed.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §7.8, p.362; Chapman [1], §8.5

GATE & IES Previous Year Concepts

EXAMPLE 2.1 Shunt Motor Voltage Halved — What Happens to Speed?
A shunt motor is running at rated speed 1000 rpm with rated voltage V. If the voltage becomes half (V/2), what will be the new speed? Choose: (1) 1000 rpm (2) 500 rpm (3) 250 rpm (4) 2000 rpm
Solution — Step by Step

The speed ratio formula is: N₂/N₁ = (Eb₂/Eb₁) × (\( \phi \)₁/\( \phi \)₂)

Key analysis: When V becomes V/2, the shunt field winding (\( R_{sh} \)) is also connected across the supply. So the field current \( I_{sh} \) = V/\( R_{sh} \) also halves → \( \phi \)₂ = \( \phi \)₁/2.

Also: Since \( I_a \)\( R_a \) is small compared to V, we can approximate Eb ≈ V. Therefore Eb₂ ≈ V/2 = Eb₁/2.

Calculation
N₂/N₁ = (Eb₂/Eb₁) × (ϕ₁/ϕ₂) = (V/2 / V) × (ϕ₁ / ϕ₁/2) = (1/2) × (2) = 1 Therefore: N₂ = N₁ = 1000 RPM ✓ Answer: Option (1) Note: If flux were constant (field separately excited), then: N₂ = N₁ × (Eb₂/Eb₁) = 1000 × (1/2) = 500 RPM → Case ②
💡
Key Distinction — GATE Frequently Asks

For a self-excited shunt motor: halving voltage halves both Eb AND \( \phi \) → speed stays same. For a separately excited shunt motor (or if only armature voltage is halved, field kept rated): halving voltage halves Eb, \( \phi \) constant → speed halves to 500 rpm. Always check whether the motor is self-excited or separately excited!

GATE EE Speed Control of DC Shunt Motor — Method Classification GATE 2016

In a DC shunt motor, the speed above rated value can be obtained by:

  • (A) Increasing armature resistance
  • (B) Decreasing field current (field weakening)
  • (C) Increasing supply voltage beyond rated
  • (D) Decreasing armature voltage

Explanation: Field weakening (decreasing field current by increasing field resistance) reduces \( \phi \). Since N ∝ Eb/\( \phi \) and Eb ≈ V (approximately constant), speed increases above rated. This is the only standard method to achieve speeds above rated in a shunt motor without exceeding rated voltage. Armature resistance control (A) gives speeds below rated. (C) would exceed insulation rating. (D) reduces speed.

GATE EE Ward–Leonard Method — Characteristic Feature GATE 2019

The Ward–Leonard method of speed control of DC motors is preferred over the resistance control method because:

  • (A) It uses less number of machines
  • (B) It requires less capital cost
  • (C) It gives smooth stepless speed control with high efficiency in both directions
  • (D) It does not require a separately excited generator

Explanation: The Ward–Leonard system uses a variable-output generator to supply variable voltage to the motor armature. Since no external resistance is added in the armature circuit, there is no I²R loss — the system is highly efficient. Speed can be controlled smoothly (stepless) from zero to rated, in both forward and reverse directions, by varying the generator excitation using the cross-over switch. The disadvantage is cost (3 machines required).

IES E&T Series Motor — Field Diverter vs Armature Diverter IES 2018

In a DC series motor, connecting a resistance in parallel with the field winding (field diverter) compared to a resistance in parallel with the armature (armature diverter):

  • (A) Both give the same speed change direction
  • (B) Field diverter increases speed; armature diverter decreases speed
  • (C) Field diverter decreases speed; armature diverter increases speed
  • (D) Both always decrease the speed

Explanation: Field diverter bypasses the field winding → \( I_{se} \) ↓ → \( \phi \) ↓ → N ↑ (speed increases above rated). Armature diverter bypasses the armature → \( I_a \) ↓ → to maintain torque, motor draws more current through field → \( \phi \) ↑ → N ↓ (speed decreases below rated). The two methods thus produce opposite effects on speed.

GATE EE Constant Power vs Constant Torque Region GATE 2022

For a DC shunt motor operating above rated speed (field weakening region), the operation is best characterised as:

  • (A) Constant torque, variable power
  • (B) Variable torque, constant power
  • (C) Constant torque, constant power
  • (D) Variable torque, variable power

Explanation: In the field weakening region (above rated speed), the armature current is limited to rated value (\( I_a \) = \( I_a \)_rated) to protect the windings. Since \( \phi \) decreases as speed increases, and T = k\( \phi \)\( I_a \), torque decreases proportionally with flux. Power P = T × ω ≈ \( E_b \) × \( I_a \) ≈ V × \( I_a \)_rated = constant (since V and \( I_a \) are both rated). Therefore the field weakening zone is a constant power, variable torque operation — same as a constant-power drive in modern industrial terminology.

CONCEPT Why Field Resistance Must Be at Minimum During Starting

During starting, the back-EMF \( E_b \) = 0. The armature current is limited by the starter resistance. For proper starting torque and to ensure the hold-in coil of the 3-point starter remains energised, the field current should be maximum at start. Maximum field current requires minimum field resistance (\( R_{ext} \) = 0 in field circuit). If field resistance is high at start: (i) starting torque is low (T = k\( \phi \)\( I_a \), \( \phi \) small), (ii) in a 3-point starter, the hold-in coil may release the starter arm prematurely, causing the full supply voltage to appear across the armature — potentially damaging the machine.

Similarly, the armature rheostat (for ARCC) must be at maximum resistance position during starting to limit starting current. These two starting conditions (field \( R_{ext} \) = min; armature \( R_{ext} \) = max) are opposite and are a very frequent GATE question.

EXAMPLE 2.2 Series Motor — Speed with Field Diverter
A DC series motor has a field winding resistance of 0.5 Ω and a field diverter of 1 Ω connected in parallel. If the rated line current is 20 A, find the field current and comment on speed change.
Solution

The parallel combination of Rse = 0.5 Ω and \( R_d \) = 1 Ω has an equivalent resistance of Req = (0.5 × 1)/(0.5 + 1) = 0.5/1.5 = 0.333 Ω.

Voltage across parallel combination = \( I_L \) × Req = 20 × 0.333 = 6.67 V.

Field current \( I_{se} \) = 6.67/0.5 = 13.3 A (instead of full 20 A without diverter).

Analysis
Without diverter: \( I_{se} \) = \( I_L \) = 20 A → rated flux \( \phi \)_rated With diverter: \( I_{se} \) = 13.3 A → \( \phi \) reduced to 13.3/20 = 66.5% of rated Since N ∝ \( E_b \)/\( \phi \), and \( E_b \) ≈ constant: N ∝ 1/0.665 → speed increases by ~50% above rated Conclusion: Field diverter significantly increases series motor speed.

Chapter Summary

Speed Equation

N ∝ \( E_b \)/\( \phi \) ∝ (V−\( I_a \)\( R_a \))/\( \phi \). Three handles: vary \( \phi \) (field), \( R_a \) (external resistance), or V (supply voltage). Each gives a different speed range and torque-power characteristic.

Field Weakening (FLOSC)

Shunt motor: \( R_{ext} \) in field circuit → \( I_{sh} \)↓ → \( \phi \)↓ → N↑. Speeds above rated only. Constant power, variable torque. 4-point starter needed. Most efficient shunt speed control.

Arm. Resistance (ARCC)

\( R_{ext} \) in armature circuit → Eb↓ → N↓. Below rated only. Constant torque, variable power. Inefficient (large I²R loss). Cooling required. Simple but wasteful.

Ward–Leonard

Variable DC generator drives motor. No armature \( R_{ext} \) losses — efficient. Smooth bidirectional speed control. 3 machines required — expensive. Used for large drives (rolling mills, cranes).

Series — Field Diverter

\( R_d \) ∥ field winding. \( I_{se} \)↓ → \( \phi \)↓ → N↑ (above rated). \( R_d \) never zero (runaway). Series — Armature Diverter: \( R_d \) ∥ armature. \(I_a\)↓ → motor draws more \( I_L \) → \( \phi \)↑ → N↓ (below rated).

Series — Other Methods

Tapped field: change \( N_{se} \) → discrete steps. Paralleling coils: switch series/parallel coil sections. Traction motors: series/parallel switching for different voltage steps.
