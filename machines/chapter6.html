§ 01

What is Armature Reaction?

In a DC machine at no load, the armature current is essentially zero (negligible magnetomotive force from the armature). The only flux present is the main field flux (\(\phi_f\)) produced by the field winding, which is uniformly distributed in the air gap in a flat-topped (trapezoidal) pattern. The Geometric Neutral Axis (GNA) and the Magnetic Neutral Axis (MNA) coincide with each other at this condition.

When the machine is loaded, current flows in the armature conductors. This armature current produces its own MMF — called the armature MMF (\(F_a\)) — and its own flux — called the armature flux (\(\phi_a\)). This armature flux is distributed uniformly throughout the armature periphery in the air gap and is directed along the brush axis (GNA direction).

💡
Definition — Armature Reaction

Armature Reaction is the effect (action) of the armature flux on the main field flux distribution. It distorts and weakens the main field, shifting the MNA from its no-load position. — Kothari & Nagrath, §5.10

Fig. 1 — No-load (left): uniform \(\phi_f\), GNA = MNA. On-load (right): armature flux \(\phi_a\) distorts the main field; MNA shifts in the direction of rotation for a generator.
📌
Key Physical Insight

The main field flux (\(\phi_f\)) and the armature flux (\(\phi_a\)) are always perpendicular (in space quadrature / \(90^\circ\) electrical) to each other. \(\phi_f\) is along the polar axis; \(\phi_a\) is along the brush axis (GNA). This orthogonality is what gives rise to cross-magnetisation. — Chapman §7.6

§ 02

Cross-Magnetisation & Demagnetisation

The armature flux has two important effects on the main field, collectively called armature reaction effects:

① Cross-Magnetisation (CM)
  • ▸The component of armature flux perpendicular to the main field axis.
  • ▸This is the dominant effect when brushes are on the GNA.
  • ▸It distorts the main flux distribution — increasing it under one pole tip and decreasing it under the other.
  • ▸Shifts the MNA in the direction of rotation (for generator) or against rotation (for motor).
  • ▸Leads to sparking at the commutator because coils being commutated have residual flux linkage.
  • ▸\(\phi_c\) = \(\phi_a\)cos\(\theta\) (component for cross-mag)
② Demagnetisation (DM)
  • ▸The component of armature flux directly opposing the main field axis.
  • ▸Occurs only when the brushes are shifted from GNA by an angle \(\theta\).
  • ▸Reduces the net main flux, thereby reducing the induced EMF and terminal voltage.
  • ▸\(\phi_d\) = \(\phi_a\)sin\(\theta\) (component for demag)
  • ▸When \(\theta\) = \(0^\circ\): \(\phi_d\) = 0, \(\phi_c\) = \(\phi_a\) → pure cross-magnetisation
  • ▸When \(\theta\) = \(90^\circ\): \(\phi_d\) = \(\phi_a\), \(\phi_c\) = 0 → pure demagnetisation

Phasor / Vector Diagram of Armature Reaction

Fig. 2 — Phasor resolution of armature flux \(\phi_a\) into cross-magnetising component \(\phi_c\) and demagnetising component \(\phi_d\). Left: Brushes on GNA (\(\theta\)=0) → pure cross-magnetisation. Right: Brushes shifted by \(\theta\) → both CM and DM components exist.
After Chapman §7.6, Kothari & Nagrath §5.10 [1,2]

§ 03

Flux Distributions & Field Distortion

To understand armature reaction graphically, we superimpose the main field distribution and the armature MMF distribution and study the resultant net flux.

📐
Three Key Waveforms (Kothari & Nagrath §5.11)

① Main field flux distribution — Flat-topped (trapezoidal) under the poles; zero in the inter-polar gap.   ② Armature MMF — Triangular (sawtooth) in shape; increases linearly from MNA and peaks at the brush axis.   ③ Net (resultant) flux — Obtained by adding the two; peaky under one pole tip, dipped under the other. The MNA shifts.

Fig. 3 — Superposition of main field flux (trapezoidal), armature MMF (triangular), and resultant net flux (distorted). The net flux peaks under the trailing pole tip and is depressed under the leading pole tip. MNA shifts forward (generator rotation direction). Due to saturation, the peak increase < the dip reduction, causing net demagnetisation.
After Kothari & Nagrath §5.11, Chapman §7.6 [1,2]

⚠️
Magnetic Saturation — The Critical Effect

Under practical conditions, the pole tips are magnetically saturated. Consequently, the increase in flux density under the trailing pole tip is comparatively less than the decrease under the leading pole tip. This asymmetry causes an overall (net) reduction in the main flux — a net demagnetisation effect — even when there is theoretically no brush shift. — Kothari & Nagrath §5.11

§ 04

Effects of Armature Reaction

Armature reaction produces three major adverse effects in DC machines, affecting performance, reliability, and machine size:

① Decreased Efficiency
  • ▸Increased flux density under pole tips → increased iron (core) losses
  • ▸Iron loss ∝ B² (hysteresis) and B (eddy current ∝ B²), so higher flux density at tips directly increases losses
  • ▸Overall efficiency of the machine decreases
② Increased Maintenance & Repair
  • ▸Cross-magnetisation shifts MNA from GNA; commutation occurs in a zone where residual flux exists
  • ▸Current reversal is not complete → sparking at commutator brushes
  • ▸Sparking damages brush surface and commutator segments → frequent maintenance
③ Increased Design Cost
  • ▸To overcome armature reaction, the machine must be designed with a stronger field system
  • ▸Methods like compensating windings add to machine complexity and cost
  • ▸Additional insulation required at high-flux regions
§ 05

Brush Shift & Additional Demagnetisation

Due to cross-magnetisation, the MNA is shifted from the GNA in the direction of rotation for a generator (and against rotation for a motor). To achieve successful commutation (sparkless), the brushes must lie on the MNA. Therefore, the brushes must also be shifted in the direction of rotation for a generator.

⚡
Problem with Brush Shift

When brushes are shifted by angle \(\theta\) from GNA, a portion of the armature conductors that were previously under the cross-magnetising zone now come under the demagnetising zone. This creates additional demagnetisation (ATd), further reducing the main flux and terminal voltage. — Chapman §7.7

Fig. 4 — Effect of brush shift by \(\theta\). The DM zone (shaded red) lies between GNA and the new brush axis (MNA). Conductors in this zone produce a demagnetising component \(\phi_d\) = \(\phi_a\)sin\(\theta\) opposing the main field. The CM zone (green) is the remaining armature arc under the poles.
After Chapman §7.7, Kothari & Nagrath §5.11 [1,2]
✅
Why Brush Shift is Abandoned

The MNA shifts continuously with load (as armature current and thus armature MMF changes with load). After design, the brushes cannot be dynamically repositioned. Moreover, brush shift causes additional demagnetisation and it is an unreliable method. It has been completely replaced by interpoles (commutating poles) in modern DC machines. — Fitzgerald §7.6

§ 06

Amp-Turn Calculations — De-mag & Cross-mag

In order to quantify the armature reaction effects, we calculate the demagnetising amp-turns per pole (\(AT_{d/p}\)) and cross-magnetising amp-turns per pole (\(AT_{c/p}\)).

Demagnetising AT/pole — \(AT_{d/p}\)

The conductors lying within the demagnetising zone (of angular spread \(2\theta\) at the brush axis, total angle 2×\(2\theta\) = \(4\theta\) per armature circumference but split per pole) produce the demagnetising mmf. Using the geometry of the brush shift angle \(\theta\) (in electrical degrees):

Demagnetising Amp-Turns per Pole Fraction of armature subtending DM zone per pole = \(2\theta\) / \(180^\circ\) \(AT_{d/p}\) = (\(2\theta\) / \(180^\circ\)) × (\(I_a\) / A) × (Z / 2P) = (\(2\theta\) / \(180^\circ\)) × (\(I_a\) × Z) / (A × 2P) Where: \(\theta\) = brush shift angle (electrical degrees) \(I_a\) = armature current (A) Z = total armature conductors A = number of parallel paths P = number of poles

Cross-Magnetising AT/pole — \(AT_{c/p}\)

Cross-Magnetising Amp-Turns per Pole Total armature MMF per pole = (\(I_a\)/A) × (Z/2P) = \(I_a\)·Z / (A·2P) \(AT_{c/p}\) = Total AT/pole − \(AT_{d/p}\) = [(\(180^\circ\) − \(2\theta\)) / \(180^\circ\)] × (\(I_a\) × Z) / (A × 2P) Total armature MMF per pole (formula) \(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\) = \(I_a\) × Z / (A × 2P)
📝
Important Note for GATE/IES

When brushes are on the GNA (\(\theta\) = \(0^\circ\)): \(AT_{d/p}\) = 0, \(AT_{c/p}\) = \(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\). When \(\theta\) = \(90^\circ\) (theoretical maximum brush shift): \(AT_{d/p}\) = \(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\), \(AT_{c/p}\) = 0. In GATE problems, always check whether \(\theta\) is given in mechanical degrees or electrical degrees. Electrical degrees = Mechanical degrees × (P/2).

EXAMPLE 6.1 Demagnetising and Cross-Magnetising AT/pole Calculation
A 4-pole DC generator has 360 armature conductors, lap wound, carrying a total armature current of 80 A. The brushes are shifted by 1\(0^\circ\) (mechanical) from the GNA. Calculate: (a) Total armature MMF/pole, (b) Demagnetising AT/pole, (c) Cross-magnetising AT/pole.
Solution

Given: P = 4, Z = 360, \(I_a\) = 80 A, Lap winding → A = P = 4, \(\theta_{\text{mech}}\) = 1\(0^\circ\)

Step 1: Convert \(\theta\) to electrical degrees
\(\theta_{\text{elect}}\) = \(\theta_{\text{mech}}\) × (P/2) = 1\(0^\circ\) × (4/2) = 1\(0^\circ\) × 2 = 2\(0^\circ\) electrical

Step 2: Total armature AT/pole
\(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\) = (\(I_a\) × Z) / (A × 2P) = (80 × 360) / (4 × 2×4) = 28800 / 32 = 900 AT/pole

Step 3: Demagnetising AT/pole
\(AT_{d/p}\) = (\(2\theta\) / \(180^\circ\)) × 900 = (2×2\(0^\circ\) / \(180^\circ\)) × 900 = (40/180) × 900 = 200 AT/pole

Step 4: Cross-magnetising AT/pole
\(AT_{c/p}\) = \(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\) − \(AT_{d/p}\) = 900 − 200 = 700 AT/pole

✔
Check

\(AT_{d/p}\) + \(AT_{c/p}\) = 200 + 700 = 900 = \(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\) ✓

§ 08

Compensating Winding ★

The compensating winding (CW) is the most effective and complete method of eliminating armature reaction under the pole face. It is used in large-rated DC machines operating at varying loads and high speeds where armature reaction effects are severe.

🔧
Principle of Compensating Winding (Kothari & Nagrath §5.12)

Conductors are placed in slots cut into the pole face (pole shoe). The CW is always connected in series with the armature winding through the brushes so that it automatically carries the same armature current. The current direction in the CW conductors is exactly opposite to the armature conductor current direction under the pole, so that the MMF of the CW exactly cancels the armature MMF point-by-point under the pole.

Fig. 6 — Compensating winding (CW) placed in pole face slots. CW conductors carry current in the opposite direction to armature conductors beneath them, producing an MMF that exactly neutralises the armature MMF under the pole. Connected in series with armature through brushes for automatic compensation at all loads.
After Chapman §7.8, Kothari & Nagrath §5.12 [1,2]
Compensating Winding — Key Formulae Total CW conductors: \(Z_c\) = Z / A CW conductors per pole: \(Z_c\) = Z / (A × P) Including pole arc/pitch factor: \(Z_c\) = (Z / A·P) × (pole arc / pole pitch) Typical pole arc/pole pitch: ≈ 0.7 (70%) Series connection ensures: CW current = \(I_a\) (armature current) at all loads → Automatic, load-independent neutralisation
⚠️
CW Limitation — GATE/IES Point

The compensating winding only neutralises armature reaction under the pole face (pole arc region, ~70% of pole pitch). It does NOT neutralise armature reaction in the inter-polar region (GNA zone). Interpoles handle the inter-polar region. This is why both CW and interpoles may be used together in critical large machines.

§ 09

Commutation — Theory & Types

Commutation is the process of reversal of current in an armature coil as the coil passes through the brush (short-circuit) zone. This process is essential for the DC machine to produce or receive unidirectional (DC) current at its external terminals.

⏱
Commutation Period — \(T_c\)

The time taken for the brush to span from one commutator segment to the adjacent segment is called the commutation period \(T_c\). During this time, the current in the coil undergoing commutation must reverse from +\(I_a\)/A to −\(I_a\)/A (a total change of 2\(I_a\)/A). — Chapman §7.8

Fig. 7 — Current reversal during commutation. Ideal (linear/straight-line) commutation is sparkless. Delayed commutation means current hasn't fully reversed by the end of \(T_c\) → spark at trailing edge of brush. Accelerated (over-commutation) means current reverses too fast → spark at leading edge.
After Chapman §7.8, Kothari & Nagrath §5.13 [1,2]

§ 10

Reactance Voltage — The Root Cause of Sparking

During commutation, the coil undergoes a total current change of 2\(I_a\)/A in commutation time \(T_c\). By Faraday's law and Lenz's law, the self-inductance of the coil opposes this rapid change in current, generating a reactance voltage (\(e_r\)) that tries to maintain the original current direction.

Reactance Voltage (Self-Induced EMF during Commutation) \(e_r\) = N · d\(\phi\)/dt = L · di/dt = L · (2·\(I_a\)/A) / \(T_c\) Where: L = self-inductance of the coil undergoing commutation 2Ia/A = total change in current during commutation \(T_c\) = commutation time (= time for brush to span one segment) Effect: \(e_r\) opposes current reversal → delayed commutation → sparking The remaining unreversed current jumps through the spark at trailing edge
⚡
Consequence of Sparking

Any unreversed current at the end of commutation time \(T_c\) must jump across the air gap between the brush and the leaving commutator segment, causing a spark at the trailing edge of the brush. This spark erodes both the brush and commutator surface, shortening their life and causing electromagnetic interference. — Kothari & Nagrath §5.13

§ 11

Methods to Improve Commutation

Three methods are used to improve (idealize) commutation by either reducing the reactance voltage or neutralising it with an opposing EMF:

① Resistance Commutation (Carbon Brushes)

By replacing low-resistance copper brushes with high-resistance carbon (graphite) brushes, the short-circuit current through the commutating coil is limited. The higher brush contact resistance forces the current to transfer more smoothly between segments, reducing the probability of sparking at the trailing edge.

Advantages of Carbon Brushes
  • ▸Not as hard as copper → less damage to commutator if spark occurs
  • ▸Self-lubricating (polishing) in nature → good mechanical condition with commutator surface
  • ▸High resistance reduces sparking tendency at trailing edge
  • ▸Spark damage is less catastrophic than with Cu brushes
Disadvantages of Carbon Brushes
  • ▸Higher brush contact voltage drop (≈1–2V vs ~0.2V for Cu)
  • ▸Low current density → requires larger brush cross-section
  • ▸Resistance commutation only partially improves commutation
  • ▸Does not eliminate the fundamental reactance voltage problem

② Voltage Commutation — Brush Shift (Obsolete)

By shifting the brushes forward (in the direction of rotation for a generator), the commutating coil enters a zone of field flux which induces an EMF in the coil in the opposite direction to the reactance voltage. If this induced EMF exactly equals the reactance voltage, ideal commutation is achieved.

However, this method introduces additional demagnetisation (as described in §5), and the MNA changes continuously with load, making the brush position design-dependent and unreliable. It is completely obsolete in modern machines.

③ Voltage Commutation — Interpoles (Modern Standard)

This is the universally adopted method. See Section 12 for complete coverage. Interpoles produce a local flux in the inter-polar (commutation) zone that induces an EMF in the commutating coil exactly equal and opposite to the reactance voltage, achieving ideal commutation. Unlike brush shift, interpoles are series connected and automatically adjust with load current.

§ 12

Interpoles (Commutating Poles)

Interpoles (also called commutating poles or compoles) are small auxiliary poles placed midway between the main poles — i.e., in the inter-polar region (GNA zone). They are tapered in shape and have comparatively larger air gaps to avoid easy magnetic saturation.

Fig. 8 — A 4-pole DC machine showing interpoles (ip) placed in the inter-polar gaps at the GNA positions. Interpoles are tapered and electromagnets with their winding connected in series with the armature for automatic load compensation.
After Chapman §7.8, Kothari & Nagrath §5.14 [1,2]

Two Functions of Interpoles

① Neutralise Reactance Voltage
  • ▸Produce a counter-flux in the commutating zone equal and opposite to the reactance voltage flux
  • ▸The commutating coil moves through this interpole flux, inducing an EMF that exactly cancels the reactance voltage
  • ▸Net voltage in commutating coil = 0 → ideal (linear) commutation
  • ▸Directly improves commutation → no sparking
② Remove Flux Inequality
  • ▸Neutralise armature flux in the inter-polar (GNA) region
  • ▸Remove the inequality in flux density in the top and bottom conductor regions of armature
  • ▸Indirectly assists in reducing cross-magnetisation effects near the commutation zone
  • ▸CW reduces arm. reaction under pole; Interpole handles inter-polar region

📌
Interpole vs Compensating Winding — GATE Key Point

Interpoles directly improve commutation by nullifying the reactance voltage. Compensating windings reduce armature reaction under the pole face (indirectly improving commutation by reducing flux distortion). They address different spatial regions and are sometimes used together in large machines. The CW improves commutation indirectly; interpoles improve it directly.

  • (A) Reduce the flux in the leading pole tip region and increase in the trailing pole tip region

  • (D) Both reduce uniformly

Correct Answer: (A) — Key Explanation

For a generator rotating in the direction shown, the trailing pole tip sees the armature flux aiding the main flux (same direction) → flux density increases. The leading pole tip sees armature flux opposing main flux → flux density decreases. Due to saturation, the net effect is an overall reduction in flux (demagnetisation).

GATE EE Cross-Magnetising AT/pole Calculation 2016

A 6-pole DC machine has 540 armature conductors, wave wound (A = 2), armature current 60 A, brushes shifted by 6° mechanical. Calculate the demagnetising AT/pole.

🔢

\(\theta\)elec = 6° × (P/2) = 6° × 3 = 18°

\(AT_{\text{total}}/\text{pole}\) = (\(I_a\) × Z) / (A × 2P) = (60 × 540) / (2 × 12) = 32400 / 24 = 1350 AT/pole

\(AT_{d/p}\) = (\(2\theta\)/\(180^\circ\)) × 1350 = (36/180) × 1350 = 0.2 × 1350 = 270 AT/pole

\(AT_{c/p}\) = 1350 − 270 = 1080 AT/pole

The reactance voltage in a commutating coil of a DC machine is proportional to:

  • (B) Inversely proportional to armature current and directly to commutation time

§ 14

Chapter Summary

Armature Reaction

Effect of armature flux on main field. On-load: arm. MMF (brush-axis) distorts main field. Main & arm. flux always \(90^\circ\)E (orthogonal). MNA shifts from GNA.

Cross-Magnetisation

\(\phi_c\) = \(\phi_a\)cos\(\theta\). Dominant effect when brushes on GNA. Distorts flux — trailing tip increases, leading tip decreases. Causes sparking; MNA shifts direction of rotation (gen).

Demagnetisation

\(\phi_d\) = \(\phi_a\)sin\(\theta\). Only when brushes shifted. Reduces main flux → reduces EMF & terminal voltage. Net demag also from magnetic saturation even at \(\theta\)=\(0^\circ\).

AT Formulae

Total AT/pole = IaZ/(A·2P). \(AT_{d/p}\) = (\(2\theta\)/\(180^\circ\))×AT_total. \(AT_{c/p}\) = AT_total − ATd. \(\theta\) in electrical degrees. Always convert mechanical → electrical.

Compensating Winding

In pole face slots. Series connected. \(Z_c\) = Z/(A·P) × (pole arc/pole pitch ≈0.7). Neutralises arm. reaction under pole. Does NOT help inter-polar zone.

Commutation

Current reversal in coil as it passes brush. Ideal = linear (no spark). Delayed = not reversed by Tc → spark at trailing edge. Cause: reactance voltage \(e_r\) = L·(2Ia/A)/Tc.

Carbon Brushes

High resistance → limits commutation spark. Self-lubricating. Disadvantage: higher brush drop, larger size required. Partially improves commutation only.

Interpoles

In inter-polar region. Series connected (auto-adjust). Tapered (large air gap). Gen: same polarity as NEXT main pole ahead. Motor: same as PREVIOUS. Directly improves commutation.

CW vs Interpoles

CW → under pole face → indirectly improves comm. IPW → inter-polar zone → directly improves comm. Used together in large, high-speed, variable-load DC machines.
