§ 01

Introduction & Principle of Operation

DC motors are among the most widely used electrical machines in industry because of their highest starting torque, wide range of accurate, simple, and efficient speed control. The same DC machine can be operated either as a generator or as a motor — this is the principle of reversibility of DC machines.[1]

⚡
Core Principle

When a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a mechanical force. The magnitude is given by F = B·i·l Newtons, and the direction by Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motor rule).

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (Motor Rule)

Hold the left hand such that the forefinger points in the direction of the magnetic field (flux), the middle finger in the direction of current, and the thumb gives the direction of the mechanical force (motion) experienced by the conductor.

Fig. 1 — Fleming's Left-Hand Rule: Forefinger = Flux, Middle finger = Current, Thumb = Force (Motion)
[Ref: Chapman §7.1; Kothari §5.1]
Fig. 2 — Basic DC motor: single coil in magnetic field. Current in conductor A is into page (⊗), in B is out of page (⊙). Forces produce clockwise torque. Commutator maintains unidirectional torque.
[Ref: Chapman §7.2; Kothari §5.2]
📌
Role of Commutator in Motor

The function of the commutator in a DC motor is to produce unidirectional torque. It ensures that the current in the conductors under a given pole always flows in the same direction, so the torque is always in the same rotational direction. If the generator rotates clockwise, the motor (with same notation) rotates anticlockwise.

§ 02

Generator Action in Motor — Back EMF

When the armature conductors of a motor rotate in the magnetic field, by Faraday's law, an EMF is induced in them. By applying the right-hand rule (generator rule) to the rotating motor armature, the direction of this induced EMF is found to be exactly opposite to the applied supply voltage. Therefore, it is called back EMF or counter EMF (\(E_b\)).[2]

Back EMF Formula (same as generator EMF formula)\[ E_b = \frac{\phi Z N P}{60 A} \] where: \(\phi\) = flux per pole (Wb) \(Z\) = total number of armature conductors \(N\) = speed of armature (rpm) \(P\) = number of poles \(A\) = number of parallel paths Also: \(E_b \propto \phi N\) (for given machine geometry)
Fig. 3 — DC motor equivalent circuit. V = supply voltage, Eb = back EMF, Ra = armature resistance, \(I_a\) = armature current.
💡
Key Insight — Eb vs V

In a motor, Eb < V always (unlike generator where Eg > V). The difference (V − Eb) drives the armature current \(I_a\) = (V − Eb)/Ra. When load increases → speed falls → Eb falls → \(I_a\) increases → torque increases to meet load. This is the self-regulating nature of back EMF.

Fig. 3b — Energy conversion occurs through opposition: Mechanical opposition in Generator, Electrical opposition (Back EMF) in Motor.

§ 05

Significance of Back EMF

Back EMF plays a fundamentally important role in the operation of DC motors. Understanding it is critical for GATE/IES. The three main significances are:[2][3]

① Electromech. Energy Conversion
  • →Back EMF opposes the applied voltage and limits armature current.
  • →Product \( E_b \cdot I_a \) represents the electrical equivalent of mechanical power developed in the armature: \( P_{\text{mech}} = E_b \cdot I_a \) (watts).
  • →No back EMF = no energy conversion (motor stalls and burns).
② Motor Efficiency
  • →\( \eta = \frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}} = \frac{E_b \cdot I_a}{V \cdot I_a} = \mathbf{\frac{E_b}{V}} \)
  • →Higher \(E_b\) → higher efficiency. Motor is most efficient near full-load speed (\(E_b \approx 0.95V\) typically).
  • →At no load: \(E_b \to V\), \(I_a \to 0\), \(\eta \to 1\) (ideal).
③ Self-Regulating Nature
  • →Load ↑ → Speed ↓ → \(E_b\) ↓ → \(I_a\) ↑ → Torque ↑ (matches load).
  • →Load removed → Speed ↑ → \(E_b\) ↑ → \(I_a\) ↓ → Torque ↓.
  • →Motor automatically reaches new equilibrium speed. \(I_a = \frac{V-E_b}{R_a}\) acts as self-regulator.
Maximum Mechanical Power
  • →\(P_m = V \cdot I_a - I_a^2 \cdot R_a\)
  • →\(dP_m/dt = 0\) gives: \(I_a \cdot R_a = V/2\), so \(E_b = V/2\)
  • →At \(P_{m,\text{max}}\): \(\eta = E_b/V = 1/2 =\) 50%
  • →Motors are not designed for this — designed for max \(\eta\) near rated load.
Power Balance in DC Motor Armature\( V \cdot I_a \) = Power input to armature \( I_a^2 \cdot R_a \) = Copper loss in armature resistance \( E_b \cdot I_a \) = Mechanical power developed (\( P_{\text{mech}} \)) Therefore: \[ P_{\text{mech}} = E_b \cdot I_a = V \cdot I_a - I_a^2 \cdot R_a \] Shaft output: \( P_{\text{shaft}} = P_{\text{mech}} - \text{Rotational losses (iron + friction & windage)} \) Rotational losses → iron losses + mechanical losses (\( = \text{lost torque} \times \omega \))
§ 06

Torque Equations

Fig. 10a — Torque is the twisting moment produced by force F acting at radius r.

Torque is the turning or twisting moment of force about an axis, expressed in N·m. For a DC motor, the electromagnetic (armature) torque Ta is derived from the power developed in the armature:[1][2]

Derivation of Torque EquationPower developed in arm: \( P = E_b \cdot I_a = \frac{2\pi N T}{60} \) (watts) \( E_b = \frac{\phi Z N P}{60 A} \) → Substitute: \( I_a \times \frac{\phi Z N P}{60 A} = \frac{2\pi N T}{60} \) \( T = \frac{\phi Z P}{2\pi A} \times I_a \) \[ T_a = \frac{1}{2\pi} \times \left(\frac{\phi Z P}{A}\right) \times I_a \quad [\text{N}\cdot\text{m}] \] \[ T_a \propto \phi \cdot I_a \quad \text{(for given machine geometry Z, P, A are constant)} \] Torque ratio: \[ \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{\phi_2}{\phi_1}\right) \times \left(\frac{I_{a2}}{I_{a1}}\right) \]
⚠️
Series vs Shunt Torque Ratio

Shunt motor (\(\phi\) constant): T2/T1 = Ia2/Ia1 (linear)
Series motor (\(\phi\) ∝ \(I_a\), below saturation): T2/T1 = (Ia2/Ia1)² — parabolic relationship!
Series motor (above saturation, \(\phi\) ≈ constant): T2/T1 = Ia2/Ia1 — reverts to linear. This is why series motors have highest starting torque and are used in traction.

Shaft Torque (Tsh) vs Armature Torque (Ta)

The armature torque Ta is not fully available at the shaft. Part is consumed to overcome iron losses and mechanical friction/windage losses (together called lost torque or stray losses).

Torque relationships\( T_a \) = Armature torque (electromagnetic torque) \( T_{sh} \) = Shaft torque (useful output torque) \[ T_a - T_{sh} = \text{Lost torque (due to iron + mechanical losses)} \] Power output = \( \frac{2\pi N \cdot T_{sh}}{60} \) (watts) Power developed = \( \frac{2\pi N \cdot T_a}{60} = E_b \cdot I_a \) (watts)
Fig. 10 — Power flow diagram in a DC motor
§ 07

Speed Equation of DC Motor

From the back EMF formula, the speed of a DC motor can be expressed as:[1][2]

Speed EquationSince \(E_b = \frac{\phi Z N P}{60A}\) → \(N = \frac{E_b \times 60A}{\phi Z P}\) For a given machine: \(Z\), \(P\), \(A\) are constants, so: \[ N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} = \frac{V - I_a R_a}{\phi} \] Speed ratio: \[ \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \left(\frac{E_{b2}}{E_{b1}}\right) \times \left(\frac{\phi_1}{\phi_2}\right) \] If flux is constant (shunt motor): \[ \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{E_{b2}}{E_{b1}} \]
📌
Speed Control Methods (GATE important)

Speed N ∝ (V − \(I_a\)·Ra)/\(\phi\). Three ways to control speed:
1. Armature resistance control — add external resistance in armature circuit (reduces speed below base speed, wasteful).
2. Field flux control — reduce \(\phi\) by weakening field (increases speed above base speed, used in shunt/compound motors).
3. Armature voltage control — vary V (Ward-Leonard method, modern: chopper/inverter control). Most efficient.

🚫
CRITICAL — Never Start Series Motor at No Load!

At no load: \(I_a\) → 0 → \(\phi\)se → 0 → N → ∞ (dangerously high speed). The series motor can reach speeds that mechanically destroy it. Series motors must always be directly coupled to the load — never use belt drives (belt may slip off → no-load runaway).

§ 08

Motor Characteristics

Three important characteristics for each motor type: (1) Torque vs \(I_a\), (2) Speed vs \(I_a\), (3) Speed vs Torque. The last two have the same shape.[1][2]

① Shunt Motor Characteristics

Fig. 11 — Shunt motor: T vs \(I_a\) is linear (T ∝ \(I_a\), \(\phi\) constant). Speed vs \(I_a\) is nearly flat — "constant speed motor" (N drops slightly from N₀ due to IaRa drop).
✅
Shunt Motor Properties

Shunt motors have excellent speed regulation; they are approximately constant-speed motors. They have low-to-medium starting torque. Best suited for manufacturing applications like steel rolling, Al rolling, lathes, machine tools. The characteristics of shunt motors are nearly identical to induction motors, but shunt motors are preferred for superior speed control.

② Series Motor Characteristics

Since \(\phi\) ∝ \(I_a\) (before saturation), torque T ∝ \(\phi\)·\(I_a\) ∝ Ia². This gives a parabolic T–\(I_a\) curve. The speed drops sharply as load increases (hyperbolic N–\(I_a\) curve).

Fig. 12 — Series motor: T ∝ Ia² below saturation (parabolic), speed drops hyperbolically with load. Highest starting torque of all DC motors.

③ Compound Motor Characteristics (Cumulative)

§ 09

Speed Regulation

Speed regulation (SR) is defined as the change in speed when the full load across the motor is disconnected (i.e., from full load to no load), expressed as a percentage of rated (full load) speed.[2]

Speed Regulation Formula\[ \%SR = \frac{N_0 - N}{N} \times 100 \] where: \(N_0\) = no-load speed (rpm) \(N\) = full-load (rated) speed (rpm) Lower SR → better speed regulation (more constant speed motor) Shunt motor: Best SR (≈ 5–10%) Series motor: Very poor SR (speed varies widely with load) Differential: Negative SR — speed rises with load (unstable, no practical use)
🎯
Generator Action in Motor — Important Contrast (GATE PYQ)

For generator: Eg > V, \(I_a\) = (Eg−V)/Ra, mechanical input → electrical output.
For motor: Eb < V, \(I_a\) = (V−Eb)/Ra, electrical input → mechanical output.
At rated load, motor has: EbIa = electrical equivalent of mech. power developed; VIL = total electrical power input. VIL − EbIa = total copper loss.

§ 10

GATE & IES Previous Year Questions

GATE EE The back EMF of a DC motor is proportional to: GATE 2019
  • (a) Armature current only
  • (b) Speed × Flux (\(\phi\)N)
  • (c) Speed only
  • (d) Flux only
✅
Solution

Eb = \(\phi\)ZNP/60A. Since Z, P, A are machine constants: Eb ∝ \(\phi\)N. Answer (b).

GATE EE A DC motor has Ra = 0.5 Ω, V = 200 V, Eb = 180 V. Find armature current \(I_a\). GATE 2018
  • (a) 400 A
  • (b) 40 A
  • (c) 20 A
  • (d) 360 A
✅
Solution

\(I_a\) = (V − Eb)/Ra = (200 − 180)/0.5 = 20/0.5 = 40 A. Answer (b).

IES E&T The condition for maximum mechanical power developed in a DC motor is: IES 2020
  • (a) Eb = V
  • (b) Eb = V/2
  • (c) \(I_a\)·Ra = Eb
  • (d) \(I_a\)·Ra = 0
✅
Solution

Pm = Eb·\(I_a\) = VIa − Ia²Ra. dPm/dIa = 0 → V = 2IaRa → IaRa = V/2. Since V = Eb + IaRa → Eb = V/2. Under this condition η = Eb/V = 50%. Answer (b).

GATE EE A 4-pole, 220V DC shunt motor has armature resistance 0.5Ω. At no load it runs at 1200 rpm with armature current 4A. At full load, armature current = 40A. Find the full-load speed. Assume flux is constant. GATE 2022

Given: V=220V, Ra=0.5Ω, N₁=1200rpm, Ia1=4A, Ia2=40A, \(\phi\)=constant

✅
Step-by-step Solution

Eb1 = V − Ia1·Ra = 220 − 4×0.5 = 220 − 2 = 218 V
Eb2 = V − Ia2·Ra = 220 − 40×0.5 = 220 − 20 = 200 V
Since \(\phi\) constant: N2/N1 = Eb2/Eb1
N2 = 1200 × (200/218) = 1200 × 0.9174 ≈ 1101 rpm

IES E&T Why is a series motor preferred over a shunt motor for electric traction? IES 2016
✅
Answer

The series motor is preferred for traction because:
1. T ∝ Ia² → very high starting torque at low speed (exactly what traction needs for starting heavy loads).
2. Speed automatically decreases as load increases — ideal for constant power characteristic of traction.
3. Regenerative braking is possible (act as series generator when decelerating).
Shunt motors have low starting torque and nearly constant speed — unsuitable for widely varying traction loads.

GATE CONCEPT Differentially Compounded Motor — Why Unstable?

In a differentially compounded motor: T ∝ (\(\phi\)sh − \(\phi\)se)·\(I_a\). As load (\(I_a\)) increases, \(\phi\)se increases, so net flux (\(\phi\)sh − \(\phi\)se) decreases. This means torque can actually decrease as \(I_a\) increases — creating unstable torque–speed characteristics. Speed regulation is negative (speed rises with load). This makes it impractical. The motor may even reverse direction at starting if series flux dominates shunt flux during the large starting current.

Solved Numerical Examples

EX 01 Back EMF, Torque, and Power in a Shunt Motor
A 240V DC shunt motor has Ra = 0.4Ω, Rsh = 200Ω. At full load, IL = 30A. Find: (i) back EMF Eb, (ii) armature copper loss, (iii) mechanical power developed.
Solution

Shunt current: Ish = V/Rsh = 240/200 = 1.2 A

Armature current: \(I_a\) = IL − Ish = 30 − 1.2 = 28.8 A

(i) Back EMF: Eb = V − \(I_a\)·Ra = 240 − 28.8 × 0.4 = 240 − 11.52 = 228.48 V

(ii) Armature Cu loss: Ia²Ra = (28.8)² × 0.4 = 829.44 × 0.4 = 331.8 W

(iii) Mechanical power developed: Pmech = Eb·\(I_a\) = 228.48 × 28.8 = 6580 W ≈ 6.58 kW

Check: \(V \cdot I_a = 240 \times 28.8 = 6912 \text{ W} = P_{\text{mech}} + \text{Cu loss} = 6580 + 332 \checkmark\)
EX 02 Speed of DC Series Motor Under Different Loads
A DC series motor runs at 800 rpm with a line current of 20A when connected to 250V supply. Ra = 0.3Ω, Rse = 0.2Ω. If the load torque is increased so that line current becomes 30A, find the new speed. Assume flux is proportional to current.
Solution

Condition 1: IL1 = Ia1 = 20A, N1 = 800 rpm

Eb1 = V − Ia1(Ra + Rse) = 250 − 20 × (0.3 + 0.2) = 250 − 10 = 240 V

Condition 2: IL2 = Ia2 = 30A

Eb2 = 250 − 30 × 0.5 = 250 − 15 = 235 V

Since \(\phi\) ∝ \(I_a\) (below saturation): \(\phi\)1/\(\phi\)2 = Ia1/Ia2 = 20/30 = 2/3

Speed ratio: N2/N1 = (Eb2/Eb1) × (\(\phi\)1/\(\phi\)2) = (235/240) × (20/30)

N2 = 800 × (235/240) × (20/30) = 800 × 0.9792 × 0.6667 = ≈ 522 rpm

EX 03 Motor Efficiency and Shaft Torque
A 10 kW, 250V DC shunt motor runs at 1000 rpm at full load with η = 85%. Rotational losses = 500W. Find: (i) shaft torque, (ii) armature torque.
Solution

Input power: Pin = Output/η = 10000/0.85 = 11765 W

(i) Shaft (output) torque: P_shaft = 2\(\pi\)N·Tsh/60 → Tsh = P_shaft × 60 / (2\(\pi\)N)

Tsh = 10000 × 60 / (2\(\pi\) × 1000) = 95.5 N·m

Mechanical power developed: Pmech = P_shaft + Rotational losses = 10000 + 500 = 10500 W

(ii) Armature torque: Ta = Pmech × 60 / (2\(\pi\)N) = 10500 × 60 / (2\(\pi\) × 1000) = 100.3 N·m

Lost torque: Ta − Tsh = 100.3 − 95.5 = 4.8 N·m (due to friction/iron losses)

Chapter Summary

Principle

\( F = BIl \) Newton. Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motor rule). Commutator produces unidirectional torque. Same DC machine can be motor or generator (reversibility).

Back EMF

\( E_b = \frac{\phi ZNP}{60A} \). \( E_b \propto \phi N \). In motor: \( E_b < V \). \( I_a = \frac{V-E_b}{R_a} \). Back EMF makes motor self-regulating. Max \( P_m \) when \( E_b = V/2 \), \( \eta = 50\% \).

Torque

\( T_a = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\phi ZP}{A}\right) \cdot I_a \). \( T \propto \phi I_a \). Shunt: \( T \propto I_a \). Series: \( T \propto I_a^2 \) (below sat.). \( \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{\phi_2}{\phi_1}\right)\left(\frac{I_{a2}}{I_{a1}}\right) \).

Speed

\( N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} = \frac{V-I_a R_a}{\phi} \). Speed control: armature resistance, field flux, armature voltage. Shunt: constant speed. Series: never at NL.

Types & Uses

Shunt: best SR, lathes, mills. Series: highest T, traction/cranes. Cumulative: presses/compressors. Differential: unstable, no practical use. Sep. excited: servo systems.

Speed Regulation

\( \%SR = \frac{N_0-N}{N} \times 100 \). Shunt: best (5–10%). Series: very poor. Diff: negative SR (unstable). Lower SR = better constant-speed performance.
