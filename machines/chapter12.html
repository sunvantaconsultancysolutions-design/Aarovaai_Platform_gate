§ 01

Introduction & Construction

An auto transformer is a transformer in which a single continuous winding serves as both the primary and secondary. A part of the winding is common to both input and output circuits, making the auto transformer fundamentally different from the conventional two-winding (2-wdg) transformer where the primary and secondary are electrically isolated from each other.[1]

🔑
Defining Property

In an auto transformer, a part of the winding is common to both the primary and secondary circuits. Energy is transferred both by electromagnetic induction (transformer action) and by direct electrical conduction through the common winding.

Step-Down Auto Transformer — Circuit Structure

Consider a step-down auto transformer. The entire winding AC has \(N_H\) turns and is connected to the high-voltage supply \(V_H\). The lower portion BC has \(N_L\) turns; this is the common winding and the low-voltage output \(V_L\) is taken across it. The upper portion AB has \((N_H - N_L)\) turns and is the series winding.

Fig. 1 — Step-down auto transformer. Orange: series winding (NH−NL); Teal: common winding (NL). The common winding carries (IL−IH), which is smaller than either terminal current.[1,2]
📐
Key Winding Terminology

Total winding (AC): \(N_H\) turns — this is the full winding, also called the HV winding.
Common winding (BC): \(N_L\) turns — shared by input and output. Also called the LV winding.
Series winding (AB): \((N_H - N_L)\) turns — carries current \((I_L - I_H)\).

§ 02

Voltage & Current Relations

Since the same magnetic flux links both the series and common windings (the flux is common to the single core), the EMF per turn is the same throughout the winding. Therefore the voltage ratio is simply the turns ratio:[1]

\[ \frac{V_H}{N_H} = \frac{V_L}{N_L} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{V_H}{V_L} = \frac{N_H}{N_L} = a_{\text{auto}} \]

This is identical in form to a two-winding transformer. The auto-transformer voltage ratio \(a_{\text{auto}}\) is defined as the ratio of the higher voltage to the lower voltage.

⚠️
Provided Condition from Notes

The notes explicitly state: \(I_L - I_H < I_H\), i.e. \(I_L < 2I_H\), giving \(\dfrac{I_L}{I_H} < 2\), so \(a_{\text{auto}} < 2\). This restricts the voltage ratio of an auto transformer to values close to 1 for efficient operation.

Current Distribution

From the MMF balance (derived in Section 3), the current relationships are:

\[ \frac{N_H}{N_L} = \frac{I_L}{I_H} = a_{\text{auto}} \]

This means the current in the series winding AB is \((I_L - I_H)\), which flows from the source. The current in the common winding BC is the difference of the two terminal currents.

Fig. 2 — Current distribution in series vs. common winding. Both carry the same reduced current \((I_L - I_H)\), leading to significant copper savings.
§ 03

MMF Balance

Applying Ampere's circuital law (MMF balance) while neglecting the magnetising current \(I_0\):[2]

\[ (N_H - N_L)\,I_H = N_L\,(I_L - I_H) \]

Expanding the left and right sides:

\[ N_H I_H - N_L I_H = N_L I_L - N_L I_H \] \[ N_H I_H - \cancel{N_L I_H} = N_L I_L - \cancel{N_L I_H} \] \[ \boxed{N_H I_H = N_L I_L} \]

This is the fundamental MMF balance equation of the auto transformer, identical in form to the two-winding transformer. It gives us:

\[ \frac{N_H}{N_L} = \frac{I_L}{I_H} = a_{\text{auto}} \]
✅
Verification

The MMF produced by the series winding current \(I_H\) in turns \((N_H-N_L)\) exactly opposes the MMF of the common winding current \((I_L - I_H)\) in turns \(N_L\). This balance ensures transformer action with a single-core winding.

EXAMPLE 1 Find new power when voltage is halved — Resistive load
A 220 V, 100 W lamp. What power does it consume at 110 V?
Solution

The lamp is a resistive load, so \(P \propto V^2\) (since \(R\) is constant):

\[ P_{\text{new}} = P_{\text{old}} \times \left(\frac{V_{\text{new}}}{V_{\text{old}}}\right)^2 = 100 \times \left(\frac{110}{220}\right)^2 = 100 \times \frac{1}{4} = \boxed{25\ \text{W}} \]
§ 04

Copper Saving

The primary merit of an auto transformer is the saving in copper (and therefore core iron and size) compared to a conventional two-winding transformer of the same rating.[1,2]

Copper Weight Proportionality

For any winding:

\[ \text{Copper weight} \propto \text{Copper volume} \propto \text{Cross-section} \times \text{Length} \propto I \times N \propto \text{MMF} \]

Derivation of Copper Ratio

For the auto transformer, copper is needed only in the series and common windings:

\[ \text{Cu}_{\text{auto}} \propto (N_H - N_L)\,I_H + N_L\,(I_L - I_H) \]

For the equivalent two-winding transformer, copper is needed in both primary and secondary:

\[ \text{Cu}_{\text{2wdg}} \propto N_H I_H + N_L I_L \]

Therefore the copper ratio is:

\[ \frac{\text{Cu}_{\text{auto}}}{\text{Cu}_{\text{2wdg}}} = \frac{(N_H-N_L)I_H + N_L(I_L-I_H)}{N_H I_H + N_L I_L} \]

Expanding the numerator:

\[ = \frac{N_H I_H - N_L I_H + N_L I_L - N_L I_H}{2N_H I_H} = \frac{2N_H I_H - 2N_L I_H}{2N_H I_H} \]

(using \(N_H I_H = N_L I_L\), so denominator \(= 2N_H I_H\))

\[ = \frac{N_H - N_L}{N_H} = 1 - \frac{N_L}{N_H} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{\text{Cu}_{\text{auto}}}{\text{Cu}_{\text{2wdg}}} = 1 - \frac{1}{a_{\text{auto}}}} \]

Percentage Copper Saving

\[ \%\,\text{Cu saving} = \frac{\text{Cu}_{\text{2wdg}} - \text{Cu}_{\text{auto}}}{\text{Cu}_{\text{2wdg}}} \times 100 = \left(1 - \frac{\text{Cu}_{\text{auto}}}{\text{Cu}_{\text{2wdg}}}\right) \times 100 \] \[ \boxed{\%\,\text{Cu saving} = \frac{1}{a_{\text{auto}}} \times 100\%} \]
💡
Interpretation

The closer \(a_{\text{auto}}\) is to 1 (i.e. input and output voltages nearly equal), the greater the copper saving. When \(a = 1\), saving = 100% (trivial case — no transformer needed). When \(a = 2\), saving = 50%.

Fig. 3 — Percentage copper saving decreases rapidly as voltage ratio increases. Maximum benefit occurs when a is close to 1.
§ 05

Power Transfer Components

Unlike a two-winding transformer (which transfers power only by electromagnetic induction), an auto transformer transfers power by two mechanisms:[1,2]

Inductive Transfer
  • →Transfer by transformer (electromagnetic) action
  • →Through mutual flux linkage
  • →\(S_{\text{ind}} = V_L(I_L - I_H)\)
  • →Same as the rating of the equivalent 2-wdg transformer
Conductive Transfer
  • →Transfer by direct electrical conduction
  • →Through the common winding connection
  • →\(S_{\text{cond}} = V_L \cdot I_H\)
  • →This is the extra power not in 2-wdg transformer

Mathematical Derivation

Total output apparent power:

\[ S_L = V_L \cdot I_L = V_L\big[(I_L - I_H) + I_H\big] \] \[ S_L = \underbrace{V_L(I_L - I_H)}_{\text{Inductive (transformer action)}} + \underbrace{V_L \cdot I_H}_{\text{Conductive (direct conduction)}} \]

Conducted VA (Winding VA)

The VA handled by the series/common winding (the "winding VA" or \(S_{BC}\)) is:

\[ S_{BC} = V_L(I_L - I_H) = V_L I_L - V_L I_H = V_H I_H - V_L I_H = (V_H - V_L)I_H \] \[ \boxed{S_{BC} = S_{AB}} \]

This is the "transformer rating" — the actual electromagnetic rating of the core.

Ratio of Winding VA to Total VA

\[ \frac{S_{\text{cond}}}{S_{\text{total}}} = \frac{V_L I_H}{V_L I_L} = \frac{I_H}{I_L} = \frac{1}{a_{\text{auto}}} \] \[ \frac{S_{\text{ind}}}{S_{\text{total}}} = 1 - \frac{1}{a_{\text{auto}}} = \text{Cu saving} \]
🎯
GATE Concept — Cu Saving = Inductive Transfer Ratio

The copper saving ratio \(\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{a}\right)\) numerically equals the ratio of the inductively transferred power to the total power. The conductive transfer is \(\dfrac{1}{a_{\text{auto}}}\) of total power — this is why the auto transformer can be rated much higher than the 2-wdg transformer of same physical size.

§ 06

kVA Rating & Conversion Formulae

When a 2-winding transformer is reconnected as an auto transformer, its kVA rating changes. The relationship depends on whether the connection is additive or subtractive.[1,2]

General kVA Conversion Formula

kVA Conversion — 2-Wdg to Auto TFIf \( a_{\text{auto}} \le 1 \): \[ S_{\text{auto}} = [a_{\text{2wdg}} \pm 1] \times S_{\text{2wdg}} \] If \( a_{\text{auto}} > 1 \): \[ S_{\text{auto}} = \left[1 \pm \frac{1}{a_{\text{auto}}}\right] \times S_{\text{2wdg}} \] \( + \) sign \( \rightarrow \) Additive (boost/step-up) [Best option] \( - \) sign \( \rightarrow \) Subtractive (buck/step-down)

Equivalently, the auto transformer rating and the 2-winding transformer rating are related by:

\[ S_{\text{auto}} = S_{\text{2wdg}} \times \frac{a_{\text{auto}}}{a_{\text{auto}} - 1} \quad \text{(for step-down, } a > 1\text{)} \]
📊
Quick Formula — Terminal-Based

If the auto transformer has HV terminal \(V_H\) and HV current \(I_H\), then:
\(S_{\text{auto}} = V_H \cdot I_H\) or \(V_L \cdot I_L\) (either terminal gives total VA)

§ 07

Reconnection — Worked Examples

This classic GATE/IES problem type: a 2-winding transformer is reconnected as an auto transformer. Find the voltage ratio and kVA rating for all possible connections.

EXAMPLE 2 25 kVA, 2500/250 V 2-wdg TF reconnected as auto TF — all possible connections
A 25 kVA, 2500/250 V two-winding transformer is to be reconnected as an auto transformer. Determine the voltage ratio and kVA rating for all possible connections.
Given Data

Two-winding ratings: 25 kVA, 2500/250 V → HV winding: 2500 V, 10 A; LV winding: 250 V, 100 A.

Connection 1 — Additive (Best Option)

Fig. 4 — Additive connection: C (LV top) tied to A (HV top). Output = 2500 + 250 = 2750 V.
STEP 1
Voltage ratio: \(a_{\text{auto}} = \dfrac{V_H}{V_L} = \dfrac{2750}{2500} = 1.1\)
STEP 2
kVA rating: \(S_{\text{auto}} = V_H \cdot I_H = 2750 \times 100 = \mathbf{275\ kVA}\) (or \(2500 \times 110 = 275\) kVA)
STEP 3
Conductive transfer = \(275 - 25 = 250\) kVA; Inductive (original 2-wdg) = 25 kVA.
✅
Best Option — Why?

The additive connection gives the highest kVA rating (275 kVA vs 22.5–27.5 kVA for other options) and a turns ratio close to 1, maximising copper saving.

Connection 2 — Additive, Bad Polarity

Connecting HV winding in subtractive additive polarity gives \(V_{\text{out}} = 2500 + 250 = 2750\) V but with HV input of 2750 V: \(a = 2750/250 = 11\), \(S_{\text{auto}} = 2750 \times 10 = 27.5\) kVA — poor utilisation.

Connection 3 — Subtractive (Good Option)

C (LV top) tied to B (HV bottom): output = \(2500 - 250 = 2250\) V.

\(a_{\text{auto}} = 2500/2250 = 1.111\); \(S_{\text{auto}} = 2500 \times 90 = 225\) kVA (or \(2250 \times 100 = 225\) kVA).

Conductive transfer = 200 kVA; Inductive = 25 kVA (= common winding).

Connection 4 — Subtractive (Worst Option)

Output = \(2500 - 250 = 2250\) V with 10 A input: \(a = 2250/2500 = 0.9\), \(S_{\text{auto}} = 2250 \times 10 = 22.5\) kVA. Conductive transfer = −2.5 kVA (negative — this means power is fed back, very poor).

§ 08

Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages
  • ✓Less copper and less iron for same duty — smaller, lighter
  • ✓Lower exciting current → higher efficiency \(\eta\)
  • ✓Lower per-unit (p.u.) impedance → better voltage regulation
  • ✓Transfers power both inductively and conductively
  • ✓Can be used as continuously variable voltage transformer (Variac)
  • ✓Advantageous when voltage ratio is close to 1
Disadvantages
  • ✗No electrical isolation between primary and secondary
  • ✗Disturbance on one side spreads rapidly to the other
  • ✗In step-down mode, open-circuit in common winding subjects load to full HV supply
  • ✗Lower p.u. impedance → higher short-circuit current (danger)
  • ✗LV winding insulation must withstand HV when reconnected
⚡
Safety Hazard — No Isolation

If the common winding develops an open circuit in step-down operation, the load is connected directly to the full HV supply (limited only by voltage drop across the series winding). This is a critical safety disadvantage of the auto transformer.

⚠️
p.u. Impedance Consideration

The p.u. impedance of an auto transformer is lower than the equivalent 2-winding transformer because the effective turns ratio is reduced. While this improves voltage regulation, it results in higher fault (short-circuit) currents, which must be accounted for in protective relay settings.

§ 10

Tertiary Winding

A tertiary winding is a third winding provided on a transformer in addition to the usual primary (1°) and secondary (2°) windings. It is used in special-purpose transformers requiring three voltage levels or functions.[2]

Voltage & Current Relations — Three-Winding Transformer

For a transformer with N₁, N₂, N₃ turns and terminal voltages V₁, V₂, V₃:

\[ \frac{V_1}{N_1} = \frac{V_2}{N_2} = \frac{V_3}{N_3} \]

MMF Balance — Three Windings

Applying MMF balance (neglecting magnetising current):

\[ N_1\,\vec{I_1} - N_2\,\vec{I_2} - N_3\,\vec{I_3} = N_1\,\vec{I_0} \] \[ \vec{I_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}\,\vec{I_2} + \frac{N_3}{N_1}\,\vec{I_3} + \vec{I_0} \]

Taking the conjugate and multiplying by \(\vec{V_1}\):

\[ \vec{S_1} = \vec{S_2} + \vec{S_3} + \vec{S_0} \]
Fig. 6 — Three-winding transformer. Orange: primary (N₁, V₁, I₁); Teal: secondary (N₂, V₂); Green: tertiary (N₃, V₃). MMF balance: \(N_1I_1 = N_2I_2 + N_3I_3\).

Applications of Tertiary Winding

Power System Uses
  • ①Interconnect 3 power systems at different voltage levels
  • ②Provide 2 different auxiliary voltage levels in unit auxiliary TF of power stations
  • ③Connect reactive power compensating equipment (capacitor banks, reactors) in substations
  • ④Provide low-voltage supply for water, lighting, AC in HV substations
Stabilising Winding
  • ★A tertiary delta winding is added in star-star (Yy) transformers
  • ★Overcomes problems related to magnetising current phenomena
  • ★Handles unbalanced loads and unbalanced faults
  • ★A tertiary delta winding, if unloaded, is called a stabilising winding
EXAMPLE 3 Three-winding transformer — primary current and power factor
Ratio of turns/phase in 1°, 2°, 3° windings: 10 : 2 : 1. Lagging currents of 45 A at PF 0.8 in 2° winding; 50 A at PF 0.71 in 3° winding. Find the 1° current and power factor. (Neglect losses and exciting current.)
Solution
STEP 1
Express currents in phasor form. Take V₁ as reference.
\(\vec{I_2} = 45\angle{-\cos^{-1}(0.8)} = 45\angle{-36.87°}\) A
\(\vec{I_3} = 50\angle{-\cos^{-1}(0.71)} = 50\angle{-44.77°}\) A
STEP 2
Apply MMF balance (neglecting I₀): \[\vec{I_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}\vec{I_2} + \frac{N_3}{N_1}\vec{I_3} = \frac{2}{10}\times 45\angle{-36.87°} + \frac{1}{10}\times 50\angle{-44.77°}\]
STEP 3
\[\vec{I_1} = 9\angle{-36.87°} + 5\angle{-44.77°}\] \[= (9\times 0.8 - j9\times 0.6) + (5\times 0.713 - j5\times 0.701)\] \[= (7.2 - j5.4) + (3.565 - j3.505)\] \[= 10.765 - j8.905 = 13.97\angle{-39.63°}\ \text{A}\]
STEP 4
\(\text{PF}_{1°} = \cos(39.63°) = \mathbf{0.7695\ \text{(lagging)}}\)
EXAMPLE 4 Three-winding transformer — primary current with capacitive tertiary
An ideal TF has 3 windings: 100 turns (P), 160 turns (S), 60 turns (T). Winding S feeds 10 A to a resistive load; winding T takes 20 A from a pure capacitive load. Calculate the primary current and its PF. (Neglect magnetising current.)
Solution

Note: Capacitive load → current leads voltage → supply-leading (leading PF). Capacitor supplies VArs.

STEP 1
Phasor form (V₁ reference): \(\vec{I_2} = 10\angle 0°\) A (resistive — unity PF); \(\vec{I_3} = 20\angle{+90°}\) A (capacitive — current leads V by 90°)
STEP 2
\[\vec{I_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}\vec{I_2} + \frac{N_3}{N_1}\vec{I_3} = \frac{160}{100}\times 10\angle 0° + \frac{60}{100}\times 20\angle{+90°}\] \[= 16\angle 0° + 12\angle{+90°} = 16 + j12 = 20\angle{+36.87°}\ \text{A}\]
STEP 3
\(\text{PF}_{1°} = \cos(36.87°) = \mathbf{0.8\ \text{(leading)}}\) — primary draws leading current due to capacitive tertiary load.

GATE EE A 50 kVA, 2000/200 V two-winding transformer is connected as an auto transformer with additive polarity. Find its kVA rating. GATE EE 2015

Solution: For additive connection (step-up), \(V_H = 2000 + 200 = 2200\) V, \(V_L = 2000\) V.
HV winding current (original secondary): \(I = 50000/200 = 250\) A (this now flows as load current at 2200 V terminal)
Wait — let us use the formula directly: \(a_{\text{2wdg}} = 2000/200 = 10\).

\[ S_{\text{auto}} = S_{\text{2wdg}} \times \left(1 + \frac{1}{a_{\text{2wdg}}}\right) = 50 \times \left(1 + \frac{1}{10}\right) = 50 \times 1.1 = \boxed{55\ \text{kVA}} \]

Alternatively: terminal voltage = \(2000+200 = 2200\) V, terminal current = \(50000/2000 + 50000/200 = 25 + 250 = 275\) A, so \(S = 2000 \times 275/1000 = 550\) kVA — using correct input side gives 550 kVA. (Exact approach depends on which terminal is input.)

  • (B) 550 kVA
  • (C) 55 kVA
  • (D) 500 kVA

IES E&T Which of the following is NOT an advantage of an auto transformer over a two-winding transformer? IES 2018
  • (A) Higher efficiency due to lower copper and iron losses
  • (B) Lower per-unit leakage impedance and better voltage regulation
  • (C) Provides electrical isolation between primary and secondary circuits
  • (D) Suitable for use as a continuously variable voltage transformer (Variac)

Answer: (C) — The auto transformer does NOT provide electrical isolation; this is its main disadvantage. The absence of galvanic isolation means fault currents and disturbances can pass freely between the two circuits.

IES E&T In a star-star transformer, a tertiary delta winding (stabilising winding) is provided mainly to: IES 2016
  • (A) Increase the kVA rating of the transformer
  • (B) Reduce copper losses in the transformer
  • (C) Suppress harmonic voltages and provide a path for zero-sequence currents
  • (D) Improve the insulation of the HV winding

Answer: (C) — The tertiary delta stabilising winding in a Yy transformer suppresses third-harmonic voltages (provides a circulating path for 3rd harmonic magnetising current) and handles zero-sequence currents from unbalanced loads and faults.

GATE EE The ratio S(inductive)/S(total) in an auto transformer with turns ratio 4:3 is: GATE EE 2019

Solution: \(a_{\text{auto}} = N_H/N_L = 4/3\)

\[ \frac{S_{\text{ind}}}{S_{\text{total}}} = 1 - \frac{1}{a} = 1 - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4} = \boxed{0.25} \]

This means 25% of the power is transferred by induction (transformer action) and 75% by direct conduction. The copper saving is also 25% compared to a 2-winding transformer for the same duty.

  • (A) 0.75
  • (B) 0.25
  • (C) 0.33
  • (D) 0.50

Chapter Summary

Auto Transformer

Single winding; common to both primary and secondary. Transfers power by both inductive and conductive action. Part of winding is series (N_H−N_L), part is common (N_L).

Copper Saving

\(\%\text{Cu saving} = \dfrac{1}{a}\times 100\%\). Saving is maximum when a ≈ 1. Practically limited to voltage ratio ≤ 2. Also equals ratio of inductive to total transfer.

Power Components

S_total = S_inductive + S_conductive. Inductive = (1−1/a)·S, Conductive = (1/a)·S. Winding VA = (V_H−V_L)·I_H = S_AB = S_BC.

kVA Conversion

Additive: \(S_{\text{auto}} = (1+1/a_{2wdg})\cdot S_{2wdg}\). Subtractive: \((1-1/a)\cdot S\). Additive gives highest rating — best option. Ratio close to 1 → maximum advantage.

Disadvantage

No electrical isolation — primary hazard. Open circuit in common winding subjects load to full HV. Lower p.u. Z → higher SC currents. LV insulation must be rated for HV.

Tertiary Winding

3rd winding for 3 voltage levels. Stabilising delta in Yy transformer suppresses 3rd harmonic voltages, handles zero-sequence currents, unbalanced loads and faults.
