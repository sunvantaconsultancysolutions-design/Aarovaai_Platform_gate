§1

Speed Relations — Shunt vs Series Motor

The fundamental speed equation for any DC motor is derived from the back-EMF expression. Since \(E_b = V - I_a R_a\) and \(E_b \propto \phi N\), we get:[2]

Universal Speed Equation
\[ N \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \propto \frac{V - I_a R_a}{\phi} \]

Shunt Motor — Speed Derivation

In a shunt motor, the field winding is connected directly across the supply voltage \(V\). The shunt field current \(I_{sh} = V/R_{sh}\) is essentially constant (independent of load). Hence flux \(\phi \approx \text{constant}\).

The armature current \(I_a = I_L - I_{sh}\), and the current through the armature of each parallel path depends on the winding configuration. For a compound motor with shunt field:

Shunt Motor Speed — Compound Winding Effects
\[ N_{sh} \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \approx \frac{V - I_{sh}R_{sh}}{\phi} \]
For field winding with half-current paths: \[ I_{sh,\text{eff}} = \frac{I}{2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad N_{sh} \propto \frac{V}{I/2} = \frac{2V}{I} \]

Series Motor — Speed Derivation

In a series motor, the field winding is in series with the armature. The field current equals the line current \(I\), so \(\phi \propto I\). This gives a hyperbolic speed-load characteristic — very high speed at no load (dangerous) and low speed at full load (high torque).[1]

Series Motor Speed
\[ \phi \propto I \quad \Rightarrow \quad N_{se} \propto \frac{E_b}{\phi} \propto \frac{V}{I} \]
With compound winding sharing current between two paths: \[ N_{se} \propto \frac{V}{2I} \]
⚠️
Key GATE Result — Speed Ratio

Comparing shunt vs series (with same compound winding configuration): \(N_{sh} \propto \frac{2V}{I}\) and \(N_{se} \propto \frac{V}{2I}\), therefore: \[ \boxed{N_{sh} = 4\,N_{se}} \] The shunt motor runs at 4× the speed of the series motor under equivalent conditions. This is a frequently tested result in GATE EE.

Fig 1 — Speed vs Load characteristics. Shunt motor has nearly constant speed (slight droop). Series motor has hyperbolic characteristic — never run a series motor at no-load!
Basis: Chapman [1] §7.8; Kothari & Nagrath [2] §5.9
§2

Torque Relations — Shunt vs Series

The electromagnetic torque developed by any DC motor is given by \(T \propto \phi I_a\). The difference between motor types comes from how \(\phi\) varies with current.[2]

Series Motor Torque
  • →Field winding in series: \(\phi \propto I_a\)
  • →\(T \propto \phi \cdot I_a \propto I_a^2\)
  • →Starting torque is very high (squared relationship)
  • →Suitable for traction, cranes, hoists
  • →\(T_{se} \propto I^2\)
Shunt Motor Torque
  • →Field nearly constant: \(\phi \approx \text{const}\)
  • →\(T \propto \phi \cdot I_a \propto I_a\)
  • →Linear torque-current relationship
  • →Good speed regulation; suitable for lathes, fans
  • →\(T_{sh} \propto \frac{I}{2} \cdot \frac{I}{2} = \frac{I^2}{4}\)
⚠️
Key GATE Result — Torque Ratio

For the same line current \(I\) with compound winding configuration:
\(T_{se} \propto I^2\) and \(T_{sh} \propto I^2/4\), therefore: \[ \boxed{T_{sh} = \frac{T_{se}}{4}} \] The series motor produces 4× the starting torque of the shunt motor — this is why series motors are used in electric trains and traction applications.

Motor Type Speed Relation Torque Relation Application
Series \(N_{se} \propto V/2I\) \(T_{se} \propto I^2\) Traction, cranes, hoists
Shunt \(N_{sh} \propto 2V/I\) \(T_{sh} \propto I^2/4\) Lathes, centrifugal pumps
Compound (Cumul.) \(N_{sh} > N > N_{se}\) \(T_{sh} < T < T_{se}\) Elevators, press machines
§3

Why DC Motors Need Starters

At the instant of starting, the motor is at standstill — rotor speed \(N = 0\). Since back-EMF \(E_b \propto N\phi\), we have \(E_b = 0\) at start. The armature current is then limited only by the armature resistance \(R_a\):[1]

Starting Current Without Starter
\[ I_{a,\text{start}} = \frac{V - E_b}{R_a} = \frac{V - 0}{R_a} = \frac{V}{R_a} \]
🔴
The Problem

Since \(R_a\) is typically very small (0.1–2 Ω for large motors), the starting current \(I_{a,\text{start}} = V/R_a\) can be 10–30 times the rated current. For a 240 V motor with \(R_a = 0.5\,\Omega\): \(I_{\text{start}} = 240/0.5 = 480\,A\)! This causes voltage dips in the supply, damages commutator brushes, and can burn out the armature winding.

For small motors that accelerate quickly, the short-duration inrush current (in-rush) may not damage the motor — because the large current builds up back-EMF rapidly and the duration is brief. However, large motors with significant mechanical inertia accelerate slowly, drawing huge current over a long period, which:

Electrical Damage
  • →Burns armature winding insulation
  • →Damages commutator segments and brushes
  • →Causes voltage dip (sag) at supply terminals
  • →Affects other equipment on the same bus
Solution: Starter
  • →Insert external resistance \(R_{ext}\) in series with armature
  • →\(I_{\text{start}} = V/(R_a + R_{ext})\) — now limited
  • →As speed builds up, \(E_b\) rises and \(R_{ext}\) is cut out in steps
  • →At full speed: \(R_{ext} = 0\), motor runs normally

Three Protective Schemes in Every Starter

Besides the starting resistance, every DC motor starter incorporates three important protective functions to safeguard the motor and operator:[2]

① No-Volt Release (NVR)

If supply voltage fails or is interrupted, the hold-on coil de-energises and the spring returns the handle to the OFF position. This prevents the motor from restarting automatically when supply is restored — protecting the operator and connected load from unexpected starting.

② Overload Release (OLR)

A current-sensing relay detects excessive load current (overload condition). On overload, it trips the starter handle to OFF, disconnecting the motor. This protects the armature winding from thermal damage due to sustained overcurrent.

③ Field Failure Prevention

If the field circuit opens (field failure), flux \(\phi \rightarrow 0\). Since \(N \propto E_b/\phi\), the motor will race to dangerously high speeds — a condition called "runaway." Field failure protection trips the starter on loss of field current to prevent this.

§4

Types of DC Motor Starters

There are three standard types of DC motor starters, each designed for specific motor configurations. The choice of starter depends on the motor type (shunt, series, or compound) and the operating conditions.

Starter Type Used For Points Field Failure Protection
3-Point Starter Shunt & Compound motors 3 terminals (L, F, A) Yes (NVR in series with field)
4-Point Starter Shunt & Compound (FWSC) 4 terminals (L, F, A, AA) No (HOC isolated from field)
2-Point Starter Series motors only 2 terminals (L, A) Yes (HOC in series with field + arm)
🔑
Terminology

HOC = Hold-On Coil (electromagnet that keeps the starter arm in the ON position). NVR = No-Volt Release. OLR = Overload Release. FWSC = Field Weakening Speed Control (varying field resistance to control speed). L = Line, F = Field, A = Armature terminals.

§5

3-Point Starter — Construction & Operation

The 3-point starter is the most common type used with shunt and compound DC motors. It has three terminals: L (Line), F (Field), and A (Armature). The handle sweeps across a series of brass studs, each connected to a tap of the starting resistance.[2]

Fig 2 — 3-Point DC Motor Starter. Handle moves from OFF through studs 1→4→ON, progressively cutting out starting resistance. Hold-On Coil (HOC) in series with field winding provides No-Volt Release (NVR).
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §5.14; Chapman [1], §9.5

Operating Sequence

The starting procedure proceeds as follows. When the handle is moved to stud 1, the full starting resistance is in series with the armature — limiting current to a safe value. As the motor accelerates, the back-EMF builds up, reducing the effective armature current. The operator then progressively moves the handle to studs 2, 3, 4 and finally to the ON (running) position, cutting out the resistance in steps.

ℹ️
How NVR Works in 3-Point Starter

The Hold-On Coil (HOC) is in series with the field winding. When supply voltage is present and the field circuit is intact, the HOC is energised and its magnetic force holds the starter arm at the ON position against the pull of the return spring. If supply fails (NVR action) or the field circuit opens (field failure protection), the HOC de-energises and the spring returns the handle to OFF. This provides both NVR and field failure protection in a single coil.

Limitation of 3-Point Starter

🔴
Problem with Field Weakening Speed Control (FWSC)

When FWSC is applied to a shunt or compound motor, the field resistance is increased to reduce field current and increase speed. However, since the HOC is in series with the field winding, reducing field current also reduces HOC magnetising force. If the field current is reduced too much during FWSC, the HOC may fail to hold the handle at ON — causing the motor to trip unnecessarily. Solution: Use a 4-point starter when FWSC is required.

§6

4-Point Starter — Construction & Operation

The 4-point starter overcomes the FWSC limitation of the 3-point starter. It has four terminals: L, F, A, and an additional L′ (or sometimes labeled as the HOC terminal). The key difference is that the Hold-On Coil is isolated from the field winding — it is connected directly across the line voltage through a separate limiting resistor.[2]

Fig 3 — 4-Point Starter. The Hold-On Coil is connected directly to the line voltage through R_hoc, completely independent of the field winding. Field current can be reduced for FWSC without affecting HOC holding force.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §5.14.3; Chapman [1], §9.5
💡
4-Point Starter: Advantage & Limitation

Advantage: Since HOC is connected directly to the supply line (not through the field winding), its holding force is independent of field current. FWSC can be applied without risk of the starter tripping.
Limitation: There is no field failure protection — if the field winding opens, the HOC remains energised (from the line) and the handle stays ON, allowing the motor to accelerate dangerously. This is the trade-off compared to the 3-point starter.

§7

2-Point Starter — For Series Motors

The 2-point starter is used exclusively for DC series motors. It has only two external terminals: L (Line) and A (Armature). Since the series motor's field winding is in series with the armature, there is no separate field terminal needed.[2]

Fig 4 — 2-Point Starter for series motors. HOC is in series with the series field winding and armature. If load is suddenly disconnected, the current drops, HOC releases, and the starter trips — preventing dangerous runaway.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §5.14.4
💡
Why 2-Point Starter Protects Against Runaway

Unlike shunt motors, series motors can reach dangerously high speeds if the load on their shaft is suddenly disconnected (because \(N \propto 1/\phi \propto 1/I_a\), and with no load, \(I_a \rightarrow 0\) and \(N \rightarrow \infty\)). In the 2-point starter, the HOC is in series with both the field winding and the armature. If load is suddenly disconnected, armature current drops, HOC loses force, and the spring returns the handle to OFF — tripping the motor before it can reach dangerous speed.

ℹ️
Using 3-Point Starter with Series Motor

A 3-point starter can be used to start a series motor with a slight modification: the F terminal must be short-circuited through a negative terminal to provide a closed path for current to flow through the HOC. This maintains the HOC energisation. The 2-point starter is simpler and preferred for series motors.

§8

DC Motor Braking

Braking is applied to a DC motor to intentionally stop it quickly or to control its speed in overhauling load conditions. Two basic braking categories exist:[2]

Mechanical Braking
  • →KE of rotating parts dissipated in brake pads
  • →Produces noise, wear, and heat
  • →High maintenance; braking not smooth
  • →Used as final stop mechanism
Electrical Braking
  • →Develops a retarding (negative) torque electrically
  • →Smooth, controllable, no mechanical wear
  • →Simply isolating from supply is not enough — motor coasts
  • →Three methods: Dynamic, Plugging, Regenerative

The fundamental principle of electrical braking is to reverse the armature current in the running motor, thereby developing a negative (retarding) torque that opposes the direction of rotation and decelerates the motor.

Fig 5 — Three electrical braking methods compared. Plugging produces the most rapid braking (highest braking current). Regenerative braking is most efficient but cannot stop the motor.

(a) Dynamic / Rheostatic Braking

In dynamic braking, the armature winding is disconnected from the supply while the field winding remains connected. The motor continues to rotate due to inertia and acts as a generator — its back-EMF drives a current through an external braking resistor \(R_{ext}\). This circulating current produces a torque opposing the rotation, decelerating the motor.[2]

Dynamic Braking — Braking Current
\[ I_b = \frac{E_b}{R_a + R_{ext}} \]
where \(E_b\) is the back-EMF at the instant of braking. As the motor decelerates, \(E_b\) reduces and so does \(I_b\) and the braking torque — smooth deceleration.
Fig 6 — Dynamic Braking circuit. Field stays energised. Armature circuit is opened from supply and closed through R_ext. Back-EMF circulates braking current through R_ext, producing retarding torque.

(b) Plugging

Plugging (also called counter-current braking) is the most powerful braking method. The armature terminals (or armature polarity) are reversed while the motor is still running. This means the supply voltage and the back-EMF both act in the same direction to drive a very large braking current through the armature, creating a strong negative torque.[1]

Plugging — Braking Current
\[ I_b = \frac{V + E_b}{R_a + R_{ext}} \]
At the moment of plugging, the effective voltage driving the braking current is \(V + E_b\) — nearly twice the supply voltage. Hence, a large external resistance must be inserted to limit this to a safe value. Apply mechanical brake at near zero speed to prevent motoring in reverse direction.
⚠️
GATE/IES Concept — Plugging

In plugging, the motor wants to run in the opposite direction once it reaches zero speed. If the power is not cut off at zero speed, the motor will start running in reverse. A zero-speed sensing relay must be used to disconnect supply at zero speed. The energy dissipated during plugging is higher than dynamic braking — it equals the kinetic energy of the load plus the energy drawn from the supply during braking.

Fig 7 — Plugging circuit. Armature terminals are reversed. Both supply voltage V and back-EMF E_b drive the braking current in the same direction, producing very high braking current and strong retarding torque.

(c) Regenerative Braking

Regenerative braking is not intentional — it occurs naturally due to the inherent properties of the motor when subjected to overhauling load conditions: a train descending a gradient, a crane lowering a load, a hoist lowering its cage. In these conditions, the load drives the motor faster than its no-load speed.[2]

Regenerative Braking Condition
When \(E_b > V\):
\[ I_a = \frac{V - E_b}{R_a} < 0 \]
Armature current reverses → Torque reverses → Motor decelerates → Machine acts as generator, returning energy to supply.

When \(E_b\) drops back to \(E_b < V\), motor resumes normal operation (\(E_b < V\)).
💡
Regenerative Braking — Key Points

Energy efficient: Energy is returned to the supply, not wasted as heat.
Cannot stop the motor: As speed drops and \(E_b\) falls below \(V\), motoring action resumes — the motor finds a new equilibrium speed, it doesn't stop.
Series motor problem: For a series motor, regenerative braking requires separate excitation of the field winding, because if \(I_a\) reverses, the series field also reverses — creating a positive torque instead of a braking torque. The field winding direction must be kept unchanged while \(I_a\) reverses, requiring external separate excitation.
Mountain railways: This is the principle used in electric mountain railway locomotives — on descent, the traction motors act as generators, returning energy to the supply grid.

Braking Method Braking Current Energy Can Stop? Application
Dynamic / Rheostatic \(I_b = E_b/(R_a+R_{ext})\) Dissipated in R_ext Yes Hoists, cranes
Plugging \(I_b = (V+E_b)/(R_a+R_{ext})\) Dissipated (highest loss) Yes (+ reverse guard) Rapid stop applications
Regenerative \(I_a\) reverses when \(E_b > V\) Returned to supply No (speed control only) Electric trains, mountain railway
GATE EE Plugging External Resistance Calculation Class Example

Problem: A 240 V shunt motor has \(R_a = 0.5\,\Omega\), \(R_{sh} = 80\,\Omega\). At full load it draws a line current \(I_L = 15\,A\). It is to be plugged (armature reversed). Maximum allowable plugging current is 1.25 times the full-load armature current. Find the external resistance \(R_{ext}\) required.

Solution (from Notes)
\[ I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}} = \frac{240}{80} = 3\,A \] \[ I_a = I_L - I_{sh} = 15 - 3 = 12\,A \] \[ E_b = V - I_a R_a = 240 - 12(0.5) = 234\,V \] During plugging, effective voltage = \(V + E_b = 240 + 234 = 474\,V\) \[ I_{b,\max} = 1.25 \times 12 = 15\,A \quad (\text{wait — check notes: }I_{b,\max} = 1.25 \times I_{a,FL})\] \[ R_{ext} = \frac{V + E_b}{I_{b,\max}} - R_a = \frac{474}{15} - 0.5 = 31.6 - 0.5 = 31.1\,\Omega \] \[ \boxed{R_{ext} = 31.1\,\Omega} \]

Generator Operating as Motor — Direction Analysis

A GATE-frequently-tested concept: When a running generator (prime mover driven) suddenly loses its prime mover, it remains connected to the bus bar and will transition to motor mode, drawing current from the bus bar to continue rotating. The direction of rotation and current depends on the machine type.

Machine Prime Mover Fails What Changes? Result
Shunt Generator → Shunt Motor Transitions to motor Only \(I_a\) direction changes (\(\phi\) same) Runs at same direction
Series Generator → Series Motor Transitions to motor Both \(\phi\) and \(I_a\) reverse \(T = (-\phi)(-I_a) = +T \rightarrow \text{Same direction}\)
Cumulative Compound Gen. → Motor \(I_a\) reverses \(\phi_{se}\) reverses; becomes differential compound Differential compound motor
🔑
GATE Trick — Direction of Torque

To determine direction of rotation when a generator transitions to motor mode, apply: \[ T \propto \phi \cdot I_a \] Track the sign of \(\phi\) and \(I_a\) separately after the transition. For shunt: only \(I_a\) sign changes (field is independent), so \(T\) reverses in sign but the machine had been generating by opposing torque — now the electrical torque becomes the driving torque in the same direction. For series: both change sign → product same sign → same rotation direction.

GATE EE CONCEPT Speed Control by Armature Voltage Halving — Rated Torque vs Rated Power Load

Case: Rated Torque load (constant torque). If armature voltage is halved (V/2) while field is kept constant (\(\phi\) = const):

\[ E_{b2} = \frac{V}{2} - I_a R_a \approx \frac{E_{b1}}{2} \] \[ \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{E_{b2}/\phi_2}{E_{b1}/\phi_1} = \frac{E_{b1}/2}{E_{b1}} = \frac{1}{2} \] Speed halves: \( N_2 = N_1/2 \). Since torque is constant and T ∝ \(\phi\)·\(I_a\) → \(I_{a2} = I_{a1}\) (same). Power \(P = E_b I_a\) halves → Constant Torque load.

Case: Rated Power load (constant power). If supply voltage is halved to V/2 and field voltage is also halved (flux halves → \(\phi\)/2):

\[ I_{ch} = \frac{V}{2R_{sh}} = \frac{I_{sh}}{2} \Rightarrow \phi \to \phi/2 \] \[ \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{E_{b1}/2}{E_{b1}} \times \frac{\phi_1}{\phi_1/2} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 = 1 \] Speed unchanged: \(N_2 = N_1\). But \(I_{a2} = 2I_{a1}\) (to maintain power). \(P = E_b I_a = (E_{b1}/2)(2I_{a1}) = E_{b1}I_{a1}\) → Constant Power.
