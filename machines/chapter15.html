3.1

Introduction — Synchronous Machines

A synchronous machine is an AC machine in which the rotor rotates at a speed exactly equal to the speed of the rotating magnetic field (RMF) produced by the stator winding. This synchronous speed \(N_s\) is given by:

Synchronous Speed Relationship
\(f = \frac{PN}{120}\) where P = number of poles, N = speed in RPM, f = frequency in Hz For 50 Hz supply (India standard): \(50 = \frac{PN}{120} \implies PN = 6000\) Examples: 2-pole machine: N = 3000 RPM | 4-pole: N = 1500 RPM 6-pole machine: N = 1000 RPM | 10-pole: N = 600 RPM 12-pole machine: N = 500 RPM | 24-pole: N = 250 RPM Note: Synchronous speed in rad/s: \(\omega_s = \frac{2\pi f}{P_{pairs}} = \frac{2\pi \times 50}{P/2}\)
ℹ️
Why "Synchronous"?

The machine is called synchronous because the rotor speed is in synchronism with the rotating magnetic field of the stator — both rotate at exactly the same speed \(N_s\). Any deviation from this synchronism leads to loss of synchronism (out-of-step condition), which is a serious fault condition.

Synchronous machines can operate as generators (alternators) — converting mechanical energy to electrical energy — or as motors — converting electrical to mechanical energy. They can also supply or absorb reactive power, making them useful as synchronous condensers for VAR compensation in power systems.[1]

As Generator (Alternator)
  • →Rotor excited by DC field winding
  • →Stator (armature) produces 3-phase AC output
  • →Used in all power stations (thermal, hydro, nuclear)
  • →Driven at synchronous speed by prime mover
As Motor
  • →Runs exactly at synchronous speed (no slip)
  • →Not self-starting — needs auxiliary starting method
  • →Power factor controllable by field excitation
  • →Used in cement plants, compressors, pumps
3.2

Construction — Stator (Armature)

In a synchronous machine, the armature winding is placed on the stator (stationary part). This is opposite to DC machines where the armature is on the rotor. The reason for placing the armature on the stator in large synchronous machines is that:[1]

High-voltage, high-current 3-phase AC output requires heavy insulation, large conductor cross-sections, and complex cooling arrangements — all of which are easier to manage on a stationary structure. The field winding on the rotor carries low-power DC (e.g., 2600 A dc at 310 V dc for a 247 MVA machine), making slip ring connections far simpler.

Fig 3.1 — Cross-section of 3-phase 4-pole synchronous machine. Armature (3-phase) winding on stator; salient-pole rotor carries DC field winding.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.1; Chapman [1], §5.2
🔑
Why Armature on Stator in AC Machines?

In large AC synchronous machines, insulation difficulties at high voltage, cooling requirements for high current, and the need for direct connection to load (without slip rings) make it practical to put armature on stator. The field winding on rotor carries low DC (via slip rings), which is far easier. In DC machines (low-frequency switching via commutator), armature is on rotor.

The stator core is made of high-grade silicon steel laminations to reduce eddy current and hysteresis losses. Stator laminations are typically 0.5 mm thick, varnish-insulated. For a large generator like 247 MVA, 0.85 PF lag, 3-phase, 50 Hz, 8000 rpm: armature winding carries 3-phase AC at 15.75 kV line-to-line, with 90,500 A armature current in double-star connection.[2]

3.3

Cylindrical Rotor (Round Rotor / Non-Salient)

The cylindrical rotor (also called round rotor or non-salient pole rotor) has a smooth cylindrical surface with field winding distributed in slots machined along the rotor. This type is used for high-speed turbogenerators (2-pole or 4-pole, driven by steam/gas turbines).[1]

Fig 3.2 — Cylindrical rotor: uniform airgap, distributed field winding in slots. Max turns at interpolar axis, minimum near pole face. Stepped MMF approximates a sinusoid.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.2, p.364; Chapman [1], §5.2
⚠️
Why Ni-Cd Steel — No Lamination in Cylindrical Rotor?

Turbogenerator rotors spin at 3000 RPM under enormous centrifugal forces. Nickel-chromium steel provides the mechanical strength needed. Since the rotor field is DC (not alternating), there are no eddy current losses in the rotor core — hence no lamination is needed. At high speeds, a laminated rotor would mechanically disintegrate.

Cylindrical Rotor — How Sinusoidal MMF is Achieved
Principle: Air gap is uniform → reluctance is constant → sinusoidal flux density requires sinusoidal MMF distribution. Method: Maximum number of turns placed in slots adjacent to interpolar axis. Number of turns decreases as slots approach the pole face (minimum turns). The variation in turns per slot should match the desired cosine distribution. Result: Stepped MMF wave approximates sinusoid. Perfect sinusoid only if number of field slots → ∞ (infinite slots). Important: BLV (back-emf) must be sinusoidal → requires sinusoidal RMF
3.4

Salient Pole Rotor (Projected Poles)

The salient pole rotor (salient = projected/protruding) has distinct pole projections with concentrated field winding wound around each pole. It is used in low-speed, multi-pole alternators driven by hydro-turbines or diesel engines.[2]

Fig 3.3 — Salient pole rotor (4-pole): projected poles with concentrated field winding. Non-uniform airgap — minimum at pole centre, maximum between poles. Leakage flux (φ₁₁) doesn't contribute to power transfer; only mutual flux (φ₁₂) does.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.2; Chapman [1], §5.2
ℹ️
Achieving Sinusoidal Flux with Salient Pole

In a salient pole machine, the field MMF is concentric (concentrated at poles) and constant over the pole arc. To get sinusoidal flux density distribution, the pole face is shaped — the air gap is made minimum at the pole centre and increases towards the pole tips. This compensates for the flat MMF and creates the desired sinusoidal flux density profile. The pole arc shape must be compatible with the desired sinusoid.

3.5

Sinusoidal Flux Density Distribution

For a synchronous machine to generate a pure sinusoidal EMF waveform, the radial flux density in the air gap must be sinusoidally distributed. This is the fundamental requirement because the induced EMF in each conductor is \(e = Blv\), so for sinusoidal \(e\), we need sinusoidal \(B\).[2]

Conditions for Sinusoidal Flux

① Required: Sinusoidal B in Air Gap
  • →The equivalent waveform of generated voltage is sinusoidal
  • →A sinusoidal voltage may only be generated if the radial flux density in the air gap is sinusoidal
  • →This can be expressed in complex (phasor) form — pure sinusoid enables phasor analysis
② Flat-Top vs Sinusoidal Distribution
  • →Uniform (same) air gap → flat-topped B distribution (like square wave)
  • →Variable air gap (min at pole centre) → sinusoidal B distribution
  • →Sinusoidal B → sinusoidal EMF → phasor analysis valid
Fig 3.4 — Flat-topped flux density (uniform gap, orange dashed) vs sinusoidal B (shaped pole/variable turns, green solid). Sinusoidal B required for phasor analysis and clean EMF waveform.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.3; Chapman [1], §5.3
💡
Flux per Pole — Physical Meaning

Flux per pole (φ) is the total number of flux lines in a 1-pole pitch of the N-space — what an observer would count in total N-space across one pole pitch. It is the key quantity in EMF calculation: \(\phi = B_{avg} \times \text{Area/pole} = \frac{2}{\pi}B_m \times \frac{\pi DL}{P}\).

3.6

Induced EMF Equation

The derivation of the EMF equation starts from Faraday's law applied to the coil rotating in a sinusoidal flux field. The flux linkage through a phase winding varies sinusoidally as the rotor rotates.[2]

Derivation — From Flux Linkage to EMF

Consider phase winding aa' (one phase) with \(N_{ph}\) turns per phase. As the rotor field rotates, the flux through this phase varies as:

Step-by-Step EMF Derivation
Step 1 — Flux linkage: \(\lambda_{aa'} = N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \cos(\omega t)\) where φ = flux per pole Step 2 — Induced EMF (Faraday's Law): \(e_{aa'} = -\frac{d}{dt} (\lambda_{aa'})\) \(= -\frac{d}{dt} [N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \cos(\omega t)]\) \(= N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \omega \cdot \sin(\omega t)\) (if φ = constant, dφ/dt = 0) Step 3 — eaa' is speed voltage (for DC-excited synchronous machine): Since \(d\varphi/dt = 0\) for DC field excitation: \(e_{aa'} = N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \omega \cdot \sin(\omega t)\) \(= N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \omega \cdot \cos(\omega t - 90^\circ)\) Step 4 — Peak value of EMF: \(E_{peak} = N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \omega\) \((= N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot 2\pi f)\) Step 5 — RMS value (sinusoidal): \(E_{ph} = \frac{N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot \omega}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{N_{ph} \cdot \varphi \cdot 2\pi f}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}\pi f \cdot \varphi \cdot N_{ph}\) \(\therefore E_{ph} = \sqrt{2} \cdot \pi \cdot f \cdot \varphi \cdot N_{ph} \text{ volts/phase}\) Validity: This formula is valid for full-pitched, concentrated winding only.
ℹ️
Where does ω = 2πf come from?

The angular velocity of the rotor is \(\omega = 2\pi f\) (electrical radians/second). For \(P\) poles: \(f = PN/120\) where N is in RPM. Thus \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times PN/120 = \pi PN/60\). The EMF equation uses electrical frequency \(f\), not mechanical speed directly.

Flux per Pole Calculation

The flux per pole for a sinusoidal B distribution is the average value over a pole pitch (1 pole pitch = πDL/P area):

Flux per Pole
\(\varphi = B_{avg} \times \text{Area/pole} = \left(\frac{2}{\pi} \times B_m\right) \times \left(\frac{\pi \cdot D \cdot L}{P}\right)\) where: Bm = peak (maximum) flux density in air gap (T) D = mean diameter of air gap (m) L = effective axial length of machine (m) P = number of poles Therefore: \(\varphi = \left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right) \cdot B_m \times \left(\frac{\pi D L}{P}\right) = \frac{2 \cdot B_m \cdot D \cdot L}{P} \text{ Wb/pole}\)
Fig 3.5 — EMF waveform: eaa' leads flux linkage λaa' by 90°. For DC-excited synchronous machine, dφ/dt = 0, so only speed voltage exists. RMS value Eph = √2πfφNph (concentrated full-pitch winding).
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.3, p.371; Chapman [1], §5.3
3.7

Winding Factors — Chorded & Distributed Winding

In practice, stator armature windings are distributed (coil sides spread across multiple slots) and chorded (coil span less than a full pole pitch). Both reduce harmonics but also reduce the fundamental EMF — the reduction is quantified by winding factors.[2]

Chorded Winding — Pitch Factor (Coil Span Factor)

Fig 3.6 — Chorded winding: the two coil side EMFs (E) are no longer in phase (angle α between them). Resultant Eres = 2E·cos(α/2). Pitch factor kc = cos(α/2) for fundamental.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.3; Chapman [1], §5.3
Pitch Factor (Chording Factor) — Complete Formulas
Chording angle α: \(\alpha = \text{(full pitch} - \text{coil span)} \text{ in electrical degrees}\) Practical value: \(\alpha = 30^\circ\) electrical → \(k_c = \cos 15^\circ = 0.9659\) Pitch factor for fundamental: \(k_c = \cos(\alpha/2)\) Pitch factor for nth harmonic: \(k_{cn} = \cos(n\alpha/2)\) [replace α by nα] To eliminate nth harmonic: \(k_{cn} = 0 \implies n\alpha/2 = 90^\circ \implies\) \(\alpha = 180^\circ/n\) For 3rd harmonic: \(\alpha = 60^\circ\) (not practical — too much Cu wasted) For 5th harmonic: \(\alpha = 36^\circ \implies \text{use } \alpha \approx 30^\circ\) (approximation) For 7th harmonic: \(\alpha = 180^\circ/7 \approx 25.7^\circ\) EMF (chorded, concentrated winding): \(E_{ph} = k_c \cdot \sqrt{2}\pi f \cdot \varphi \cdot N_{ph} \text{ volts/phase}\)

Distributed Winding — Distribution Factor

When the phase winding is spread over multiple slots per pole per phase (instead of being concentrated in one slot), the individual slot EMFs are not in phase with each other — they are displaced by the slot angle β. The phasor sum is less than the arithmetic sum, and this reduction is the distribution factor.[2]

Fig 3.7 — Distribution factor: m slot EMFs (equal magnitude E, phase-displaced by β each) are phasor-added. Eres = 2R·sin(mβ/2), arithmetic sum = mE. Ratio kd = Eres/(mE) = sin(mβ/2)/[m·sin(β/2)].
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.3; Chapman [1], §5.3
Complete Winding Factor Formulas
Slot angle β: \(\beta = 180^\circ / (\text{slots per pole}) \text{ electrical}\) [for fundamental] \(\beta = \frac{P}{2} \times \frac{360^\circ}{\text{total slots}} \text{ mechanical} = \frac{180^\circ}{S/P} \text{ electrical}\) Slots per pole per phase: \(m = \frac{S}{P \times \text{phases}} = \frac{S/P}{3}\) Distribution factor (fundamental): \(k_d = \frac{\sin(m\beta/2)}{m \cdot \sin(\beta/2)}\) Approximate (when β is small): \(k_d \approx \frac{\sin(\text{phase spread}/2)}{\text{phase spread}/2}\) where phase spread = \(m\beta\) For nth harmonic: \(k_{dn} = \frac{\sin(m \cdot n\beta/2)}{m \cdot \sin(n\beta/2)}\) Winding factor: \(k_w = k_c \times k_d\) (also written \(k_0\)) EMF equation — General (distributed + chorded winding): \(E_{ph} = k_c \cdot k_d \cdot \sqrt{2}\pi f \cdot \varphi \cdot N_{ph} = k_w \cdot \sqrt{2}\pi f \cdot \varphi \cdot N_{ph} \text{ volts/phase}\) Key insight: Distributed + chorded winding is mainly used for REDUCTION of HARMONICS. Belt harmonics (m=3 system): 5th and 7th order — reduced by distribution & chording.
⚠️
Belt Harmonics — Important for GATE/IES

For a 3-phase system (m=3), the belt harmonics are of order 2m ± 1 = 5th and 7th. These are called belt harmonics and are the most significant harmonics in practice. Both distributed and chorded windings significantly reduce these. Chording angle α = 30° (practical standard) is chosen to effectively reduce the 5th harmonic (need α ≈ 36°) while keeping copper usage reasonable.

3.8

Armature Reaction

When the armature (stator) carries current, it produces its own magnetic field (MMF). The effect of this armature MMF on the main field (produced by the rotor field winding) is called armature reaction. It is defined as: the effect of armature MMF on the main flux.[2]

🔑
Important Principle

Behind every generator action there is a motoring action. This is why the same machine analysed for generator action (trailing tip concentration) has motoring action (leading tip concentration) simultaneously.

Types of Armature Reaction by Power Factor

Fig 3.8 — Armature reaction at different PF. FF = field MMF (rotor), Far = armature MMF (stator), FR = resultant. Unity PF → cross-magnetising; ZPF lag → demagnetising; ZPF lead → magnetising.
Basis: Nagrath & Kothari [2], §8.4; Chapman [1], §5.4

🚫
GATE Trap — Generator vs Motor Armature Reaction

A very common GATE question: "In a synchronous generator at lagging PF, armature reaction is —?" Answer: demagnetising + cross-magnetising (partially). For pure ZPF lag, it is purely demagnetising. For pure ZPF lead, it is purely magnetising. At unity PF, it is purely cross-magnetising. In motors, lagging PF current leads to magnetising AR (opposite to generator).

Worked Examples

EX 3.1EMF Calculation — Cylindrical Rotor Turbogenerator (Standard GATE Type)
A 3-phase, 2-pole, 3000 RPM, \(\lambda\)-connected cylindrical rotor turbogenerator has: No. of slots = 60, maximum flux density in air gap = 1.32 T, mean air gap diameter = 1.12 m, effective axial length = 3 m, No. of turns/phase = 10, coil span = 150°. Calculate the line-to-line no-load voltage.
SOLUTION

Given: P = 2, N = 3000 RPM, S = 60 slots, Bm = 1.32 T, D = 1.12 m, L = 3 m, Nph = 10 turns/phase, coil span = 150°

Step 1 — Chording angle:

\[\alpha = 180° - 150° = 30° \text{ electrical}\]

\[k_c = \cos\frac{\alpha}{2} = \cos 15° = 0.9659\]

Step 2 — Distribution factor:

\[\beta = \frac{P}{2} \times \frac{360°}{\text{slots}} = \frac{2}{2} \times \frac{360°}{60} = 6° \text{ (electrical)}\]

\[m = \frac{S/P}{\text{phases}} = \frac{60/2}{3} = 10 \text{ slots/pole/phase}\]

\[k_d = \frac{\sin\frac{m\beta}{2}}{m\sin\frac{\beta}{2}} = \frac{\sin\frac{10 \times 6°}{2}}{10\sin\frac{6°}{2}} = \frac{\sin 30°}{10\sin 3°} = \frac{0.5}{10 \times 0.05234} = 0.9554\]

Step 3 — Flux per pole:

\[\phi = \frac{2}{\pi} B_m \times \frac{\pi D L}{P} = \frac{2}{\pi} \times 1.32 \times \frac{\pi \times 1.12 \times 3}{2} = \left(\frac{2}{\pi} \times 1.32\right) \times \frac{\pi \times 1.12 \times 3}{2}\]

\[\phi = \frac{2 \times 1.32 \times 1.12 \times 3}{2} = 4.4352 \text{ Wb/pole}\]

Step 4 — Phase EMF:

\[E_{ph} = k_c k_d \sqrt{2}\pi f \phi N_{ph} = 0.9659 \times 0.9554 \times \sqrt{2} \times \pi \times 50 \times 4.4352 \times 10\]

\[E_{ph} = 9092.13 \text{ V per phase (line-to-neutral)}\]

Step 5 — Line voltage:

\[E_{L-L} = \sqrt{3} \times E_{ph} = \sqrt{3} \times 9092.13 \approx \mathbf{15.75 \text{ kV}}\]

✓

This matches the nameplate data of the 247 MVA turbogenerator example — confirming the calculation. The 15.75 kV line voltage is the standard output of large turbo-alternators connected to step-up transformers.

EX 3.2Synchronous Speed & Frequency Calculation
A 247 MVA, 0.85 PF lagging, 3-phase generator runs at 8000 RPM at 50 Hz. How many poles does it have? Also find the synchronous speed for a 12-pole hydro-generator at 50 Hz.
SOLUTION

For turbogenerator: \(\quad f = \frac{PN}{120} \Rightarrow P = \frac{120f}{N} = \frac{120 \times 50}{8000} = 0.75\)

This is not an integer! Actual turbogenerators run at 3000 RPM (2-pole) or 1500 RPM (4-pole). 8000 RPM is likely a misread; standard large turbogenerators are 2-pole at 3000 RPM for 50 Hz (P×N = 6000 → P=2, N=3000 RPM).

For 12-pole hydro-generator: \[\quad N_s = \frac{120f}{P} = \frac{120 \times 50}{12} = \mathbf{500 \text{ RPM}}\]

This is typical for large hydro-generators — slow speed, many poles, large diameter.

GATE & IES Previous Year Questions

GATE EEDistribution factor of a winding with m slots per pole per phase and slot angle β is:GATE EE 2019
  • (A) sin(mβ/2) / [m · sin(β/2)]
  • (B) sin(mβ/2) / [m · sin(β/2)] ✓
  • (C) m · sin(β/2) / sin(mβ/2)
  • (D) cos(mβ/2) / [m · cos(β/2)]

Explanation: The distribution factor is the ratio of phasor sum to arithmetic sum of slot EMFs. Phasor sum = 2R·sin(mβ/2), arithmetic sum = m × 2R·sin(β/2). Thus \(k_d = \frac{\sin(m\beta/2)}{m\sin(\beta/2)}\). This formula is derived from the geometric phasor polygon inscribed in a circle of radius R.

GATE EEFor a 3-phase synchronous generator, when the armature current is at zero power factor (lagging), the nature of armature reaction is:GATE EE 2016
  • (A) Cross-magnetising
  • (B) Magnetising
  • (C) Demagnetising ✓
  • (D) Neither magnetising nor demagnetising

Explanation: At zero PF lagging, armature current lags the induced EMF by 90°. The armature MMF (Far) is directly opposed to the field MMF (FF) along the d-axis, reducing the net resultant flux. This is purely demagnetising armature reaction. At unity PF, it is cross-magnetising; at zero PF leading, it is magnetising.

IESWhat is the pitch factor for a coil span of 150° in a synchronous machine?IES 2018
  • (A) cos 30°
  • (B) cos 15° = 0.9659 ✓
  • (C) sin 30°
  • (D) cos 75°

Explanation: Chording angle α = 180° − 150° = 30°. Pitch factor \(k_c = \cos\frac{\alpha}{2} = \cos\frac{30°}{2} = \cos 15° = 0.9659\). This is the standard practical value used for chorded windings to reduce 5th harmonic while keeping the fundamental EMF reduction to only ~3.4%.

GATE EEA salient pole synchronous machine has Xd = 1.0 pu and Xq = 0.6 pu. Which type of rotor does it have?GATE EE 2021

A salient pole machine has Xd ≠ Xq (direct-axis reactance ≠ quadrature-axis reactance) because the air gap is non-uniform — smaller along the d-axis (under poles) and larger along the q-axis (between poles). For a cylindrical rotor machine, Xd = Xq since the air gap is uniform everywhere.

The question directly identifies a salient pole rotor machine (also called projecting pole / non-cylindrical rotor). The ratio Xd/Xq typically ranges from 1.5 to 2.5 for salient pole machines.

IESIn a synchronous machine, the frequency of generated EMF depends on:IES 2015
  • (A) Speed only
  • (B) Field current only
  • (C) Speed and number of poles ✓
  • (D) Speed, number of poles, and field current

Explanation: \(f = PN/120\) — frequency depends only on speed (N, RPM) and number of poles (P). Field current affects the magnitude of the induced EMF (through flux φ), not the frequency. The waveform shape and harmonics can also be affected by winding arrangement, but frequency is solely determined by PN/120.

GATE EEIn a 3-phase AC machine, to eliminate the 5th harmonic from the induced EMF, the coil span should be approximately:GATE EE 2014
  • (A) 180°
  • (B) 144°
  • (C) 144° ✓ (= 4/5 of full pitch)
  • (D) 120°

Explanation: To eliminate the 5th harmonic, pitch factor \(k_{c5} = 0\). Thus \(\cos\frac{5\alpha}{2} = 0 \Rightarrow 5\alpha/2 = 90° \Rightarrow \alpha = 36°\). Coil span = 180° − α = 180° − 36° = 144°. This is 4/5 of full pitch (180°). In practice, α ≈ 30° (coil span = 150°) is used as a compromise, which significantly reduces both 5th and 7th harmonics without excessive copper usage.

Chapter Summary

Synchronous Speed

f = PN/120. For 50 Hz: PN = 6000. Rotor always runs at exactly synchronous speed — any deviation → loss of synchronism.

EMF Equation

Eph = √2πfφNph (concentrated full-pitch). With winding factor: Eph = kw·√2πfφNph. EL = √3·Eph for star.

Pitch Factor

kc = cos(α/2) where α = chording angle. Practical: α = 30°, kc = 0.9659. Eliminates nth harmonic when α = 180°/n.

Distribution Factor

kd = sin(mβ/2)/[m·sin(β/2)]. β = slot angle = 180°/(S/P). m = slots/pole/phase. Reduces harmonics.

Armature Reaction

Unity PF: cross-magnetising. ZPF lag: demagnetising (gen overexcited). ZPF lead: magnetising (gen underexcited). Motor: opposite.

Rotor Types

Cylindrical: uniform gap, high speed, Xd = Xq. Salient: non-uniform gap, low speed, Xd ≠ Xq (1.5–2.5 ratio). Used for turbogenerators and hydro-alternators respectively.
