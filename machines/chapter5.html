1.1

Why DC Generators Exist — The Big Picture

Every electromechanical energy conversion device works on one universal principle: a conductor moving through a magnetic field has an EMF induced in it (Faraday's Law), and a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force (Lorentz Law). A DC generator uses the first principle — it converts mechanical energy supplied by a prime mover (diesel engine, turbine, hand crank) into electrical energy in the form of direct current.

Before large-scale AC transmission became practical, DC generators were the backbone of every power system. Today they remain essential wherever a stable, controllable DC supply is required: battery charging stations, electroplating and electrolysis plants, DC traction sub-stations, welding sets, excitation systems for large alternators, and as the core teaching machine that builds the foundation for understanding all rotating electrical machines — including the DC motor, induction motor, and synchronous machine.

⚡
Engineering Insight

A DC generator and a DC motor are constructionally identical — the same machine. Whether it behaves as a generator or a motor depends only on whether mechanical energy is being fed in (generator action) or electrical energy is being fed in (motor action). This reversibility is why understanding generator construction thoroughly makes the motor chapter almost free.

Fig 1.1 — A DC generator converts mechanical input into DC electrical output via electromagnetic induction. The same machine run in reverse is a DC motor.

1.2

Construction & Working Principle

A DC machine has two electrically distinct circuits — the stationary field system (produces the magnetic flux) and the rotating armature (where EMF is induced). A commutator with carbon brushes performs mechanical rectification, converting the alternating EMF generated in the rotating armature conductors into a unidirectional (DC) output at the terminals.

Fig 1.2 — Cross-section of a 4-pole DC machine: yoke, salient poles (N-S-N-S), field windings, armature core with embedded conductors, shaft, and commutator-brush assembly.

Main Constructional Parts

Part Material Function
Yoke (frame) Cast steel / rolled steel Mechanical support; provides path for magnetic flux; protective outer cover
Pole core & pole shoe Laminated/solid cast steel Pole shoe spreads flux over the air gap and reduces its reluctance
Field winding Copper wire wound on pole core Carries excitation current; produces the working magnetic flux Φ
Armature core Laminated silicon steel Houses armature conductors; laminated to reduce eddy current loss
Armature winding Copper conductors in slots EMF is induced here as it rotates through the field flux
Commutator Hard-drawn copper segments Mechanical rectifier — converts induced AC in armature to DC at brushes
Brushes Carbon / graphite Stationary contact that collects current from the rotating commutator

Working Principle

When the armature is rotated by the prime mover inside the stationary magnetic field, each armature conductor cuts the flux lines and an EMF is induced in it as per Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. Since the conductor alternately passes under a North pole and then a South pole, the EMF induced in any one conductor is alternating in nature. The commutator — rotating along with the armature, but always in contact with stationary brushes positioned at the magnetic neutral axis — switches the connection at exactly the right instant so that the current delivered to the external circuit through the brushes is unidirectional (DC), even though the EMF inside each individual armature coil is still AC.

🔑
Core Idea

The armature conductors always generate AC EMF. The commutator-brush assembly is purely a mechanical rectifier. This is the single most important conceptual fact about DC machines, frequently tested as a conceptual GATE question.

Essential Parts for Generator Action

Three conditions must exist simultaneously for a generator to produce EMF, summarised by Faraday's Law in the motional EMF form:

Motional EMF — Faraday's Law
$$e = Blv \sin\theta$$

where B is the flux density (T), l is the active conductor length in the field (m), v is the relative velocity between conductor and field (m/s), and θ is the angle between the conductor's velocity and the flux direction. For maximum EMF, the conductor must cut flux at 90° (θ = 90°). The three essentials are therefore:

Three Essentials
  • ①A magnetic field (produced by field winding)
  • ②A conductor (armature winding)
  • ③Relative motion between field and conductor
Right-Hand Rule (Generator)
  • ▸Thumb → direction of motion of conductor
  • ▸Forefinger → direction of field (N→S)
  • ▸Middle finger → direction of induced EMF/current
1.3

EMF Equation of a DC Generator

The EMF equation connects the physical and electrical design parameters of the machine — flux per pole, speed, number of poles, number of armature conductors, and number of parallel paths — into a single expression for the generated EMF. This is the single most numerically tested formula in the entire DC machines syllabus.

Step 1 — EMF Induced in One Conductor per Revolution

Consider a conductor that sweeps through the entire flux of one pole, Φ webers, in one revolution. If the armature rotates at N revolutions per minute, the time taken to complete one revolution is 60/N seconds. By Faraday's law, average EMF induced per conductor equals the rate of change of flux linkage:

Average EMF per conductor
$$e = \frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{P\phi}{60/N} = \frac{\phi P N}{60}\ \text{volts}$$

Here P is the number of poles, because the conductor cuts the flux of all P poles (each contributing Φ) once every revolution.

Step 2 — EMF per Parallel Path

The total number of armature conductors is Z, arranged into A parallel paths. Each parallel path therefore contains Z/A conductors connected in series, and the generated EMF of the path is the sum of the EMFs of these series conductors:

$$e_{\text{path}} = \frac{\phi P N}{60} \times \frac{Z}{A}$$

Step 3 — Final EMF Equation

Since all parallel paths are identical and connected to the same pair of brushes, the generator's output (terminal) EMF equals the EMF of one parallel path:

EMF Equation of a DC Generator
$$\boxed{E_g = \frac{\phi Z N P}{60 A}\ \text{volts}}$$
Symbol definitions Φ = flux per pole (Wb) Z = total number of armature conductors N = armature speed (rpm) P = number of poles A = number of parallel paths A = 2 for simplex wave winding (always 2, independent of poles) A = P for simplex lap winding (= number of poles)
✓
Physical Interpretation

Eg is directly proportional to flux Φ and speed N — everything else (Z, P, A) is fixed once the machine is built. This single fact explains almost all generator characteristic curves and voltage build-up behaviour discussed later in this chapter.

Proportionality Relation — The Most Useful GATE Tool

Since Z, P, A are constants for a given machine, the EMF equation reduces to a simple proportionality that is extremely useful for solving "before–after" type numericals without recomputing the full formula:

$$E_g \propto \phi N \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{E_{g1}}{E_{g2}} = \frac{\phi_1}{\phi_2}\cdot\frac{N_1}{N_2}$$

Two important special cases, frequently used directly in GATE/IES numericals:

If Speed N is Constant
$$\frac{E_{g2}}{E_{g1}} = \frac{\phi_2}{\phi_1}$$
If Flux Φ is Constant
$$\frac{E_{g2}}{E_{g1}} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}$$

Concept-Building Questions

❓
Why does this happen?

Q: What if the number of parallel paths A increases (re-winding from lap to wave, or vice-versa)? EMF is inversely proportional to A. Increasing A (more parallel paths) reduces voltage per path but increases the current-carrying capacity (since current divides among more paths). This is the classic lap vs wave trade-off: lap winding (A = P) → high current, low voltage machines; wave winding (A = 2) → high voltage, low current machines.

Common Student Mistakes

⚠ Frequent Errors
  • ✗Forgetting to convert N to rpm consistently (some texts use N in rps — always check units before substituting)
  • ✗Using total conductors Z instead of conductors per path (Z/A) when computing series-path EMF
  • ✗Assuming A = P always — true only for lap winding; for wave winding A = 2 regardless of P
  • ✗Confusing Φ (flux per pole) with total flux from all poles
1.4

Classification of DC Generators by Excitation

To control a DC machine's electromagnets, the poles require a DC voltage source to excite (energise) the field winding. Generators are classified entirely according to how this excitation current is supplied to the field winding. This single criterion produces the complete family tree of DC generators used throughout this chapter and the GATE/IES syllabus.

Fig 1.3 — Complete classification tree of DC generators based on method of field excitation. Compound generators further split into Long Shunt and Short Shunt connections (Section 1.7).

Two Broad Categories

Separate Excitation
  • ▸Field winding excited from an independent external DC source
  • ▸Field and armature circuits are electrically isolated
  • ▸Rarely used commercially — needs an extra DC source
  • ▸~95% of practical DC generators are self-excited
Self Excitation
  • ▸Field winding excited by the armature's own output
  • ▸Requires residual flux in poles to "self-start"
  • ▸Sub-types: Shunt, Series, Compound
  • ▸Compound further divides into Long Shunt / Short Shunt
🔑
General EMF Balance Equation (applies to every type)

For every DC generator, regardless of excitation type, the relation between generated EMF Eg, armature current Ia, armature resistance Ra, brush contact drop (BCD), and terminal voltage V is always:

$$E_g = I_a R_a + \text{BCD} + V$$

This single equation, applied with the correct expression for Ia in each connection type, is the basis of every numerical problem in this chapter.

1.5

Separately Excited Generator

In a separately excited generator, the field winding is connected to an independent DC source (a battery, or another small generator called an exciter) rather than to the generator's own armature. This means the field current If and hence the flux Φ can be controlled completely independently of the load on the armature.

Fig 1.4 — Separately excited DC generator. Field current If is supplied from an independent source Vf, completely isolated from the armature circuit. Armature current Ia equals load current IL directly.

Key Characteristics

Defining Features
  • ▸Requires a separate DC source for excitation
  • ▸Field and armature windings are electrically isolated
  • ▸Terminal voltage V across the load does not affect excitation in any way — Φ stays fixed regardless of loading
  • ▸Due to the additional voltage source requirement, this type is rarely used in practice; most DC generators are self-excited
Governing Equations
$$I_a = I_L$$
$$E_g = I_a R_a + \text{BCD} = V$$
⚙️
Where It Is Actually Used

Despite being rare for stand-alone power generation, separate excitation is essential wherever precise, load-independent flux control is needed — for example, in Ward-Leonard speed control systems for DC motors, and as pilot exciters for large alternators in power plants.

1.6

Self-Excited Generators — Shunt, Series & Compound

In a self-excited generator, the field winding draws its excitation current from the machine's own armature output — there is no external DC source. This is only possible because the poles retain a small amount of residual flux from previous operation, which lets the machine "bootstrap" its own voltage (explained fully in Section 1.9). Based on how the field winding is connected relative to the armature, three self-excited types exist.

Shunt Generator

The field winding (high resistance, many turns of thin wire) is connected directly in parallel with the armature, across the brushes — the terminal voltage itself drives the field current.

Fig 1.5 — Shunt generator: field winding in parallel with armature. Range of shunt field resistance Rsh ≈ 50–250 Ω (high resistance, thin wire, many turns).
Shunt Generator Equations
$$E_g = I_a R_a + \text{BCD} = V \qquad I_a = I_L + I_{sh} \qquad I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}}$$
✓
Key Property — "Voltage-Operated Field"

Since field current depends on terminal voltage V (which stays roughly constant from no-load to full-load), the field current and hence flux remain approximately constant across the operating region. This is why a shunt generator is also called a voltage-operated field machine, and why loading any machine is equivalent to reducing the external circuit resistance.

Series Generator

The field winding (low resistance, few turns of thick wire) is connected in series with the armature and the load — the same current flows through all three.

Fig 1.6 — Series generator: field winding (few turns, thick conductor) in series with armature and load, so the entire load current flows through the field.
Series Generator Equations
$$E_g = I_a(R_a + R_{se}) - \text{BCD} = V \qquad I_a = I_L = I_{se}$$
⚡
Key Property — "Current-Operated Field"

Field current equals load current directly, so flux varies strongly with load — at no-load (IL = 0), Ise = 0 and there is almost no flux (only residual), giving almost zero terminal voltage. This is why series generators are unsuitable as general power sources and are mainly used for special applications like series boosters.

Compound Generator

A compound generator has both a shunt field winding (many turns, high resistance) and a series field winding (few turns, thick wire) on the same pole — combining the constant-flux behaviour of shunt excitation with load-responsive boosting from series excitation.

Fig 1.7 — Compound pole: shunt winding (fine wire, many turns) and series winding (thick wire, few turns) share the same pole core. In the cumulative mode, currents in both windings produce flux in the same direction — fluxes add.
Cumulative Compounding
  • ▸Series and shunt fluxes act in the same direction on the pole core
  • ▸Total flux increases with load (Φ = Φsh + Φse)
  • ▸Used to compensate for voltage drop with load — practically the most useful compounding type
Differential Compounding
  • ▸If the series winding is reversed, its flux opposes the shunt flux
  • ▸Total flux decreases with load (Φ = Φsh − Φse)
  • ▸Used in special applications like DC arc-welding generators needing a steeply drooping characteristic
🔑
Remember

Shunt flux always dominates series flux in a properly designed compound machine — the series winding only fine-tunes (boosts or bucks) the dominant shunt flux, it never reverses the overall polarity of the pole.

1.7

Long Shunt & Short Shunt Compound Generators

A compound generator can connect its shunt field winding across the armature in two slightly different ways relative to the series field winding — giving rise to the Long Shunt and Short Shunt connections. The naming refers to whether the shunt field winding is connected across (parallel to) just the armature, or across the armature plus series winding combined.

Fig 1.8 — Long shunt: shunt field connected across (armature + series field). Short shunt: shunt field connected across armature alone, with the series field carrying the line current IL.

Governing Equations

Long Shunt
$$E_g = I_a(R_a + R_{se}) - \text{BCD} = V$$
$$I_a = I_L + I_{sh}$$
$$I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}}$$
Short Shunt
$$E_g - I_a R_a - I_L R_{se} - \text{BCD} = V$$
$$I_a = I_{sh} + I_L$$
$$I_{sh} = \frac{V + I_L R_{se}}{R_{sh}}$$
✓
There Is No Major Practical Difference

For all practical purposes there is no distinctive performance difference between long shunt and short shunt generators of the same rating — the series field resistance Rse is typically very small compared to Rsh, so the small difference in where exactly the shunt field taps off barely affects performance. The induced EMF in a long shunt connection is marginally greater than that of an equivalent short shunt machine since Ia > IL in long shunt (extra Ish flows through Rse too) — but the difference is negligible in practice. Long shunt is far more commonly used and almost always assumed unless stated otherwise.

1.8

Compounding — Adjusting the Degree

Compounding means adjusting the terminal voltage behaviour of a cumulative compound generator by varying the effective series field ampere-turns — typically done by connecting a variable resistor called a diverter in parallel with the series field winding. Some of the series current bypasses through the diverter instead of the series winding, reducing the effective series ampere-turns and hence the series flux contribution.

Fig 1.9 — Compounding by diverter: varying the diverter resistance Rd from 0 to its maximum value sweeps the generator through five distinct compounding modes.

Five Modes Achieved by Varying Diverter Resistance

Diverter Rd Mode Behaviour
Rd = 0 Shunt All series current bypassed; machine behaves as pure shunt generator
Rd ↑ (small) Under-compounded Full-load voltage < no-load voltage (series boost insufficient)
Rd ↑↑ (medium) Flat / Level Compounded Full-load voltage = no-load voltage exactly
Rd ↑↑↑ (large) Over-compounded Full-load voltage > no-load voltage (series boost is excessive)
Rd → ∞ (open) Basic Cumulative Entire series current flows through the field winding (no diverter path)
🔑
Flat Compound — The Design Target

In a flat (level) compound generator, the full-load induced EMF is exactly equal to the rated no-load voltage, because the series flux is precisely adjusted to compensate for all internal voltage drops (armature resistance drop + brush contact drop) at rated load — this is the most commonly desired compounding for general-purpose DC supply, since it maintains nearly constant terminal voltage from no-load to full-load.

⚠
Important Exception

Compounding cannot be performed on a differentially compound generator, because there its series flux already opposes the shunt flux — adding a diverter only further weakens an already-reducing flux and cannot recreate the rising/flat characteristics that compounding modes describe.

1.9

Voltage Build-Up in Self-Excited Generators

This is conceptually the richest topic in the chapter, and one of the most frequently tested ideas in GATE EE. The central question: if a self-excited generator has no external source for its field, how does it generate any voltage at all the first time it is switched on? The answer lies in a small amount of magnetism — residual flux — permanently trapped in the pole material from earlier magnetisation, and a self-reinforcing (cumulative) process that builds on it.

The Build-Up Process — Step by Step

Consider a shunt generator run at rated speed for the very first time. The poles retain a small residual flux ΦR (typically 2–3% of rated flux) from the last time the machine was magnetised at the factory. The build-up proceeds as a chain reaction:

Fig 1.10 — Voltage build-up loop. Residual flux → small EMF → small field current → reinforces flux → larger EMF → repeats, eventually flattening out at the intersection point on the OCC, as the iron core saturates.
Build-up sequence (matches the standard textbook derivation) Φ_R → E_R → I_R (residual stage) I_R·N_sh → Φ_1+Φ_R → E_1+E_R → I_1+I_R (first reinforcement) (I_1+I_R)·N_sh → Φ_2+Φ_1+Φ_R → E_2+E_1+E_R → I_2+I_1+I_R (process continues...)

Each cycle, the new field current — being slightly larger than before — produces slightly more flux, which induces slightly more EMF, which drives slightly more field current. This is a cumulative process. It does not run away to infinity: as current increases, the pole iron approaches magnetic saturation, so equal increments of field current produce progressively smaller increments of flux. Eventually flux essentially stops increasing even though current keeps rising — the voltage curve flattens, and the build-up settles at a stable operating point.

⚙️
Engineering Insight — Magnetic Inertia

The open-circuit characteristic (OCC) is essentially the B–H curve of the magnetic circuit redrawn in terms of E0 vs If. It rises almost linearly at first, then bends over near the knee point as saturation sets in, and may even droop slightly at very high field current due to armature reaction and magnetic hysteresis effects.

Open-Circuit Characteristic (OCC) and the Field Resistance Line

Fig 1.11 — The field resistance line Rsh intersects the OCC at point P, fixing the steady no-load voltage OQ. The critical resistance line Rc is the tangent to the OCC from the origin — beyond this slope, the generator cannot build up voltage.

The straight line drawn from the origin with slope equal to the field circuit resistance Rsh intersects the OCC curve at point P. The corresponding voltage OQ is the steady no-load terminal voltage the generator settles at. As Rsh is increased (steeper line), point P moves down the curve and the build-up voltage falls — until the line becomes tangent to the initial linear part of the OCC. At and beyond this tangent slope, the line never properly intersects the curved (saturated) part of OCC, and the machine fails to build up any useful voltage.

Critical Resistance Rc

The resistance of the field circuit above which the generator fails to build up voltage at a given speed. Geometrically, Rc is the tangent to the OCC drawn from the origin.

$$R_c = \frac{R_T \cdot V}{O_T \cdot A}$$
Critical Speed Nc

For a fixed field resistance, the speed below which the generator fails to build up voltage. Found by scaling: if (ST) and (RT) are intercepts on two OCCs at different speeds,

$$N_c = \frac{ST}{RT}\times N$$

The Five (and a Half) Essential Conditions for Voltage Build-Up

Required Conditions
  • ①Residual flux must be present in the poles
  • ②Field winding must be connected so its MMF reinforces (not opposes) the residual flux
  • ③Operating speed must be ≥ critical speed Nc
  • ④Field circuit resistance must be ≤ critical resistance Rc
  • ⑤Best condition: machine should build up on no-load (open circuit) first, then load can be applied
  • ⑥Brush–commutator contact must be sound — even with all the above satisfied, poor brush contact alone can prevent build-up

Effect of Reversing Connections — A Classic GATE Conceptual Trap

Scenario Result Reason
Normal connection, normal rotation Builds up normally Field MMF reinforces residual flux as expected
Field winding reversed only No build-up Field MMF now opposes residual flux — cancels it instead of reinforcing it
Direction of rotation reversed only Builds up with reversed polarity Eg ∝ Φ(−N) gives a negative EMF — same magnitude, opposite sign
Both rotation and field winding reversed Builds up normally (+ve) Eg ∝ (−Φ)(−N) = +ve — two negatives cancel, normal polarity restored
✓
Memory Trick

Think of it like multiplying signs: Eg ∝ Φ·N. Reversing only one of (Φ, N) flips the sign of Eg (reversed polarity, but it still builds up). Reversing both restores normal polarity. Reversing the field winding alone is the only case that actually kills build-up entirely, because it directly cancels the residual flux rather than just flipping the output sign.

1.10

Characteristics of DC Generators

Three characteristic curves describe the complete behaviour of any DC generator, relating EMF, terminal voltage, field current, and load current.

Characteristic Plotted As Reveals
Open Circuit (Magnetisation, OCC) E0 vs If at constant N, no load Magnetic saturation behaviour of the iron — the "B-H curve" of the machine
Internal (Total) Eg vs Ia True generated EMF, falls slightly due to armature reaction with load
External (Load / Performance) V vs IL What the customer actually sees — terminal voltage drop with increasing load
Fig 1.12 — Comparative load characteristics. Shunt generator droops steadily with load. Series generator rises steeply then saturates/droops. Compound generator's slope depends entirely on its degree of compounding.

Why Shunt Generator Voltage Droops with Load

Three independent effects all act in the same direction to reduce shunt generator terminal voltage as load current increases:

Three Causes of Voltage Drop
  • ①Armature resistance drop IaRa increases directly with load current
  • ②Armature reaction — the armature's own MMF distorts and slightly weakens the main flux at higher loads
  • ③Reduced field current — as V falls (due to ① and ②), Ish = V/Rsh also falls, weakening flux further — a compounding (self-reinforcing) drop
⚠
Critical Load Resistance for Series Generator

A series generator's V-IL curve rises initially (more current → more flux → more EMF, dominating over the Ia(Ra+Rse) drop) but eventually the curve bends over as the iron saturates, and finally droops because resistive drop eventually outpaces the (now nearly constant) EMF rise. There exists a load resistance below which V actually starts to fall even though IL keeps rising — an important qualitative GATE concept.

Solved Numericals

EX 1.1 EMF equation — basic application, lap vs wave winding
A 4-pole DC generator has an armature with 250 conductors. It is driven at 1000 rpm and the flux per pole is 0.02 Wb. Calculate the generated EMF when the armature is (a) lap wound, and (b) wave wound.
Solution

Given: P = 4, Z = 250, N = 1000 rpm, Φ = 0.02 Wb

(a) Lap winding: A = P = 4

$$E_g = \frac{\phi Z N P}{60A} = \frac{0.02 \times 250 \times 1000 \times 4}{60 \times 4} = \frac{20000}{60} = 333.3\ \text{V}$$

(b) Wave winding: A = 2 (always, regardless of poles)

$$E_g = \frac{0.02 \times 250 \times 1000 \times 4}{60 \times 2} = \frac{20000}{30} = 666.7\ \text{V}$$
✓
Key Observation

Wave winding gives exactly double the EMF of lap winding for the same machine here, because A is halved (4 → 2) while everything else stays the same. This is the direct, numerical consequence of Eg ∝ 1/A — a frequently tested relation.

EX 1.2 Shunt generator — find Eg, Ia from terminal data
A shunt generator delivers a load current of 50 A at a terminal voltage of 220 V. The armature resistance is 0.1 Ω and the shunt field resistance is 110 Ω. Neglect brush contact drop. Find the generated EMF.
Solution

Given: IL = 50 A, V = 220 V, Ra = 0.1 Ω, Rsh = 110 Ω

Step 1 — Shunt field current:

$$I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}} = \frac{220}{110} = 2\ \text{A}$$

Step 2 — Armature current:

$$I_a = I_L + I_{sh} = 50 + 2 = 52\ \text{A}$$

Step 3 — Generated EMF:

$$E_g = I_a R_a + V = (52 \times 0.1) + 220 = 5.2 + 220 = 225.2\ \text{V}$$
✓
Common Mistake to Avoid

Many students use IL instead of Ia in the Eg equation — always remember that armature current includes the shunt field current too, since the field winding is fed directly off the armature terminals.

EX 1.3 Long shunt compound generator — full circuit numerical
A long-shunt compound generator supplies a load current of 100 A at 250 V. The armature, series field, and shunt field resistances are 0.05 Ω, 0.03 Ω, and 125 Ω respectively. Calculate the generated EMF, ignoring brush contact drop.
Solution

Given: IL = 100 A, V = 250 V, Ra = 0.05 Ω, Rse = 0.03 Ω, Rsh = 125 Ω

Step 1 — Shunt field current (long shunt: Rsh is across V directly):

$$I_{sh} = \frac{V}{R_{sh}} = \frac{250}{125} = 2\ \text{A}$$

Step 2 — Armature current (in long shunt, Ia flows through both Ra and Rse):

$$I_a = I_L + I_{sh} = 100 + 2 = 102\ \text{A}$$

Step 3 — Generated EMF:

$$E_g = I_a(R_a + R_{se}) + V = 102 \times (0.05 + 0.03) + 250 = 102 \times 0.08 + 250 = 8.16 + 250 = 258.16\ \text{V}$$
🔑
Pattern to Remember

In long shunt, both Ra and Rse carry the same current Ia, so they can simply be added together: Ra + Rse. This is exactly why "long shunt" is the easier of the two compound connections to solve numerically.

EX 1.4 Critical resistance — from OCC test data
An OCC test on a shunt generator at 1000 rpm gives the following readings: at If = 1 A, E0 = 60 V; at If = 2 A, E0 = 110 V (in the linear region before saturation). Estimate the critical resistance of the field circuit at this speed.
Solution

Given: Two points on the linear (unsaturated) part of OCC: (1 A, 60 V) and (2 A, 110 V)

The critical resistance is the slope of the OCC's initial straight-line (unsaturated) portion — the tangent line through the origin in this linear region:

$$R_c = \frac{\Delta E_0}{\Delta I_f} = \frac{110 - 60}{2 - 1} = \frac{50}{1} = 50\ \Omega$$

Verification using the second point alone (since this region is linear and passes near the origin):

$$R_c \approx \frac{E_0}{I_f} = \frac{110}{2} = 55\ \Omega\ \text{(close enough — confirms linear region)}$$
⚠
Practical Implication

If this generator's actual shunt field resistance Rsh exceeds ≈ 50–55 Ω at 1000 rpm, it will fail to build up voltage. To restore normal build-up, the field rheostat must be reduced, or the speed must be increased (since Rc ∝ N).

GATE EE Essential condition for voltage build-up in a self-excited shunt generator Concept

Statement: A self-excited DC shunt generator fails to build up voltage at rated speed. Which of the following is the LEAST likely cause?

  • (A) Residual flux has been lost from the poles
  • (B) Field winding connections are reversed, opposing residual flux
  • (C) Field circuit resistance exceeds the critical resistance
  • (D) Load resistance connected to the armature is too high

Explanation: Voltage build-up is governed entirely by the field circuit (residual flux, field winding polarity, field resistance vs critical resistance, and speed). It is independent of the external load — in fact, the best build-up condition is on open circuit with no load connected at all. A high or even infinite load resistance does not prevent build-up; only field-circuit issues do.

GATE EE EMF equation — effect of winding type on terminal voltage Concept

A DC generator is rewound from lap to wave winding, keeping Z, P, Φ and N unchanged. The new generated EMF compared to the original will be:

  • (A) Same as before
  • (B) Half of the original value
  • (C) P/2 times the original value
  • (D) 2/P times the original value

Explanation: For lap winding A = P; for wave winding A = 2. Since Eg ∝ 1/A, the ratio of new to old EMF is Aold/Anew = P/2. For a 4-pole machine this means a wave-wound EMF is exactly 2 times the lap-wound EMF — matching the result derived in Example 1.1.

IES E&T Reversal of rotation and field connections together Concept

A shunt generator that normally builds up voltage successfully has BOTH its direction of rotation AND its field winding connections reversed. What happens to the build-up?

  • (A) No voltage builds up at all
  • (B) Voltage builds up but with reversed polarity
  • (C) Voltage builds up normally, with original (correct) polarity
  • (D) Voltage builds up to only half the rated value

Explanation: Eg ∝ Φ × N. Reversing rotation makes N effectively negative; reversing field connections makes Φ effectively negative. The product of two negative quantities is positive, so Eg is restored to its original positive (correct-polarity) value — the generator builds up exactly as it did before either reversal.

GATE EE Cumulative vs differential compound generator — series flux direction Concept

In a differentially compounded DC generator, as load current increases, the terminal voltage:

  • (A) Increases continuously
  • (B) Remains exactly constant
  • (C) Decreases more steeply than in a plain shunt generator
  • (D) First increases, then decreases

Explanation: In differential compounding, series flux opposes shunt flux, so net flux (and hence Eg) actively decreases as load current — and therefore series flux — increases. This is on top of the usual armature resistance drop and armature reaction effects already present in a shunt generator, making the voltage droop more steeply. This drooping characteristic is intentionally exploited in DC arc-welding generators.

Quick Revision — Memory Tricks

🧠
Mnemonic: "SAW" for the Three Excitation Families

Shunt — Voltage-operated field, droops with load. Armature-fed series field — Current-operated, rises then droops. With-both (Compound) — best of both, tunable via diverter. Remembering "voltage-operated vs current-operated" instantly tells you how flux behaves with load in each type.

🔑
The "RFC" Build-Up Checklist

Residual flux present, field winding Field-MMF reinforces it (correct polarity), and Critical resistance/speed condition satisfied (Rsh < Rc and N > Nc) — if any one of these three fails, the generator will not build up voltage.

Chapter Summary

EMF Equation

Eg = ΦZNP/60A volts. Eg ∝ ΦN. A = P for lap winding, A = 2 for wave winding. Always use Ia, not IL, in armature voltage equations.

Excitation Types

Separate (rare, isolated field source) vs Self-excited (Shunt, Series, Compound). Self-excited needs residual flux to bootstrap. ~95% of DC generators are self-excited.

Shunt vs Series

Shunt: high-R field in parallel, voltage-operated, flux ≈ constant with load. Series: low-R field in series, current-operated, flux varies strongly with load.

Long Shunt & Short Shunt

Long shunt: Rsh across (Ra+Rse); easier to solve, more common. Short shunt: Rsh across armature alone. No major performance difference between them.

Compounding

Cumulative: series flux adds to shunt flux. Differential: series flux opposes shunt flux. Diverter resistance tunes degree: under / flat / over compound. Flat compound: VFL = VNL.

Voltage Build-Up

Needs residual flux + reinforcing field connection + Rsh < Rc + N > Nc. Reversing field alone kills build-up; reversing both rotation and field restores normal polarity.
