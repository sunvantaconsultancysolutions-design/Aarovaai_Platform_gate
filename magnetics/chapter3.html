§ 3.1

Why Maxwell's Equations Matter

Before 1864, electricity and magnetism were considered entirely separate phenomena. James Clerk Maxwell synthesised the experimental laws of Gauss, Faraday, and Ampère into a single, self-consistent mathematical framework — and discovered that electromagnetic waves propagate at the speed of light.[1]

💡
The Big Picture

Maxwell's four equations completely describe all classical electromagnetic phenomena — from the static field of a point charge to the propagation of a microwave signal across the globe. Every antenna, transformer, motor, and optical fibre operates according to these four equations.

Fig. 3.1 — Maxwell's Four Equations: integral and differential forms with physical meaning

The profound insight is that electricity and magnetism are not separate — they are two aspects of a single electromagnetic field. A changing electric field produces a magnetic field (Ampere–Maxwell Law), and a changing magnetic field produces an electric field (Faraday's Law). This mutual regeneration is precisely what allows electromagnetic waves to self-propagate through empty space.[1]

⚡
Real-World Applications

Every mobile phone antenna, radio transmitter, microwave oven, MRI machine, optical fibre, electric motor, and power transformer operates according to Maxwell's four equations. They are not abstract mathematics — they are the engineering backbone of modern civilisation.

§ 3.2

Gauss's Law for Electric Fields 🟢 HIGH

Intuitive Understanding

Imagine you're standing inside a sealed room. You want to know how many electric charges are inside — but you cannot look inside. Gauss's Law tells you the answer by measuring only the electric field at the surface of the room (the "Gaussian surface"). The total outward electric flux through any closed surface equals the total charge enclosed, divided by permittivity.

Think of water flowing from a sprinkler: the total water crossing any sphere surrounding the sprinkler is the same, regardless of the sphere's radius. Similarly, electric field lines "flow" outward from positive charges and "flow" inward towards negative charges.[1]

Integral Form

Gauss's Law — Integral Form (in terms of D and free charge)
$$\oint_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_V \rho_v \, dV$$
Equivalent form in terms of E-field
$$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_V \rho_v \, dV$$
Symbol Quantity SI Unit Physical Meaning
\(\mathbf{D}\) Electric Flux Density C/m² Displacement field; \(\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}\)
\(\mathbf{E}\) Electric Field Intensity V/m Force per unit positive test charge
\(d\mathbf{S}\) Outward area element m² Points outward from closed surface S
\(Q_{\text{enc}}\) Total enclosed charge C (Coulombs) Algebraic sum of all charges inside S
\(\rho_v\) Volume charge density C/m³ Charge per unit volume
\(\varepsilon_0\) Permittivity of free space F/m \(8.854 \times 10^{-12}\) F/m

Differential Form — Derivation

The integral form is converted to a differential (point) form using the Divergence Theorem, which states that the surface integral of a vector field over a closed surface equals the volume integral of its divergence:[2]

Step 1 — Divergence Theorem applied to flux integral
$$\oint_S \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D}) \, dV$$
Step 2 — Equate to the enclosed charge
$$\int_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D}) \, dV = \int_V \rho_v \, dV$$
Step 3 — Since this holds for any arbitrary volume V
$$\boxed{\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v}$$
✅
Physical Meaning of ∇ · D = ρᵥ

The divergence of D at a point tells you whether that point acts as a source (positive divergence, positive charge) or a sink (negative divergence, negative charge). Where there are no charges, \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = 0\), meaning field lines pass through without starting or ending.

Fig. 3.2 — Gauss's Law: electric field lines diverge from positive charges (source) and converge on negative charges (sink)
Concept: Sadiku, Elements of Electromagnetics, Ch. 3 [2]

Special Cases & Applications

Point Charge
  • ·Spherical Gaussian surface, radius \(r\)
  • ·\(E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\)
  • ·\(\mathbf{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\hat{a}_r\) — Coulomb's Law!
  • ·Gauss's Law contains Coulomb's Law
Infinite Line Charge
  • ·Cylindrical Gaussian surface, length \(L\), radius \(r\)
  • ·\(E \cdot 2\pi r L = \frac{\rho_L L}{\varepsilon_0}\)
  • ·\(\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_L}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\hat{a}_r\)
  • ·Field falls as \(1/r\) (not \(1/r^2\))
Infinite Sheet of Charge
  • ·Pillbox Gaussian surface, area \(A\)
  • ·\(2EA = \frac{\rho_s A}{\varepsilon_0}\)
  • ·\(\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_s}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat{a}_n\)
  • ·Field is uniform — no \(r\) dependence!
In Free Space (No Charge)
  • ·\(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0\)
  • ·No sources or sinks
  • ·Field lines pass through without starting or ending
  • ·Leads to Laplace's equation
⚠️
Common Mistake — GATE Trap

Students often confuse \(\mathbf{D}\) and \(\mathbf{E}\). The relation is \(\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon \mathbf{E} = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \mathbf{E}\). Gauss's Law in terms of \(\mathbf{D}\) uses only free charges. When using \(\mathbf{E}\), you must include all charges (free + bound). In free space, \(\varepsilon_r = 1\), so \(\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E}\).

§ 3.3

Gauss's Law for Magnetic Fields 🟢 HIGH

The Profound Difference from Electric Fields

Electric field lines can start on a positive charge and end on a negative charge. But magnetic field lines never start or end anywhere — they always form closed loops. This is the physical content of Gauss's Law for Magnetic Fields, and it tells us something deep about nature: magnetic monopoles do not exist.[1]

🧲
Analogy: Cut a Magnet in Half

If you cut a bar magnet in half hoping to isolate a north pole and a south pole, you don't get two monopoles — you get two smaller bar magnets, each with its own north and south pole. Cut again, and the same thing happens, all the way down to the atomic level. This is Gauss's Law for Magnetic Fields in action.

Integral Form

Gauss's Law for Magnetic Fields — Integral Form
$$\oint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$$

The total magnetic flux through any closed surface is always zero — because every field line that enters the surface must also exit it. The net flux is zero.[2]

Differential Form — Derivation

Apply Divergence Theorem
$$\oint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}) \, dV = 0$$
Since valid for any arbitrary volume
$$\boxed{\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0}$$
Fig. 3.3 — Magnetic field lines always form closed loops. For any closed surface, total inward flux = total outward flux → ∮ B·dS = 0
Property Electric Field (Gauss) Magnetic Field (Gauss)
Integral form \(\oint \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}\) \(\oint \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0\)
Differential form \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v\) \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\)
Field line behaviour Start/end on charges Always closed loops
Monopoles? Yes (point charges) No — not found in nature
If divergence = 0 Source-free region Always true everywhere
§ 3.4

Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction 🟢 HIGH

The Physical Idea

Michael Faraday discovered in 1831 that a changing magnetic flux through a surface induces an electromotive force (EMF) around the boundary of that surface. The induced EMF drives a current in a loop — this is the principle of every electric generator, transformer, and induction motor ever built.[3]

🔄
Intuitive Analogy

Imagine a coil of wire and a moving bar magnet. When the magnet moves, the magnetic flux through the coil changes. This change "pushes" charges around the coil — a voltage appears at the terminals. The faster the magnet moves (faster flux change), the larger the induced voltage. This is Faraday's Law.

Integral Form — Faraday's Law

Faraday's Law — Integral Form
$$\oint_C \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$
Symbol Meaning Unit
\(\oint_C \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) EMF around closed contour C (line integral of E) Volts (V)
\(\Phi_B\) Total magnetic flux through surface bounded by C Webers (Wb)
\(\mathbf{B}\) Magnetic flux density Tesla (T)
Negative sign Lenz's Law — opposition to change (energy conservation) —

The Negative Sign — Lenz's Law

The negative sign is not just a mathematical convention — it has deep physical meaning. Lenz's Law states that the induced EMF opposes the change in flux that caused it. If flux is increasing, the induced current creates a field opposing the increase. This is nothing but the law of energy conservation: you must do work to push the magnet against the opposing force.[3]

Differential Form — Derivation

Applying Stokes' Theorem to the left side of the integral form converts the closed line integral to a surface integral of the curl:

Step 1 — Apply Stokes' Theorem to the left side
$$\oint_C \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$
Step 2 — Bring time derivative inside the integral (for stationary surface S)
$$\int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\int_S \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$
Step 3 — Since valid for any surface S
$$\boxed{\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}}$$
Fig. 3.4 — Faraday's Law: increasing B-flux (into page) induces a counter-clockwise E-field (Lenz's Law in action). Right: transformer application

❌
Common Mistake — Static E-field vs Induced E-field

A static (electrostatic) E-field is conservative: \(\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0\). An induced E-field (from time-varying B) is non-conservative: \(\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} \neq 0\). Never apply the electrostatic path-independence concept to induced fields — this is a classic GATE trap!

GATE EC Faraday's Law — Conceptual 2018

Which of the following statements is/are correct regarding Faraday's Law?

  • (A) It relates the EMF in a loop to the rate of change of electric flux
  • ✓ (B) It relates the line integral of E around a closed path to the negative rate of change of magnetic flux
  • (C) It implies magnetic monopoles exist
  • (D) The line integral of E is always zero in a time-varying field

Answer (B): Faraday's Law states \(\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Phi_B/dt\). It relates to changing magnetic (not electric) flux. Static fields give zero line integral, but time-varying fields do not.

§ 3.5

The Ampere–Maxwell Law 🟢 HIGH

Ampère's Original Law — and Its Fatal Flaw

Ampère's Law (pre-Maxwell) stated that the line integral of H around any closed path equals the total conduction current through the enclosed surface: \(\oint \mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{\text{enc}}\). This worked perfectly for DC and low-frequency circuits.[1]

But Maxwell noticed a critical inconsistency: consider a capacitor being charged. Current flows in the wire, but no current flows through the gap between the plates. Choose a flat surface cutting through the wire — current passes through it. Choose a balloon-shaped surface bulging through the capacitor gap — no current passes. Two different surfaces, same contour, two different answers! This violates mathematical consistency.

⚠️
Maxwell's Genius Insight

Maxwell reasoned: inside the capacitor gap, the electric field is changing as charge builds up. A changing electric flux must produce a magnetic field, just as conduction current does. He introduced the "displacement current" \(J_D = \partial \mathbf{D}/\partial t\) to fix Ampère's Law and make it consistent. This single addition predicted electromagnetic waves.

Integral Form

Ampere–Maxwell Law — Integral Form
$$\oint_C \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{S} + \int_S \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = I_c + I_d$$

where \(I_c = \int_S \mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) is the conduction current, and \(I_d = \int_S \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) is the displacement current.[1]

Differential Form — Derivation

Apply Stokes' Theorem to left side
$$\oint_C \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{H}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$
Equate integrands (valid for any S)
$$\boxed{\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t}}$$
Fig. 3.5 — Displacement Current in a capacitor. Both surfaces S₁ and S₂ are bounded by the same contour C, yet S₁ intercepts conduction current and S₂ intercepts displacement current. Maxwell's term makes both equal.

Physical Meaning of Each Term

Term Symbol Physical Source Present When
Conduction current density \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}\) Actual flow of free electrons Conductors, resistors, wires
Displacement current density \(\mathbf{J}_D = \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\) Time-varying electric field Capacitors, dielectrics, free space
Total current \(\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\) Either or both Always (Continuity equation)
🌊
Why Displacement Current Predicts EM Waves

In free space, \(\mathbf{J} = 0\). The Ampere–Maxwell law becomes \(\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\). Combined with Faraday's Law \(\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\), taking the curl of one and substituting the other gives the wave equation: \(\nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}\). The wave speed is \(c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 3\times10^8\) m/s — exactly the speed of light.

The bridge between integral and differential forms is the two key vector calculus theorems. Mastering this conversion is essential for GATE and IES — you will be tested on both directions.[2]

  • ·$$ \oint_S \mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})\,dV $$

  • ·$$ \oint_C \mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{A})\cdot d\mathbf{S} $$

🧠
Memory Trick — GATE Favourite

"D-B via Divergence; E-H via Stokes" — The two Gauss Laws involve \(\mathbf{D}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) (both closed surface integrals, hence Divergence Theorem). Faraday and Ampere–Maxwell involve \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{H}\) (both closed line integrals, hence Stokes' Theorem). Never mix these up in a GATE conversion question.

§ 3.7

Conservation Laws from Maxwell's Equations 🟡 MEDIUM

Continuity Equation — Conservation of Charge

Taking the divergence of the Ampere–Maxwell Law and using Gauss's Law for electric fields, we can derive the Continuity Equation, which is the mathematical statement of charge conservation:[1]

Derivation of Continuity Equation
$$\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D})}{\partial t} = 0$$ $$\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\rho_v}{\partial t} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho_v}{\partial t}}$$
⚖️
Physical Meaning of the Continuity Equation

Charge flowing out of a volume (positive divergence of J) must equal the rate of decrease of charge inside that volume. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed. In integral form: \(\oint\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{dQ}{dt}\). This is Kirchhoff's Current Law in its most general, three-dimensional form.

Poynting's Theorem — Conservation of Energy

Combining Faraday's Law and the Ampere–Maxwell Law by multiplying one by E and the other by H, then subtracting and integrating over a volume gives Poynting's Theorem — the electromagnetic energy conservation law:[4]

Poynting's Theorem (Differential Form)
$$-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}) = \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon|\mathbf{E}|^2 + \frac{1}{2}\mu|\mathbf{H}|^2\right)$$
Integral Form — Power Flow
$$-\oint_S (\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H})\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{J}\,dV + \frac{d}{dt}\int_V\left(\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2 + \frac{1}{2}\mu H^2\right)dV$$
Term Symbol Physical Meaning
Poynting Vector \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\) Power flow per unit area (W/m²) — direction of EM power propagation
Ohmic Loss \(\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{J}\) Power dissipated as heat per unit volume (W/m³)
Electric Energy Density \(w_e = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2\) Energy stored in electric field per unit volume (J/m³)
Magnetic Energy Density \(w_m = \frac{1}{2}\mu H^2\) Energy stored in magnetic field per unit volume (J/m³)

GATE EC Conversion of Integral to Differential Form 2020

Which theorem is used to convert \(\oint_S \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}\) to its point form \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_v\)?

GATE EE Displacement Current — Physical Significance 2019

Maxwell's modification of Ampère's Law by adding displacement current \(\varepsilon\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t\) was necessary because:

  • (A) Ohm's Law is not valid in dielectrics
  • ✓ (B) The original Ampère's Law gave inconsistent results for surfaces bounded by the same contour
  • (C) Magnetic monopoles need to be accounted for
  • (D) Faraday's Law is not valid in conductors

Answer (B): For a charging capacitor, two surfaces bounded by the same contour give different values of \(\oint \mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) using original Ampère's Law. Maxwell added the displacement current term to restore mathematical consistency — and in doing so predicted EM waves.

IES/ESE Gauss's Law — Magnetic Monopoles 2018

The equation \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) implies that:

  • (A) Magnetic field is always zero in free space
  • (B) Magnetic flux density B must be constant
  • ✓ (C) Isolated magnetic poles (monopoles) do not exist in nature
  • (D) The divergence of B equals the magnetic current density

Answer (C): \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) means B-field lines have no sources or sinks — they always form closed loops. This is equivalent to saying that every north pole is always paired with a south pole; isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.

GATE EC Faraday's Law — Differential Form 2022

For a time-varying magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) in a region, which of the following must be true?

  • (A) \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0\) everywhere
  • (B) \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\) everywhere
  • ✓ (C) \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \neq 0\)
  • (D) \(\mathbf{E} = 0\) everywhere

Answer (C): Faraday's Law in differential form states \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\). If \(\mathbf{B}\) is time-varying, the curl of E is non-zero — the E-field is non-conservative and circulates around the region of changing B.

Common Mistakes — GATE Traps

❌
Mistake 1 — Confusing Which Theorem for Which Law

The Divergence Theorem applies to Gauss Laws (surface integrals). Stokes' Theorem applies to Faraday and Ampere–Maxwell (line integrals). Using Stokes' on a Gauss Law question is a zero-mark error.

❌
Mistake 2 — Forgetting the Negative Sign in Faraday's Law

\(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\) — the negative sign is Lenz's Law and cannot be dropped. Writing \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = +\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\) is fundamentally wrong and implies EM waves would amplify rather than propagate.

❌
Mistake 3 — Original Ampère's Law vs Modified

Ampère's original law \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}\) is valid only for DC or quasi-static fields. For time-varying fields, you MUST include the displacement current: \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t\). Using the static form in a time-varying problem is incorrect.

⚠️
Mistake 4 — D vs E, B vs H

In free space: \(\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_0\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}=\mu_0\mathbf{H}\). In a medium: \(\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}=\mu_0\mu_r\mathbf{H}\). Gauss's Law for E uses \(\mathbf{D}\) with free charge \(\rho_v\). If you use \(\mathbf{E}\) instead, you must include bound charges too — missing bound charges leads to wrong answers.

§ 3.9

Solved Numerical Examples

EXAMPLE 3.1 Gauss's Law — Find E from volume charge density
A spherical region of radius \(a = 2\) m carries a uniform volume charge density \(\rho_v = 3\) nC/m³ embedded in free space. Find the electric field intensity \(\mathbf{E}\) at a point \(r = 1\) m (inside) and \(r = 4\) m (outside).
✦ Solution

Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius r centred at the origin. By symmetry, E is radial and uniform on S.

Case 1: Inside (r = 1 m < a = 2 m)

Enclosed charge within sphere of radius r = 1 m
$$Q_{\text{enc}} = \rho_v \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 3\times10^{-9} \times \frac{4}{3}\pi(1)^3 = 4\pi \times 10^{-9} \text{ C}$$
Apply Gauss's Law: E · 4πr² = Q_enc / ε₀
$$E \cdot 4\pi(1)^2 = \frac{4\pi\times10^{-9}}{8.854\times10^{-12}} \Rightarrow E = \frac{10^{-9}}{8.854\times10^{-12}} = 112.9 \text{ V/m}$$

Case 2: Outside (r = 4 m > a = 2 m)

Total charge Q = ρᵥ · (4/3)πa³
$$Q = 3\times10^{-9}\times\frac{4}{3}\pi(2)^3 = 32\pi\times10^{-9} \text{ C}$$
E outside — same as a point charge at origin
$$E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{32\pi\times10^{-9}}{4\pi\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times16} = \frac{32\times10^{-9}}{4\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times16} \approx 56.5 \text{ V/m}$$
💡
Key Observation

Inside a uniformly charged sphere, E ∝ r (increases linearly). Outside, E ∝ 1/r² (decreases as inverse square). This is a very common GATE pattern.

EXAMPLE 3.2 Faraday's Law — Find induced EMF from time-varying B
A rectangular loop of area \(A = 0.05\) m² lies in the \(z = 0\) plane. The magnetic flux density is \(\mathbf{B} = 0.1\sin(1000\pi t)\,\hat{a}_z\) T. Find the induced EMF in the loop and state the maximum induced voltage.
✦ Solution

The magnetic flux through the loop:

Magnetic flux Φ_B = B · A (since B ⊥ loop plane)
$$\Phi_B = B \cdot A = 0.1\sin(1000\pi t)\times 0.05 = 5\times10^{-3}\sin(1000\pi t) \text{ Wb}$$
Apply Faraday's Law: EMF = −dΦ_B/dt
$$\text{EMF} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -5\times10^{-3}\times1000\pi\cos(1000\pi t) = -5\pi\cos(1000\pi t) \text{ V}$$

Maximum induced EMF occurs when \(\cos(1000\pi t) = \pm 1\):

Peak EMF
$$|\text{EMF}|_{\max} = 5\pi \approx 15.71 \text{ V}$$

Physical interpretation: The EMF is 90° out of phase with the flux — maximum EMF occurs when the flux is zero (rate of change is maximum), and zero EMF when flux is at its peak. This is the principle of AC generators.

EXAMPLE 3.3 Verify Maxwell's Equation — Check if field satisfies ∇·D = ρᵥ
Given \(\mathbf{D} = (2x\,\hat{a}_x + 3y\,\hat{a}_y - 5z\,\hat{a}_z)\) C/m², find the volume charge density \(\rho_v\) at the point (1, 2, 3). Also verify Gauss's Law.
✦ Solution

Using the differential form of Gauss's Law: \(\rho_v = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}\)

Compute Divergence of D
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \frac{\partial D_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial D_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial D_z}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial(2x)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(3y)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(-5z)}{\partial z}$$ $$= 2 + 3 + (-5) = 0 \text{ C/m³}$$

Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0\) everywhere, there is no free charge anywhere in this region. The field \(\mathbf{D}\) is a valid source-free field satisfying \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_v = 0\).

💡
GATE Pattern

GATE frequently asks: given a vector field, find the charge density using \(\rho_v = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}\). Always compute \(\partial D_x/\partial x + \partial D_y/\partial y + \partial D_z/\partial z\). No integration needed — it's a straightforward differentiation.

§ 3.11

Chapter Summary

Gauss — Electric

\( \oint \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}} \). Differential: \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v \). E-field lines start on + and end on − charges. Apply Divergence Theorem to convert. \( \mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\mathbf{E} \) in linear media.

Gauss — Magnetic

\( \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0 \). Differential: \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0 \). B-field lines always form closed loops. No magnetic monopoles exist in nature. True for any medium everywhere.

Faraday's Law

\( \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \). Differential: \( \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \). Changing B creates circulating E. Negative sign = Lenz's Law. Apply Stokes' Theorem to convert.

Ampere–Maxwell

\( \oint \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_c + I_d \). Differential: \( \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \). Maxwell added \( \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \) (displacement current). Key to EM wave propagation. Apply Stokes' Theorem.

Conservation Laws

Charge: \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho_v}{\partial t} \) (continuity equation). Energy: Poynting vector \( \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H} \) (W/m²). Energy densities: \( w_e = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2 \), \( w_m = \frac{1}{2}\mu H^2 \).

GATE Key Rules

Divergence Thm \(\rightarrow\) Gauss Laws. Stokes' Thm \(\rightarrow\) Faraday & Ampere. Never drop negative in Faraday. Include displacement current for time-varying fields.
