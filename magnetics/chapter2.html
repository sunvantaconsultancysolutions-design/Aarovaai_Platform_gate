1.1

Coulomb's Law

Every branch of electrical and electronics engineering — from capacitor design to antenna theory — rests on one foundational experimental fact: charges exert forces on each other even without touching. Coulomb's law quantifies this force, and it is the starting point from which the entire structure of electrostatics — field, flux, potential, and capacitance — is built.

Statement

Coulomb's law states that the force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, directed along the line joining them.

Coulomb's Law — Vector Form
$$\vec{F}_{12}=\dfrac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_{12}^{2}}\,\hat{a}_{R_{12}}$$

Here Q₁, Q₂ are the point charges in coulombs, R₁₂ is the distance between them in metres, â_R12 is the unit vector directed from charge 1 to charge 2, and ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space. The constant 1/4πε₀ ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² is often written as k.

Like charges repel along the line joining them — forces are equal and opposite (Newton's third law).
🔑
Key Property

Coulomb's law obeys the inverse square law — doubling the separation reduces the force to one-quarter. It also obeys the principle of superposition: the net force on any charge due to several charges is the vector sum of individual forces.

Force in a Material Medium

When charges sit in a dielectric medium of relative permittivity εr, the force reduces because the medium partially shields the field:

Force in dielectric\( F = \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r R^2} = \frac{F_{\text{vacuum}}}{\varepsilon_r} \)
⚠️
Common Mistake

Students often forget that Coulomb's law applies strictly to point charges (or spherically symmetric charge distributions, treated as a point charge at the centre). For extended or irregular charge distributions, direct application without integration gives a wrong answer — this is exactly why Gauss's law (Section 1.4) becomes essential.

1.2

Electric Field Intensity

Instead of asking "what force would charge Q₂ feel," it is more powerful to ask "what does charge Q₁ do to the space around it, independent of any test charge placed there?" That question defines the electric field intensity — a vector field that exists whether or not a second charge is present to feel it.

Definition
$$\vec{E}=\lim_{Q_t\to0}\dfrac{\vec{F}}{Q_t}=\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^{2}}\,\hat{a}_R \quad \text{(V/m or N/C)}$$

E is measured in volts per metre (V/m), points radially outward from a positive charge, and radially inward toward a negative charge. For a collection of n point charges, the resultant field at any point is the vector sum of the individual fields — the same superposition principle inherited from Coulomb's law.

Field of Standard Charge Distributions

Distribution Field Expression Notes
Point charge Q E = Q/4πε₀R² Radial, ∝ 1/R²
Infinite line charge ρL E = ρL/2πε₀ρ ∝ 1/ρ (cylindrical radial distance)
Infinite sheet charge ρS E = ρS/2ε₀ (n̂) Uniform — independent of distance
Charged sphere (outside, r>a) E = Q/4πε₀r² Behaves like a point charge at centre
Charged sphere (inside, r<a), uniform ρv E = ρv r/3ε₀ Increases linearly with r
Electric field lines radiate symmetrically outward from an isolated positive point charge.
1.3

Electric Flux Density

Electric field intensity E depends on the medium (it changes if a dielectric is inserted), which makes it awkward when dealing with multiple materials. Electric flux density D, also called displacement density, was introduced by Maxwell specifically to be medium-independent in form — it depends only on the source charge, not on ε_r.

Relation between D and E
$$\vec{D}=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\vec{E}=\varepsilon\vec{E}\quad(\text{C/m}^2)$$

For a point charge, D = Q/4πR² — notice ε₀ disappears entirely from this expression, which is precisely why D is the natural quantity to use when stating Gauss's law in its most general form.

💡
Why Engineers Prefer D

In multi-dielectric capacitor problems (e.g., layered insulation in a cable), the normal component of D is continuous across a boundary with no free surface charge, while E changes abruptly. This makes D the preferred variable for boundary-value problems.

1.4

Gauss's Law

Direct integration of Coulomb's law over a charge distribution can be tedious. Gauss's law exploits symmetry to bypass that integration entirely whenever the charge distribution is spherically, cylindrically, or planar symmetric — a technique GATE examiners test repeatedly.

Statement

The total electric flux passing out of any closed surface is equal to the total charge enclosed by that surface.

Integral Form
$$\oint_S \vec{D}\cdot d\vec{S}=Q_{enc}=\int_v \rho_v\,dv$$
Point (Differential) Form — via Divergence Theorem
$$\nabla\cdot\vec{D}=\rho_v$$
Flux lines crossing a closed (Gaussian) surface depend only on the net charge enclosed — not on its exact position inside.

Worked Application — Field of an Infinite Line Charge

Choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius ρ and length L coaxial with the line charge ρL. By symmetry D is radial and constant in magnitude over the curved surface, and zero flux crosses the flat end-caps:

\( D(2\pi\rho L) = \rho_L L \implies D = \frac{\rho_L}{2\pi\rho} \implies E = \frac{\rho_L}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\rho} \)
✅
Exam Strategy

Whenever a GATE/IES question gives an infinite sheet, line, or a symmetric sphere/ cylinder of charge, reach for Gauss's law first — it is almost always faster than direct Coulomb integration.

1.5

Electric Potential

The electric field is a vector — directionally awkward to integrate over complex paths. The electric potential V is the scalar "energy-per-unit-charge" description of the same field, and because the electrostatic field is conservative, V depends only on position, not on the path taken to reach it.

Potential due to a Point Charge (reference at infinity)
$$V=\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\qquad \vec{E}=-\nabla V$$

The potential difference between two points A and B is the work done in moving a unit positive charge from B to A against the field:

Potential differenceVAB = VA − VB = −∫BA E·dL
🔑
Path Independence

Because ∇ × E = 0 in electrostatics, $$\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{L}=0$$ — the work done around any closed path is zero. This is the defining property of a conservative field and is a frequently tested GATE conceptual question.

Equipotential Surfaces

A surface on which V is constant is an equipotential surface; field lines always cross equipotentials at right angles, and no work is done moving a charge along an equipotential.

1.6

Capacitance

Capacitance is the measure of a conductor system's ability to store charge per unit potential difference applied. It is a purely geometric and material property — independent of Q and V individually, dependent only on their ratio.

Definition
$$C=\dfrac{Q}{V}\quad(\text{farad, F})$$
Parallel-plate capacitor — uniform field D between oppositely charged plates of area A.

Standard Capacitor Formulas

Configuration Capacitance
Parallel plate (area A, gap d) C = ε₀εr A/d
Isolated sphere (radius a) C = 4πε₀εr a
Coaxial cable (a inner, b outer) C = 2πε₀εr L / ln(b/a)
Two parallel wires (sep. d, radius a) C = πε₀εr L / ln(d/a)
⚠️
GATE Trap

Capacitance increases when a dielectric (ε_r > 1) is inserted with the charge held constant (battery disconnected) — but V drops. If the voltage is held constant (battery connected), C still increases but now Q increases instead. Always check which quantity is held fixed before reasoning about energy stored, U = ½CV² = ½QV = ½Q²/C.

1.7

Biot–Savart Law

Magnetostatics is the magnetic mirror of electrostatics: instead of static charges producing E, steady currents produce a magnetic field H (and flux density B). The Biot–Savart law is the magnetic analogue of Coulomb's law — it gives the field due to an elemental current source.

Biot–Savart Law
$$d\vec{H}=\dfrac{I\,d\vec{L}\times\hat{a}_R}{4\pi R^{2}}\quad(\text{A/m})$$

I dL is the current element, R is the distance from the element to the field point, and the cross product shows that dH is always perpendicular to both the current direction and the line joining the element to the field point — magnetic field lines circle the current, they never point radially toward or away from it the way E does for a charge.

Field dH due to a current element circles the conductor, governed by the right-hand rule.

Field on the Axis of a Circular Loop

Integrating Biot–Savart around a circular loop of radius a carrying current I, the field on the axis at distance h from the centre is:

Axial field of circular loopH = Ia² / [2(a²+h²)^1.5] (A/m), max at centre: H = I/2a
1.8

Ampere's Circuital Law

Just as Gauss's law shortcuts symmetric electrostatic problems, Ampere's circuital law shortcuts symmetric magnetostatic problems — it is the magnetic counterpart of Gauss's law, exploiting circular, coaxial, or planar symmetry of current distributions.

Statement

The line integral of H around any closed path equals the total current enclosed by that path.

Integral Form
$$\oint_L \vec{H}\cdot d\vec{L}=I_{enc}$$
Point Form — via Stokes' Theorem
$$\nabla\times\vec{H}=\vec{J}$$
H is constant along a circular Amperian loop centred on a long straight current-carrying conductor.

Worked Application — Field of an Infinite Straight Wire

\( H(2\pi\rho) = I \implies H = \frac{I}{2\pi\rho} \text{ (A/m)} \)
✅
Exam Strategy

Ampere's law is fastest for: infinite straight wire, infinite solenoid (H = NI/l inside, 0 outside), toroid (H = NI/2πρ), and coaxial cable regions. If symmetry is absent, fall back to Biot–Savart.

1.9

Magnetic Flux & Flux Density

Magnetic flux density B relates to H through the permeability of the medium, exactly as D relates to E through permittivity:

B–H Relation
$$\vec{B}=\mu_0\mu_r\vec{H}=\mu\vec{H}\quad(\text{tesla, T})$$

Total magnetic flux through a surface is Φ = ∫_S B·dS (webers). Unlike electric flux, magnetic flux lines always close on themselves — there is no magnetic monopole, so the net flux out of any closed surface is always zero:

Gauss's Law for Magnetism
$$\oint_S\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S}=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\nabla\cdot\vec{B}=0$$
🔑
Conceptual Pillar

∇·B = 0 is one of Maxwell's four equations, and it is the formal mathematical statement that isolated magnetic poles do not exist — break a bar magnet in half and you get two complete magnets, never an isolated N or S pole.

1.10

Inductance

Inductance is the magnetic analogue of capacitance — it measures a current-carrying circuit's ability to link its own magnetic flux, storing energy in the surrounding field.

Self-Inductance
$$L=\dfrac{N\Phi}{I}\quad(\text{henry, H})$$

Standard Inductor Formulas

Configuration Inductance
Long solenoid (N turns, length l, area A) \( L = \frac{\mu_0\mu_r N^2 A}{l} \)
Toroid (N turns, mean radius ρ, area A) \( L = \frac{\mu_0\mu_r N^2 A}{2\pi\rho} \)
Coaxial cable per unit length \( L = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) \)
Two parallel wires per unit length \( L = \frac{\mu_0}{\pi} \ln\left(\frac{d}{a}\right) \)

Energy stored in the magnetic field of an inductor, the magnetic dual of \(\frac{1}{2}CV^2\) for a capacitor:

Energy stored\( W = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \frac{1}{2}\int \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{H} \, dv \)
💡
Electrostatic ↔ Magnetostatic Duality

Q ↔ Φ, V ↔ I, C ↔ L, E ↔ H, D ↔ B, ε ↔ μ. Recognising this duality lets you derive any magnetic result by "translating" the matching electrostatic result — a major time-saver under GATE exam pressure.

1.11

Solved Examples

EX 1Basic — Coulomb Force
Two point charges of +2 μC and +3 μC are placed 0.3 m apart in air. Find the force between them.
Solution

$$F=\dfrac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}=\dfrac{9\times10^9\times2\times10^{-6}\times3\times10^{-6}}{(0.3)^2}$$

F = 9×10⁹ × 6×10⁻¹² / 0.09 = 0.6 N (repulsive)

EX 2GATE-Level — Gauss Law on a Charged Sphere
A sphere of radius 5 cm carries a uniform volume charge density ρ_v = 10 μC/m³. Find E at r = 10 cm from the centre.
Solution

Since r > a (outside the sphere), the total enclosed charge behaves as a point charge at the centre:

$$Q=\rho_v\cdot\dfrac{4}{3}\pi a^3 = 10\times10^{-6}\times\dfrac{4}{3}\pi(0.05)^3=5.24\times10^{-9}\text{ C}$$

$$E=\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}=\dfrac{9\times10^9\times5.24\times10^{-9}}{(0.1)^2}\approx\mathbf{4716\ V/m}$$

EX 3Conceptual — Field vs Potential
At a point in space, V = 0. Is the electric field necessarily zero there?
Solution

No. E = −∇V depends on the rate of change (gradient) of V, not its absolute value. The midpoint between equal and opposite charges has V = 0 but a strong, non-zero E field pointing from + to −. Conversely, the centre of two equal like charges has E = 0 but V is non-zero.

EX 4GATE-Level — Inductance of a Solenoid
A 500-turn air-core solenoid is 0.5 m long with cross-sectional area 4 cm². Find its self-inductance.
Solution

$$L=\dfrac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{l}=\dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times(500)^2\times4\times10^{-4}}{0.5}$$

L ≈ 0.251 mH

Which of the following statements about magnetic flux lines is correct?

  • They originate from N pole and terminate at infinity
  • They form closed loops with no beginning or end
  • They can terminate on a magnetic monopole
  • Their net flux through any closed surface is non-zero

Reasoning: Since ∇·B = 0 everywhere (no magnetic monopoles), B-field lines must always close on themselves, unlike E-field lines which begin and end on charges.

PSU INTERVIEWFrequently Asked — Field Theory Basics

Q: Why is the electric field inside a perfect conductor in electrostatic equilibrium always zero?
A: Free charges in a conductor redistribute instantly until the internal field that would drive further charge motion vanishes; any net field would imply continuing current flow, contradicting "equilibrium." This is also why a conductor's surface must be an equipotential and why charge resides only on the outer surface (used in Faraday-cage shielding, cable design, and surge protection design in PSU transmission systems).

Chapter Summary

Coulomb & Field

\(\mathbf{F} = \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\) (vector, superposition holds). \(\mathbf{E} = \frac{\mathbf{F}}{Q_t}\), points outward from \(+Q\). Inverse-square law; obeys superposition.

D, Gauss's Law

\(\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}\), medium-independent in form. \(\oint \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}\). Point form: \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v\). Best for symmetric distributions.

Potential & Capacitance

\(V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\), \(\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\). \(\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{L} = 0\) (conservative). \(C = \frac{Q}{V}\); parallel plate \(C = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{d}\).

Biot–Savart & Ampere

\(d\mathbf{H} = \frac{I \, d\mathbf{L} \times \mathbf{\hat{a}}_R}{4\pi R^2}\). \(\oint \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{L} = I_{\text{enc}}\). Point form \(\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}\). Straight wire: \(\mathbf{H} = \frac{I}{2\pi\rho}\).

Flux & Inductance

\(\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}\); \(\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0\), \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\) (no monopoles). \(L = \frac{N\Phi}{I}\); solenoid \(L = \frac{\mu_0\mu_r N^2 A}{l}\). \(W = \frac{1}{2}LI^2\).

E–H Duality

\(Q \leftrightarrow \Phi\), \(V \leftrightarrow I\), \(C \leftrightarrow L\), \(\mathbf{E} \leftrightarrow \mathbf{H}\), \(\mathbf{D} \leftrightarrow \mathbf{B}\), \(\varepsilon \leftrightarrow \mu\). Translate any electrostatic result to its magnetostatic counterpart and vice versa.
